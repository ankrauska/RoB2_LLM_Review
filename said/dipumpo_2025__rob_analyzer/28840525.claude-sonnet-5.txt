# RoB 2 Assessment: Laparoscopic Sleeve Gastrectomy vs Silastic Ring-LRYGB for T2D and Obesity

**Preliminary considerations:**
- Study design: Individually-randomized parallel-group trial
- Experimental/Comparator: SR-LRYGB vs LSG (analysed as two active comparators)
- Outcome assessed: Primary outcome — proportion achieving HbA1c thresholds (e.g., <42 mmol/mol) without diabetes medication at 1 year
- Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (ITT-type effect)
- Sources used: Journal article, trial protocol (published), registry record (ACTRN/NCT)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Low risk**

**Signalling questions:**
- Sequence random? **Yes** — "computer-generated random number codes," with minimization based on age, BMI, diabetes duration, insulin treatment, and ethnicity.
- Allocation concealed? **Yes** — codes were "managed by an independent study member" and "disclosed after anaesthesia induction," i.e., after enrolment and after the patient was under anaesthesia, precluding any influence on enrolment/allocation by participants, surgeons, or recruiters.
- Baseline imbalance suggesting problem? **No** — Table 1 shows groups well balanced in age, sex, ethnicity, diabetes duration, insulin use, HbA1c, weight, and BMI category. No differences approach statistical or clinical significance suggestive of a randomization failure (minimization variables were explicitly balanced).

**Motivation:** All three criteria for low risk are met: adequate random sequence generation, robust concealment (disclosure only after anaesthesia induction — an unusually strong concealment method), and no evidence of baseline imbalance indicating problems with randomization.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention) — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- Participants aware of assigned intervention? **Probably No** — study explicitly described as double-blind (patient and assessor); protocol paper (Murphy et al. 2016) describes blinding methodology (e.g., identical dressings/port placements, standardized information given to patients). However, sleeve gastrectomy and gastric bypass have different possible physiological consequences (dumping syndrome, different bowel habits) which could theoretically unblind some participants; no formal blinding-success check (e.g., asking patients to guess allocation) is reported.
- Carers/personnel aware? **Yes** — surgeons performing the operation cannot be blinded to the procedure performed. However, the *postoperative medical/endocrinology team* responsible for adjusting diabetes medication (which determines the primary outcome) were explicitly blinded: "Metabolic medications were commenced post-operatively according to a standard medical review protocol, by endocrinologists blinded to treatment allocation." This is a key strength.
- Deviations from intended intervention due to trial context? **Probably No** — the standardized post-operative medication algorithm applied by blinded endocrinologists specifically aimed to prevent unequal deviation in diabetes management between arms. No non-protocol interventions differentially applied are reported.
- Appropriate analysis (ITT)? **Some concerns** — Analyses were based on participants completing follow-up at 1 year (56 SR-LRYGB, 53 LSG, excluding 5 lost to follow-up in LSG group), rather than a formal full ITT analysis of all 114 randomized participants with imputation for missing data. This is closer to a "modified ITT" (excluding missing outcome data), which is acceptable under this domain (bias due to missing data is assessed separately in Domain 3), so this alone would not raise a high level of concern, but the paper does not explicitly report the analysis approach as ITT/mITT, creating ambiguity.

**Motivation for "Some concerns":** While the operating surgeons could not be blinded (unavoidable given differing procedures), the outcome-relevant decision-makers (endocrinologists adjusting glycaemic medication, defining remission) were blinded, and a standardized medication-withdrawal protocol was followed — substantially mitigating the main plausible source of performance bias. However, participant blinding success was not formally verified, and the exact analysis population/approach (whether truly ITT) is not unambiguously stated. This combination leads to "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

**Ambiguity/limitation noted:** The report does not explicitly confirm whether participant-blinding was checked or maintained throughout follow-up, nor does it explicitly label the statistical analysis as ITT vs mITT.

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- Outcome data available for all/nearly all randomized participants? **No** — Of 114 randomized (56 SR-LRYGB, 58 LSG), 5 participants (all in the LSG arm) were lost to follow-up ("failure to make contact and/or to attend appointments beyond 3 months post-operatively"), leaving 53/58 (91%) in LSG and 56/56 (100%) in SR-LRYGB analysed at 1 year for the primary outcome.
- Evidence result not biased by missing data (e.g., sensitivity analysis)? **No** — no sensitivity analyses or imputation methods for missing outcome data are reported.
- Could missingness depend on true outcome value? **Yes/Probably Yes** — loss to follow-up ("failure to make contact/attend appointments") is plausible to be related to poor engagement with care, which could correlate with worse glycaemic outcomes; this cannot be excluded.
- Likely that missingness depended on true value? **Probably No** — the dropout was small (5/114, ~4.4% overall, 5/58 ~8.6% in LSG only) and entirely in one arm, but the reasons given (loss of contact) are generic/administrative rather than clearly outcome-related (e.g., not specifically due to poor glycaemic outcome or dissatisfaction with surgery). There is no differential reporting of reasons suggesting outcome-related dropout, and the imbalance (0% vs ~8.6%) is modest for a study of this size and the given event rates are not razor-thin.

**Motivation for "Some concerns":** The proportion of missing data is small but asymmetric between groups (100% follow-up in SR-LRYGB vs 91% in LSG), and no sensitivity analysis was performed to demonstrate robustness of the primary result to missingness. While the reasons for dropout do not obviously suggest outcome-dependency, the absence of formal exploration of this issue and the between-group asymmetry preclude a "Low risk" judgement.

---

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk**

**Signalling questions:**
- Method of measuring outcome inappropriate? **No** — HbA1c is an objective, standardized laboratory measurement, appropriate for assessing glycaemic remission.
- Measurement/ascertainment differ between groups? **No** — HbA1c was measured identically in both groups at the same standardized timepoints (3, 6, 9, 12 months).
- Outcome assessors aware of intervention allocation? **No** — Endocrinologists making medication decisions and presumably assessing/interpreting HbA1c results were explicitly blinded to treatment allocation ("by endocrinologists blinded to treatment allocation"). Additionally, HbA1c is a laboratory (automated) test, essentially an "observer-reported outcome not involving judgement," which is inherently resistant to assessor bias regardless of blinding status.
- Could assessment be influenced by knowledge of intervention? **No** — given the objective nature of the HbA1c assay and blinding of clinical decision-makers, influence is very unlikely.

**Motivation:** The primary outcome (HbA1c-based remission) is an objective laboratory measure not susceptible to subjective interpretation, ascertainment methods were identical across groups, and the personnel responsible for outcome-relevant clinical decisions (medication withdrawal) were blinded. This satisfies criteria for low risk.

---

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Low risk**

**Signalling questions:**
- Analysed in accordance with pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded data available? **Yes** — a detailed trial protocol was published in advance (Murphy et al., BMJ Open 2016) and prospective registration occurred (ACTRN12611000751976, prior to enrolment; NCT01486680, retrospectively registered). The four HbA1c thresholds reported (≤38, <42, <48, <53 mmol/mol) are explicitly stated in Methods as pre-specified to enable comparison with prior literature, and are reported comprehensively and consistently (not selectively) in Table 2.
- Selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements? **No** — all four pre-specified HbA1c thresholds are fully reported, along with the full set of pre-specified secondary outcomes (weight loss, lipids, blood pressure, medication use, SF-36 domains, HADS anxiety/depression), with no evidence of selective omission.
- Selected from multiple eligible analyses? **No** — a single, consistent statistical approach (Fisher's exact test for proportions, repeated-measures mixed models for continuous trajectories) is applied uniformly across outcomes; no evidence of multiple competing analyses being tried and selectively reported.

**Motivation:** The prospective protocol publication and registry allow direct comparison of intended outcomes/analyses with those reported; the correspondence is complete, comprehensive, and non-selective. This meets criteria for low risk of bias in this domain.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement — **Some Concerns**

**Rationale:** 
- Domain 1 (randomization): Low risk
- Domain 2 (deviations from intended interventions): Some concerns
- Domain 3 (missing outcome data): Some concerns
- Domain 4 (measurement of outcome): Low risk
- Domain 5 (selection of reported result): Low risk

Per RoB 2 rules, a judgement of "Some concerns" in at least one domain (here, two domains) — without any domain reaching "High risk" — places the overall judgement at **Some concerns**. The concurrent presence of "Some concerns" in *two* domains (deviations from intended intervention and missing outcome data) warrants consideration of whether this should be escalated to "High risk" per the rule that "some concerns for multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result" constitutes high risk. However, in this case:
- The missing data are minimal (4.4% overall) and asymmetric only modestly,
- The main outcome-determining personnel (endocrinologists) were blinded, mitigating the primary concern in Domain 2,

Therefore, these concerns are not judged to substantially or cumulatively lower confidence in the result to the level of "High risk." The overall judgement is **Some Concerns**.

**Stated limitation/ambiguity:** The trial report does not explicitly confirm (a) whether blinding of participants was formally tested/maintained throughout the 1-year follow-up, or (b) whether the analysis population for the primary outcome corresponds strictly to an ITT or modified-ITT approach; nor does it report sensitivity analyses addressing the impact of the asymmetric loss to follow-up. These ambiguities are reported here as limitations of the risk-of-bias assessment rather than assumptions.
