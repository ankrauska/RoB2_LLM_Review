## RoB 2 assessment

**Study and result assessed:** Individually randomized, parallel-group, double-blind trial comparing **simvastatin 40 mg/day plus interferon beta-1a** with **placebo plus interferon beta-1a** in relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis.  
**Outcome assessed:** Total number of relapses during the 1-year follow-up (the stated primary outcome).  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to simvastatin versus placebo (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Sources assessed:** Published article and ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT00668343).

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states that “a computer-based blocking method (four blocks) was used for the randomization.” This is a random sequence-generation method.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  Eligibility and follow-up examinations were undertaken by four neurologists, whereas “separate physicians were assigned to randomly allocate patients.” This separation of eligibility assessment from allocation supports that the recruiting/assessing neurologists did not control allocation. Patients were allocated after eligibility assessment. Although the report does not describe central randomization, sealed envelopes, or another formal concealment mechanism, the reported separation of roles supports a judgement of probably adequate concealment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between groups suggest a problem with randomization? — No information.**  
  The reported baseline table does not appear to include all randomized participants: it reports 38 placebo and 42 simvastatin participants, whereas 42 and 43 participants, respectively, were randomized. Therefore, baseline balance cannot be fully assessed for the randomized population. However, no reported imbalance suggests manipulation or a systematic failure of randomization.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The random sequence was explicitly computer-generated using blocks, and allocation was performed by physicians separate from those assessing eligibility. No evidence indicates a compromised randomization process. The limitation is that the allocation-concealment procedure is incompletely described and baseline data are not clearly presented for every randomized participant; nevertheless, this does not provide evidence of a material randomization problem.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  The study is described as double-blind; patients “were unaware of their group assignment”; and medications were provided in similar packages every three months.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering interventions aware of assigned intervention? — Probably no.**  
  The registry describes quadruple masking, including care providers, investigators, and outcome assessors. The article also reports blinding of the neurologists conducting follow-up assessments.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention arising because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The report does not identify trial-context-driven differences in co-interventions, implementation, or treatment administration between groups. All participants were intended to continue interferon beta-1a, with simvastatin or placebo added.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — No.**  
  Although the authors state that analyses were conducted according to intention-to-treat principles, the reported primary relapse analysis was not conducted in all randomized participants. Eighty-five participants were randomized (43 simvastatin; 42 placebo), but the primary Mann–Whitney relapse analysis reports only 40 simvastatin and 36 placebo participants, i.e. 76 participants. Thus, nine randomized participants were absent from this principal analysis.  
  Moreover, the authors state that only withdrawal cases who had used simvastatin or placebo for at least six months were included in the final analysis, because they considered six months necessary for simvastatin to show an effect. This is a post-randomization exclusion based on duration of treatment/follow-up, not a full intention-to-treat analysis.

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of failure to analyse participants in their randomized groups? — Yes.**  
  At least 9 of 85 randomized participants were excluded from the main relapse analysis (approximately 11%). The report also describes withdrawals related to disease activity: six participants withdrew after two relapses (two simvastatin and four placebo), and one placebo participant withdrew due to chronic exacerbation. Exclusion or incomplete follow-up of participants with disease deterioration could materially affect the total-relapse comparison.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Blinding was adequately described, and there is no evidence of differential non-protocol treatment caused by the trial context. However, the result selected for assessment is not based on a valid full intention-to-treat analysis. The stated decision to include only certain withdrawal cases according to treatment duration, together with exclusion of randomized participants from the main relapse analysis, is incompatible with estimating the effect of assignment to treatment. The number excluded and the relation of some withdrawals to relapses/disease worsening create potential for a substantial effect on the estimated relapse result.

**Important reporting ambiguity:**  
The participant numbers are internally inconsistent. The flow diagram reports 42 simvastatin and 38 placebo participants analysed (80 total), whereas the reported primary relapse analysis uses 40 and 36 participants (76 total). The text states that nine withdrawal cases who had at least six months’ treatment were included, which would imply a different analysis population again. This inconsistency prevents precise reconstruction of who was included in the primary analysis, but it does not remove the demonstrated post-randomization exclusions.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for total relapses available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants? — No.**  
  The primary relapse analysis includes 76 participants, while 85 were randomized. Therefore, data contributing to the principal analysis were unavailable or excluded for at least nine participants. This proportion is not sufficiently small to conclude that missing data could not affect the result.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The article reports per-protocol and last-observation-carried-forward analyses, but neither provides evidence that the primary 1-year relapse result was robust to plausible assumptions about outcomes among participants absent from the primary analysis. No sensitivity analysis addressing missing relapse data is reported.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  This is plausible because withdrawal was explicitly related, in several cases, to disease activity: six participants withdrew after two relapses and one placebo participant withdrew due to chronic exacerbation. Since the outcome is total relapse count, withdrawal related to relapses or worsening disease can be associated with the unobserved future relapse outcome.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness depended on the true value of the outcome? — Yes.**  
  The reported reasons for withdrawal include two relapses and chronic exacerbation, both directly related to the clinical disease activity that determines the relapse outcome. In addition, premature withdrawals due to exacerbation were more frequent in the placebo group in the flow diagram (six placebo versus three simvastatin). These facts support that missingness was likely outcome-related and potentially differed by group.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The main relapse analysis omitted a non-trivial proportion of randomized participants. Importantly, several withdrawals were explicitly caused by relapses or clinical worsening, making outcome-dependent missingness likely. The study does not report an analysis demonstrating that the result is robust to the missing or excluded relapse data. Therefore, the relapse-effect estimate is at high risk of bias from missing outcome data.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring relapse inappropriate? — No.**  
  Relapse was prospectively defined as “any new or worsening neurological deficit in the absence of high body temperature that lasted for more than 24 h after 1-month stability.” Participants were assessed every three months by neurologists. This is an appropriate clinical method for assessing relapses in this context.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of relapse have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  Follow-up assessments were scheduled every three months for both groups. The report provides no evidence that one group underwent more intensive clinical surveillance or had a different relapse-ascertainment process.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of treatment received? — No.**  
  The four neurologists assessing eligibility and conducting follow-up examinations were explicitly reported to be blinded to treatment assignment. The registry also identifies outcome assessors as masked.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The relapse outcome was prospectively defined, evaluated on the same schedule in both groups, and assessed by neurologists blinded to treatment allocation. There is no reported evidence of different assessment methods or differential ascertainment between study arms. Therefore, risk of bias in measurement of the relapse outcome is low.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data analysed according to a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The ClinicalTrials.gov record identifies “attack number” at one year as the primary outcome, consistent with the article’s stated main outcome of number of relapses over one year. However, no dated statistical analysis plan is available. The registry was first submitted in April 2008, after the registered actual primary-completion date of March 2008. It therefore cannot establish that the detailed analysis approach was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The article does not provide a pre-specified analysis plan or document timing of analytical decisions.

- **5.2 Was the result likely selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the relapse domain? — No information.**  
  Relapses were considered both as total relapses during one year and as relapse numbers over three-month periods in the LOCF analysis. The report identifies total relapses during the one-year follow-up as the main outcome, but no sufficiently detailed pre-specified plan is available to establish that this measurement/time-point was selected independently of the observed findings.

- **5.3 Was the result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses? — No information.**  
  The paper reports several approaches: a main analysis labelled intention-to-treat, a per-protocol analysis, and a LOCF analysis. It also reports the relapse result using both Mann–Whitney testing and mean relapse counts. No pre-specified statistical analysis plan establishes which analysis was intended as primary before outcome data were available.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The one-year relapse outcome is consistent with the registry’s primary outcome, and the article does not provide direct evidence that the published result was selected because it was favourable. Therefore, high risk of bias cannot be concluded. However, the absence of a pre-unblinding, detailed analysis plan—and the presence of multiple outcome-analysis approaches—means that selective selection of the reported numerical result cannot be excluded.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement — **High risk of bias**

The overall judgement is **High risk of bias** because the primary relapse result is judged at **high risk of bias in two domains**:

1. **Bias due to deviations from intended interventions:** the principal analysis excluded randomized participants and was not a valid full intention-to-treat analysis of assignment to simvastatin versus placebo.
2. **Bias due to missing outcome data:** a non-trivial number of participants were absent from the primary analysis, and withdrawal was in several cases directly related to relapses or disease exacerbation.

There are also **some concerns** regarding selection of the reported result because no pre-specified, pre-unblinding statistical analysis plan is available. The randomized sequence, blinding, and blinded clinical outcome measurement were adequately described, but these strengths do not overcome the high-risk problems in analysis and missing outcome data for the reported 1-year relapse result.
