**Assessment scope.** This RoB 2 assessment concerns the **effect of assignment** to the therapist-assisted computer brief intervention (TBI) versus brochure control on **severe peer aggression at 12 months**, estimated by the reported GEE group-by-time interaction (OR 0.40, 95% CI 0.23–0.69). The trial was an individually randomized, three-arm parallel-group trial. The assessment uses the article and the supplied ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT00251212). It applies to the reported 12-month self-reported peer-aggression result; other outcomes and the computer-only intervention would require separate result-specific assessments.

1. **Bias arising from the randomization process — Some concerns**

   **Signalling questions**
   - **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?** **No information.** The article states that eligible participants were “randomly selected” and “assigned” after stratification by gender and age, while the registry describes the trial as randomized. Neither source describes the sequence-generation method (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, random permuted blocks).
   - **1.2 Was allocation concealed until enrolment and assignment?** **No information.** Neither source reports central randomization, an independent allocation service, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another allocation-concealment mechanism.
   - **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization?** **Probably no.** The article reports no significant differences between conditions in age, race, or gender. The reported baseline prevalence of severe peer aggression was 82.7% in TBI, 75.5% in CBI, and 77.9% in control; the report does not identify these differences as evidence of a failure of randomization. However, a full baseline-characteristics table and formal assessment of all important prognostic factors are not presented in the supplied material.

   **Motivation.** Stratification by age and gender is reported, and there is no reported baseline imbalance clearly beyond what could occur by chance. However, RoB 2 requires evidence that allocation was concealed for a low-risk judgement. Since neither the method for generating the sequence nor, particularly, the mechanism for concealing the upcoming allocation is reported, the randomization process cannot be confirmed as adequately protected from selection/manipulation before assignment. Under the RoB 2 algorithm, no information on concealment with no evident problematic baseline imbalance results in **some concerns**.

2. **Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — Some concerns**

   **Signalling questions**
   - **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention?** **Yes.** Participants either received a therapist-delivered intervention, a stand-alone computerized intervention, or a brochure control. These conditions could not be concealed from participants.
   - **2.2 Were carers/people delivering the interventions aware of assigned intervention?** **Yes.** Therapists necessarily knew when they delivered the therapist-assisted intervention. The control condition was a brochure and therefore also distinguishable from the active intervention.
   - **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?** **No information.** The report states that more than 95% received their assigned intervention during the ED visit, but does not report whether trial-context-related co-interventions, differential attention beyond the intended intervention, or departures from protocol occurred.
   - **2.4–2.5 Were such deviations likely to affect the outcome and balanced between groups?** **Not applicable**, because evidence of trial-context deviations was not reported.
   - **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment?** **Yes.** The article explicitly reports an intention-to-treat approach: “All randomly assigned participants were included whether the intervention was received or not.” Participants were analysed by assigned condition, and GEE models used available longitudinal data. Missing outcome data are considered separately in Domain 3.
   - **2.7 Potential substantial impact of failure to analyse by assignment?** **Not applicable.**

   **Motivation.** Participants and intervention personnel were aware of condition assignment. The report does not document whether this awareness led to deviations from the intended protocol due to the trial context that could affect peer aggression. The reported analysis is appropriate for the effect of assignment because it retained randomized participants in their assigned groups and did not analyse them according to treatment received. Nevertheless, in an unblinded behavioral intervention trial, the absence of information about trial-context deviations prevents a low-risk judgement. Therefore, this domain has **some concerns** rather than high risk: there is no reported evidence that outcome-affecting, unbalanced deviations occurred, but the necessary information to exclude them is absent.

3. **Bias due to missing outcome data — High risk of bias**

   **Signalling questions**
   - **3.1 Were outcome data available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants?** **No.** Twelve-month follow-up was completed by 607 of 726 randomized participants (83.6%); thus, outcome data were missing for 119 participants (16.4%). The article’s Table 1 indicates approximately 201 TBI, 199 CBI, and 200 control participants contributed 12-month outcome data, compared with baseline group totals of approximately 254, 237, and 235, respectively.
   - **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?** **No.** No sensitivity analysis addressing plausible values of unobserved peer aggression outcomes is reported. No analysis specifically demonstrating robustness to missingness is reported.
   - **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?** **Probably yes.** The outcome is past-year self-reported severe peer aggression. It is possible that follow-up completion is related to participants’ behavioral circumstances, including ongoing aggression, victimization, disengagement, or related social instability. The report does not provide documented reasons for missing 12-month outcome data that would rule out such a relationship.
   - **3.4 Is it likely that missingness depended on its true value?** **Yes.** Follow-up completion differed across conditions: approximately 79% in the TBI group (201/254), 84% in the CBI group (199/237), and 85% in the control group (200/235). Thus, the TBI-versus-control comparison has a greater proportion missing in the intervention group (approximately 21%) than in the control group (approximately 15%). The report does not provide reasons for attrition by assigned condition or evidence that missingness was unrelated to peer aggression.

   **Motivation.** Missing data were substantial for this behavioral outcome and were not balanced across the TBI and control groups. The analysis used all *available* observations, but this does not itself address bias if loss to follow-up is related to the unobserved true outcome. No missing-data sensitivity analysis, model justified to correct outcome-dependent missingness, or attrition-reason analysis is reported. In accordance with RoB 2, the combination of incomplete data, unequal missingness between groups, and no evidence excluding an association between missingness and true peer aggression warrants **high risk of bias**.

4. **Bias in measurement of the outcome — High risk of bias**

   **Signalling questions**
   - **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?** **Probably no.** Severe peer aggression was measured using items from the conflict tactics scale and operationalized as a binary indicator of any severe peer aggression in the past year. The report does not indicate that this method was unsuitable for assessing the stated outcome.
   - **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment have differed between intervention groups?** **Probably no.** The 12-month survey was self-administered by computer for all conditions. The article does not report different instruments, thresholds, or scheduled outcome-assessment methods between groups.
   - **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received?** **Yes.** For this self-reported outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor under RoB 2. Participants necessarily knew whether they had received the therapist intervention or the brochure control.
   - **4.4 Could outcome assessment have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?** **Yes.** The outcome is a participant-reported behavior, and the intervention was designed to change violence-related attitudes, self-efficacy, conflict-resolution behavior, and aggression. Knowledge of having received the intervention could influence reporting of aggression.
   - **4.5 Is it likely that assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?** **Yes.** The TBI involved direct therapist contact and motivational interviewing specifically addressing violence, whereas the control received a resource brochure. This contrast creates a material risk that participants’ expectations, desire to report improvement, or social desirability could affect self-reported severe peer aggression.

   **Motivation.** Although the computer-administered survey was used consistently and may support privacy, it does not blind the participant, who is the assessor of their own aggression. The registry’s statement that outcome assessors were masked cannot establish blinding for this participant-reported outcome, because the article specifies self-administered computer follow-up surveys and participants knew their assigned intervention. Given the subjective, self-reported behavioral outcome and the overtly motivational intervention directed at that behavior, knowledge of assignment was likely to influence reporting. This domain is therefore at **high risk of bias**.

6. **Overall risk-of-bias judgement — High risk of bias**

   The overall judgement is **high risk of bias** for the TBI-versus-control result on self-reported severe peer aggression at 12 months.
