# ROB 2 Assessment Report: Sertraline for Depression in TBI (Ashman et al., 2009)

**Study result assessed:** Effect of sertraline vs placebo on HAM-D score (primary outcome) at end of treatment (10 weeks), intention-to-treat/last-observation-carried-forward analysis, random-effects modeling.
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect).

---

## Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Sequence generation: "computer-generated random number sequences" used with a block design (blocks of 8) → **Yes**, random component present.
- Allocation concealment: sequences were placed in "individual presealed envelopes" and dispensing/masking was implemented by the pharmacy independently of study physicians and staff. This suggests reasonable concealment, but details on whether these were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof envelopes are not explicitly reported. This falls short of "extended criteria" for concealment, and the block size of 8 (a relatively small, fixed block size) creates some theoretical risk of predictability if research staff enrolling patients could observe preceding allocations, although this is mitigated by pharmacy-controlled concealment.
- Baseline imbalance: The paper explicitly states, "The income level of those receiving placebo was significantly lower than those participants receiving medication." This is a specific, reported baseline imbalance in a variable that could plausibly relate to healthcare access, treatment engagement, and depression outcomes. Additionally, examination of Table 1 (published data) shows notable imbalances in injury severity (mild TBI: 20% sertraline vs 50% placebo) and ethnicity distributions, though these differences were reported as not statistically significant overall by the authors ("No significant differences were found between drug and placebo groups on sex; age; education; race; income...") — however, this statement is contradicted by the explicit mention of a significant income difference. This inconsistency itself is a limitation and ambiguity in the report.
- Given: adequate random sequence generation (Yes), plausible but not fully detailed allocation concealment (Probably yes), and a documented, statistically significant baseline imbalance (income) that could relate to prognosis → this combination corresponds to "Some concerns" per the RoB2 algorithm (sequence random + concealed, but baseline imbalance suggestive of a possible randomization problem).

**Reported ambiguity/limitation:** The article's text is internally inconsistent — it states no significant baseline differences were found on income, yet simultaneously reports "The income level of those receiving placebo was significantly lower." This ambiguity limits confident judgment on whether randomization successfully balanced key prognostic/socioeconomic factors.

## Domain 2: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)
**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- 2.1/2.2 Blinding of participants and personnel: The trial was double-blind; medications were identically packaged/marked by the pharmacy, and "both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received." → **No** (unaware).
- Because participants, carers, and intervention-providers were blinded, the domain algorithm allows a default "Low" judgment (Part 1) provided no evidence indicates unblinding or trial-context-driven deviations. No evidence in the text suggests unblinding occurred (no report of guessing exercises, but no evidence of a problem either).
- Analysis method (2.6): The study used an intention-to-treat analysis including participants who dropped out early, supplemented with a last-observation-carried-forward (LOCF) approach to combine those who completed at least 8 weeks with full completers. All 52 randomized participants were accounted for in flow, and the intent-to-treat analysis explicitly compared "all study participants, including participants who received minimal treatment." This is consistent with ITT principles.
- Note: The random-effects model was applied to all 41 who had at least some follow-up data; the additional LOCF imputation for two participants (8-week completers) is a minor deviation but does not fundamentally violate ITT (as per RoB2 guidance, LOCF is a data-handling method addressed more properly under Domain 3, missing outcome data).

Overall, given effective blinding and use of an appropriate (ITT-oriented) analytic approach, this domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

## Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Signalling Q3.1: Outcome data were NOT available for all randomized participants. Of 52 randomized, only 41 completed the trial (11 dropouts: ~21% attrition). This is not a "nearly all" scenario given the modest overall sample size (52) and the a priori power calculation based on n=21/group.
- Attrition was somewhat unbalanced: 5 dropped out from sertraline arm (27→22, ~18.5%) vs 6 from placebo arm (25→19, ~24%). This is a modest but non-trivial difference.
- Reasons for dropout differed importantly between groups: one sertraline dropout was due to side effects (allergic reaction was actually placebo, per adverse event table — see below), while other reasons were "noncompliance," missed appointments, or clinical worsening (2 dropouts from placebo were due to increased depression/allergic reaction). The article explicitly reports: "The intent-to-treat analyses found differences between the last assessment HAM-D score for those who dropped out of treatment as compared to...those who remained in treatment (F=4.0, P=.052), with the dropouts tending to have higher HAM-D scores" — this indicates that missingness in the outcome is related to its true value (higher depression scores predict dropout), which is a documented, non-trivial finding.
- Because missingness plausibly depends on the true value of the outcome (worse depression → more likely to drop out) and reasons differ somewhat between arms (clinical worsening in placebo group vs allergic reaction/noncompliance in both), there is a real possibility of bias due to missing outcome data, though it is not definitively shown to be severe or clearly differential by intervention assignment beyond the noted trend.
- The study used LOCF for two participants only and analyzed only those with ≥1 follow-up assessment in the random-effects model (implicitly excluding some early dropouts entirely from repeated-measures models, as only "6 assessments" data feed the model). This is not a full ITT model for the primary comparative analysis with a robust sensitivity analysis exploring missing-data assumptions.
- No formal sensitivity analysis (e.g., multiple imputation, pattern-mixture models) was reported to confirm robustness of the missing-data handling.

Given (i) meaningful attrition (21%), (ii) some evidence that missingness depends on the outcome's true value (higher HAM-D predicts dropout), but (iii) no strong evidence that this differentially and substantially biased the specific comparative result (dropout rates similar between groups, and authors argue findings are "robust despite the small sample"), the judgment is **Some concerns** rather than High risk.

## Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- 4.1: The method of measuring the outcome (HAM-D, a validated, clinician-administered scale) was appropriate. → **No** (not inappropriate).
- 4.2: Measurement/ascertainment methods did not differ between groups — same assessment schedule and same instruments applied identically to both arms. → **No**.
- 4.3: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? The study states that "a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist rated the clinical severity of the participant's depressive (HAM-D) and anxiety (BAI) symptoms independently from the physician," and overall the study was double-blind with identical pill markings dispensed by pharmacy — supporting that raters were blinded. → **Probably no** (assessors were probably unaware).
- 4.4/4.5: However, the HAM-D is a clinician-administered but subjective/judgment-based rating scale (observer-reported outcome involving some judgment), and side-effect profiles of sertraline (an active SSRI) could have partially unblinded assessors or participants if side effects occurred (nausea, sexual dysfunction, insomnia are known sertraline effects), even if allocation was nominally concealed. The article does not report any formal test of blinding integrity (e.g., asking participants/raters to guess allocation), and it does not explicitly rule out that side-effect-driven unblinding could have influenced outcome ratings, especially for a subjective/judgment-laden measure. There is no information confirming that assessment was clearly not influenced by inadvertent unblinding.
- Given the absence of explicit information testing/confirming maintained blinding throughout the trial, combined with the inherently judgment-based nature of the HAM-D (a scale that could be influenced by subtle unblinding effects such as reported side effects), this domain cannot be rated definitively "Low." The lack of information on whether blinding success was verified constitutes a limitation.

**Reported ambiguity/limitation:** No formal assessment of blinding integrity (e.g., participant/rater guessing exercises) was reported, despite this being an active drug vs. placebo comparison in which the study drug has recognizable side effects.

## Domain 5: Bias in selection of the reported result
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- 5.1: A power analysis and primary outcome (HAM-D) were pre-specified in the trial registration and design (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00233103; sample size a priori based on HAM-D as primary outcome). However, the full statistical analysis plan is not available for independent verification, and it is unclear whether all analysis approaches (random-effects modeling, ANCOVA, repeated-measures ANOVA, "responder" threshold definitions) were pre-specified before unblinded data were available, or determined post hoc.
- 5.2/5.3: The paper reports the primary HAM-D total score result, but also reports multiple additional "unidimensional subscales" of the HAM-D (Beck Melancholia, Maier & Philipp, Gibbons, "5 most sensitive" items) that were not part of the original power calculation or pre-specified primary analysis. Critically, the text states: "Two of the subscales, HamG and HamFive, did show a significant fixed effect for group... suggesting that 2 scales were more sensitive to changes in depression symptoms related to medication use." This is a clear example of exploring multiple outcome measurements within the same domain (depression) and highlighting the two that reached significance among several tested — a hallmark of selective emphasis/outcome-multiplicity, even though the authors do report the null results for the primary HAM-D total score transparently.
- Additionally, multiple statistical models were applied to the same outcome data (ANCOVA, repeated-measures ANOVA, random-effects models, "responder" chi-square analyses with and without continuity correction), and results are reported selectively with different emphases (e.g., reporting exact P values close to significance such as P=.08, P=.09, P=.052 as "trends," which risk selective emphasis/interpretation).
- Given the multiplicity of outcome measurements (multiple HAM-D subscales) and multiple analytic approaches applied to the same underlying data, with some selective highlighting of nominally significant subscale results, there is a realistic possibility that the numerical result being assessed (or ancillary results) reflects selection based on statistical significance, though the primary HAM-D total score itself appears consistently and transparently reported across analyses.

**Reported ambiguity/limitation:** No statistical analysis plan was available for independent verification of whether subscale analyses and multiple modeling approaches were pre-specified prior to unblinding, limiting certainty about selection of the reported result.

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall judgment: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Applying the RoB2 decision rules for overall judgement:
- No domain was rated "High" risk in isolation.
- However, **four of the five domains** (Randomization process, Missing outcome data, Measurement of the outcome, and Selection of the reported result) were rated **"Some concerns"**, with only Domain 2 (deviations from intended interventions) rated "Low."
- Per RoB2 guidance: "'Some concerns' in multiple domains may lead the review authors to decide on an overall judgement of 'High' risk of bias for that outcome... The study is judged to have some concerns for multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result."
- The accumulation of concerns — an internally inconsistent report of baseline imbalance (income), documented differential/informative missingness (dropouts had higher depression scores), an unverified blinding-integrity issue for a judgment-based primary outcome measure, and evidence of exploring/selectively emphasizing multiple outcome subscales — collectively and substantially reduce confidence in the reported null finding for the primary sertraline vs. placebo comparison.

**Overall conclusion:** The trial result for the primary outcome (HAM-D at end of treatment) is judged to be at **High risk of bias**, driven not by a single fatal flaw but by the cumulative effect of multiple "Some concerns"-level issues across domains that collectively undermine confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported effect estimate.
