## RoB 2 assessment

**Target result assessed:** Effect of assignment to the BACE intervention versus control on **observed agitation at posttest**, represented by the reported repeated-measures ANCOVA Group × Time result: *F*(1,69) = 4.26, *p* = .043 (reported as .046 elsewhere), partial η² = .06.  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to BACE versus control (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Sources available:** The published article and its tables/figures only. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial registry record, or other prespecified documentation was provided.

A limitation affecting this assessment is that allocation was described as being conducted in one-week time blocks, with weeks randomized to treatment or control. This is not described sufficiently to establish whether the design was individually randomized, cluster-randomized by week, or another allocation structure. The assessment below applies the requested five-domain RoB 2 framework for an individually randomized trial, while recognizing this reporting ambiguity.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The report states that there was “random assignment” and that participants were “randomly assigned … in time blocks of 1 week”; it also states that “time blocks” were randomized as treatment or control weeks. However, it does not state how the allocation sequence was generated. There is no description of a computer-generated sequence, random-number table, randomly permuted blocks, or another method with a documented random component.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The article does not describe allocation concealment. There is no report of central randomization, an independent allocator, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or another concealment procedure. The allocation of weeks to conditions could potentially have been known to those recruiting or managing participants, but the article does not provide enough information to determine this.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  Groups were similar on reported gender, FAST stage, age, MMSE score, length of stay, and baseline agitation. There was a statistically significant baseline difference in pretest arousal imbalance: 107.4 versus 84.6 minutes per 12 hours (*p* = .012), with greater imbalance in the BACE group. The authors adjusted for arousal imbalance in the ANCOVA. This single difference does not, by itself, demonstrate a randomization failure according to RoB 2 criteria, which require imbalance clearly beyond that plausibly due to chance or a pattern of substantial imbalances. Nevertheless, the imbalance is relevant because arousal imbalance was central to the intervention rationale and analysis.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
A low-risk judgment requires adequately concealed allocation and no concerns about random sequence generation. Neither the random-sequence generation method nor allocation concealment was reported. The reported baseline data do not establish a randomization failure, but the absence of essential allocation information raises **some concerns**.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  The article states that “data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions.” Participants had moderate or severe dementia, and the intervention consisted of changes to their activity schedule. The report explicitly claims participant blinding; no information establishes that this blinding was broken.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The geriatric nurse practitioner and staff nurse developed the revised activity schedule, and nursing staff implemented it. These personnel necessarily knew which participants had prescribed activity-schedule changes and therefore knew the intervention condition.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  The report documents that the intervention was not given to 10 participants because staff were short-staffed, busy, or had emergent needs. However, it does not provide sufficient information to determine whether these deviations occurred differentially by group in a manner arising from trial context that would bias the effect of assignment.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — No.**  
  The published analysis did not include all randomized participants. Of 102 participants who entered the study, 10 were “dropped from the study for not being given the intervention,” 12 died, and 2 were excluded for “erroneous or missing data,” leaving 78 participants analysed (36 BACE; 42 control). The explicit requirement that a participant must have received at least one prescribed activity-schedule change to remain in the analysis means that analysis eligibility depended on post-randomization intervention delivery. This is not an intention-to-treat analysis of assignment to BACE versus control.

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of failure to analyse participants in the group to which they were randomized? — Yes.**  
  Ten of 102 enrolled participants (approximately 10%) were excluded specifically because they did not receive the intervention. Their nonreceipt resulted from staffing shortages, competing demands, or emergent care needs. These circumstances may be associated with participant condition, care needs, or the care environment, all of which could also be related to agitation. Excluding these participants after allocation can materially alter the comparison. In addition, the final analysis excluded a total of 24 of 102 enrolled participants for death, non-delivery of intervention, or erroneous/missing data.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The key reported estimate is based on the final sample of 78 rather than all 102 participants initially included. In particular, the exclusion of 10 participants for receiving no intervention is a post-randomization exclusion directly related to implementation of the allocated intervention. The resulting analysis does not estimate the effect of assignment to BACE and has potential for substantial impact on the result. Under RoB 2, this leads to a **high risk of bias** in this domain.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  Outcome data were analysed for 78 of 102 participants. Twelve participants died, 10 were excluded because the intervention was not given, and 2 were excluded for erroneous or missing data. Thus, the report does not provide posttest agitation data for a substantial proportion of participants initially included.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No sensitivity analysis, imputation approach justified for the missingness mechanism, inverse-probability weighting, or other analysis addressing possible bias from missing outcome data was reported.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  It could. Death, acute/emergent care demands, staffing-related inability to implement the planned schedule, and erroneous/missing observations may be related to participant health status, level of care need, or circumstances that could be associated with agitation.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — No information.**  
  The report gives overall counts for deaths and exclusions but does not report these separately by randomized group. It does not report timing of death, whether posttest agitation was observed before death, reasons for missing observations by group, baseline agitation among excluded participants, or analyses demonstrating that missing outcome data were unrelated to agitation. This absence of relevant information prevents concluding that missingness was unlikely to be related to the outcome.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The final result excludes 24 of 102 enrolled participants. The article does not provide group-specific attrition, outcome data for excluded participants, or analyses showing that missingness could not affect the estimated intervention effect. Since missingness could be related to health status and agitation, and the available information does not rule this out, the RoB 2 algorithm supports a **high risk of bias** judgment.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  Agitation was measured through direct observation every 15 minutes from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. on the pretest and posttest days. The initially planned Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument was not used because sufficient interrater reliability could not be achieved. The study instead used a 0–100 visual analogue agitation scale with prespecified intensity parameters and operationalization based on the 29 Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory items. The article reports extensive observer training and post-training interrater reliability for agitation ranging from .95 to .984. The report does not establish that this direct-observation approach was invalid or insensitive for observed agitation.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  Both groups were observed according to the same stated schedule: three-minute observations every 15 minutes over 12 hours, on both pretest and posttest days. The article does not report different observation thresholds, schedules, or ascertainment procedures by assigned group.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — No.**  
  The report explicitly states that “data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions.” Research assistants collecting the agitation outcome were the relevant outcome assessors.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The study used the same direct-observation procedure for both groups, implemented substantial observer training and reliability monitoring, and explicitly states that data collectors were blinded to treatment allocation. On the information reported, there is no evidence of differential outcome measurement or assessor awareness. Therefore, risk of bias in outcome measurement is judged **low**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a prespecified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No protocol, statistical analysis plan, registry record, or dated prespecified analysis document was available. Therefore, it cannot be established that the repeated-measures ANCOVA, its covariates, the tested interaction, or the reported analysis population were specified before outcome data were examined.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The report identifies agitation as the outcome and describes repeated direct observations on pretest and posttest days. However, without a protocol or analysis plan, it cannot be determined whether additional agitation measurement approaches, summaries, time periods, or outcome definitions were collected and analysed but not fully reported.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The report presents several analyses: the adjusted repeated-measures ANCOVA; analyses with pretest arousal imbalance and MMSE as covariates; a further model adding therapeutic activities as a covariate; paired *t* tests; stratified comparisons; and three additional repeated-measures ANCOVA models examining interactions with cognition, daytime sleep, and verbal ability. The article does not provide a prespecified hierarchy or analysis plan that identifies the primary analysis before outcome data were available. There is no direct evidence that the reported Group × Time interaction was selected because of its result, but selection cannot be excluded.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The article reports a statistically significant adjusted Group × Time interaction but supplies no prospectively finalized analysis plan. The presence of several analytic approaches and covariate choices, combined with the absence of protocol/SAP information, leaves unresolved potential selection among analyses. There is no direct evidence sufficient for a high-risk judgment, but there are **some concerns**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgment  
**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation for overall judgment:**  
The study is judged at **high risk of bias** because at least one domain is at high risk—specifically:

- **Bias due to deviations from intended interventions:** participants who did not receive the intervention were excluded after randomization, rather than analysed in their allocated groups; and  
- **Bias due to missing outcome data:** the analysed sample excluded 24 of 102 enrolled participants, with inadequate group-specific attrition information and no analysis demonstrating that missingness did not bias the agitation result.

In addition, there are **some concerns** about the randomization process because neither random-sequence generation nor allocation concealment was described, and about selection of the reported result because no prospectively finalized analysis plan was available. Although outcome measurement was judged at low risk of bias, it does not overcome the high-risk concerns in the deviations and missing-data domains.
