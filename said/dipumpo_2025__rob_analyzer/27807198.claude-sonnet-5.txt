# ROB 2 ASSESSMENT REPORT
## Doxycycline in Early CJD: Double-Blinded Randomised Phase II and Observational Study

**Preliminary Notes:** RoB 2 is designed for randomized controlled trials. This report assesses **only the double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled component** of the paper (n=12 analysed: 7 doxycycline vs 5 placebo), as this is the only portion meeting RCT design criteria. The "observational study" (55 treated vs 33 controls) is a non-randomized comparison and falls outside the scope of RoB 2 (it would require ROBINS-I); it is therefore not formally assessed here, though its influence on the overall reported conclusion (via meta-analytic pooling) is noted as a contextual limitation.

**Outcome assessed:** Primary outcome – Survival time (Cox proportional hazard model; HR for doxycycline vs placebo).
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), as intended by the trial design.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomisation process — **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Signalling questions and findings:**
- **Sequence generation:** The article states only "Patients were randomly assigned (ratio 1:1)." No description of the method used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated randomisation, block size) is provided in the article or the linked EudraCT registry. → **No information / Probably yes** (benefit of doubt given academic trial with pharmacy-administered randomisation).
- **Allocation concealment:** "The randomisation list and the study medication were administered by the Charité, Universitätsmedizin, Berlin" — an independent institution separate from the recruiting site. This supports a judgement of adequate concealment → **Probably yes**.
- **Baseline imbalance:** Table 2 reveals substantial imbalances in key prognostic factors between the very small groups (n=7 vs n=5):
  - Age at onset: 58.0 vs 72.0 years (large difference)
  - MMSE (baseline cognition, a key prognostic covariate per the paper's own adjustment models): 21 vs 16 (favouring the doxycycline arm with better baseline status)
  - Codon 129 genotype: 0% MM in the doxycycline group vs 20% MM in placebo — the MM genotype is explicitly stated by the authors to be associated with the *most rapidly progressive and shortest survival* disease course. Its complete absence in the treatment arm and presence in the placebo arm mechanically biases survival comparisons in favour of doxycycline.
  - Barthel index roughly comparable (45 vs 50).

These are not merely "chance" imbalances compatible with random sampling variability — they involve a covariate (codon 129 genotype) with documented, large, and directionally consistent influence on the primary outcome (survival), and the imbalance is large relative to the tiny sample size. This constitutes an imbalance in a key prognostic factor "big enough to result in bias in the intervention effect estimate," per RoB 2 criteria.

**Judgement rationale:** Even with adequate allocation concealment, a severe imbalance in a major, mechanistically important prognostic factor (codon 129 genotype) combined with imbalances in age and baseline MMSE, in a trial of only 12 analysed patients, raises the risk that chance/failure of randomisation to balance prognostic factors materially confounds the survival result. Per Table 3/4 of the RoB2 algorithm, when baseline imbalance suggestive of a problem with randomisation is present, even with concealment given as "Yes," the domain defaults to **"Some concerns" to "High"** depending on severity; here the magnitude and clinical importance of the imbalance (given the small sample size and the known strong prognostic effect of genotype) is judged severe enough to elevate risk to **High**.

**Limitation/ambiguity:** No explicit description of the sequence generation method was available; judgement relies on inference from the description of the trial's independent randomisation/drug-supply arrangement.

---

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention) — **SOME CONCERNS**

**Signalling questions and findings:**
- **Participant/personnel blinding:** The trial is explicitly described as "double-blinded," with placebo capsules of identical form (per EudraCT: "Capsule, hard" for both IMP and placebo, oral route). This supports adequate blinding of participants, caregivers, and treating personnel → **Probably no** (not aware).
- **Deviations from intended intervention arising from trial context:** The study flow diagram indicates high attrition from assigned treatment: 4 of 7 patients in the doxycycline arm "discontinued" during follow-up, versus 1 of 6 lost in the placebo arm. Reasons for discontinuation are not detailed (e.g., adverse events, disease progression, death, withdrawal). Given the severe adverse event rate reported (40% SAE in both arms) and high overall AE rate (70% doxycycline vs 60% placebo), some deviations plausibly relate to tolerability/disease severity rather than trial-context-specific issues, but this cannot be fully verified due to lack of detail.
- **Appropriateness of analysis:** One randomised participant (placebo arm) who "dropped out" was excluded entirely from the survival analysis (12 of 13 randomised patients analysed). This is a deviation from strict ITT principles (which require inclusion of all randomised participants) and could affect the primary result, although the numerical impact is presumably small (1/13, ~8%).

**Judgement rationale:** Blinding was well maintained, which minimizes risk of bias from trial-context-driven non-protocol interventions. However, substantial rates of treatment discontinuation (4/7, 57%, in the intervention arm) combined with exclusion of one randomised participant from the analysis (violating strict ITT), without clear reporting of reasons or their balance across arms, introduces uncertainty about whether the "effect of assignment" was accurately estimated. This does not clearly rise to "High risk" because blinding was preserved and death (a hard, objective endpoint largely unaffected by care-seeking behaviour differences) is the outcome, but the deviations and analytic exclusion prevent a "Low risk" judgement.

**Limitation/ambiguity:** Reasons for the four discontinuations in the doxycycline arm and the one placebo dropout are not reported, precluding a definitive judgement on whether these deviations were balanced or outcome-relevant.

---

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **SOME CONCERNS**

**Signalling questions and findings:**
- **Availability of outcome data for all/nearly all randomised participants:** Of 13 randomised patients, 12 were included in the survival analysis; one placebo-assigned patient who "dropped out" was excluded. For a primary outcome of survival/death, if this patient's ultimate survival status was unknown (true loss to follow-up) this constitutes missing outcome data for ~8% of a very small (n=13) sample — a proportion that, in a trial with only 12 total analysed events/observations, could plausibly influence the effect estimate materially given the tiny denominator.
- **Evidence that missingness did not bias the result:** No sensitivity analysis addressing this exclusion is reported.
- **Could missingness depend on true outcome value:** Not clearly excluded; no documented outcome-unrelated reason (e.g., administrative loss, equipment failure) is given for the dropout.

**Judgement rationale:** Given the very small sample size, exclusion of even a single participant from a group of 5–6 represents a non-trivial proportion of the comparator arm and could not be assumed to have "made no important difference" to the estimated effect. Absence of information on reasons for dropout, and absence of any sensitivity analysis, prevents a "Low risk" judgement. However, there is no explicit evidence that the missingness was outcome-dependent (e.g., that the patient's survival status specifically drove their loss), so "High risk" is not definitively supported either.

**Limitation/ambiguity:** No stated reason for the excluded placebo patient's dropout; no CONSORT-style detailed accounting of reasons for the four doxycycline arm discontinuations in relation to the survival outcome specifically (as opposed to treatment discontinuation, which is distinct from being lost to outcome ascertainment).

---

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **LOW RISK OF BIAS**

**Signalling questions and findings:**
- **Appropriateness of outcome measurement method:** Survival time (death) is an objective, unambiguous endpoint not subject to interpretation → method appropriate.
- **Comparability of measurement/ascertainment across groups:** No indication that ascertainment of death differed systematically between arms; both groups were followed under the same protocol-defined visit/telephone-contact schedule.
- **Blinding of outcome assessors:** The trial being double-blind implies both those managing/assessing participants clinically were unaware of allocation. Because the primary outcome (death) is an "observer-reported outcome not involving judgement," knowledge of intervention assignment is very unlikely to influence ascertainment of this endpoint even if incomplete blinding occurred at some point (e.g., due to distinguishing side effects).

**Judgement rationale:** For the primary outcome (survival/death), the objective nature of the endpoint combined with maintained blinding supports a low risk of measurement bias. (Note: secondary outcomes such as cognitive/functional decline measures, e.g., MMSE/Barthel changes, akinetic mutism onset, are more judgement-dependent and might carry higher risk, but the primary outcome assessed here is survival.)

**Limitation/ambiguity:** No explicit statement confirms that personnel remained unaware of allocation for the entire follow-up duration (e.g., unblinding due to adverse effect profiles was not formally assessed/reported), but this is unlikely to materially affect an objective mortality endpoint.

---

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **SOME CONCERNS**

**Signalling questions and findings:**
- **Pre-specified analysis plan:** The EudraCT registry entry explicitly defines the primary endpoint as "survival time of patients **from symptom onset**" ("Überlebenszeit der Patienten von Symptombeginn an"). However, in the published article, for the double-blind RCT component, survival follow-up is explicitly redefined to start "with the randomisation (first application of study medication)" rather than from symptom onset as pre-specified. This is a clear discrepancy between the registered primary endpoint definition and the analysis actually reported for the RCT, introducing risk that the change in time-origin definition was influenced by the resulting numerical outcome.
- **Selection from multiple eligible analyses:** The double-blind RCT result alone (HR 0.841, 95% CI 0.238–2.966; not statistically significant) is reported, but the paper's overall conclusions and abstract emphasize the **pooled meta-analytic HR** (0.633, 95% CI 0.402–0.999, p=0.049) combining the non-significant RCT with a non-randomized observational comparison. This pooling strategy, combined with the differential outcome start-time definitions between the RCT (randomisation) and observational study (symptom onset) being merged into a single meta-analysis, raises concern that the analytic approach was constructed/selected in a manner that yields a significant combined result, rather than being pre-specified in the trial protocol (the protocol describes only the single RCT with survival from symptom onset — no mention of a planned meta-analytic pooling with a separate observational cohort).
- **Secondary endpoints:** Time to sphincter dysfunction, feeding difficulty, and akinetic mutism are reported consistently with protocol-specified secondary endpoints, with no evidence of selective omission of these results (all are reported, including non-significant findings — "all p values are about 0.9").

**Judgement rationale:** The change in the definition of survival follow-up start-time between the pre-specified protocol and the published RCT analysis, combined with the emphasis on a post hoc meta-analytic combination (not specified in the protocol) that yields the only statistically significant treatment effect reported, represents a credible risk that the presented/interpreted result was shaped by knowledge of the data. This does not reach "High risk" because the underlying RCT-only result is transparently reported (allowing readers to see the non-significant primary comparison), but the overall reporting strategy raises material concern.

**Limitation/ambiguity:** No statistical analysis plan (SAP) date-stamped prior to unblinding was available for review; the discrepancy between protocol and publication regarding the survival time-origin was inferred by direct comparison of the EudraCT record and the article text.

## 6. OVERALL RISK-OF-BIAS JUDGEMENT

| Domain | Judgement |
|---|---|
| 1. Randomisation process | **High risk** |
| 2. Deviations from intended interventions | Some concerns |
| 3. Missing outcome data | Some concerns |
| 4. Measurement of the outcome | Low risk |
| 5. Selection of the reported result | Some concerns |

**Overall judgement: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Rationale:** Per RoB 2 rules, a "High risk" judgement in any single domain mandates an overall "High risk of bias" judgement for the result, regardless of the status of other domains. Domain 1 (randomisation process) was judged high risk owing to a severe, clinically consequential imbalance in the codon 129 genotype (a genotype the authors themselves identify as a major determinant of survival) and other prognostic factors (age, baseline MMSE) in the context of an exceptionally small sample (n=12). This alone is sufficient to classify the overall result as high risk of bias. This conclusion is reinforced by the presence of "Some concerns" in three additional domains (deviations from intended intervention, missing outcome data, and selection of the reported result), which further and substantially lowers confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported treatment effect for the randomised comparison.

**Key overarching limitations reported as ambiguities (not assumptions):**
1. No details of the random sequence generation method were available in the source documents examined.
2. Reasons for treatment discontinuation (4/7 in the doxycycline arm) and for the single excluded placebo dropout were not reported.
3. A discrepancy exists between the protocol-specified primary outcome (survival from symptom onset) and the analysis reported for the double-blind RCT (survival from randomisation), the basis for which is not explained in the article.
4. The statistically significant overall conclusion of the paper rests substantially on a post hoc combination of the underpowered RCT with a non-randomized (observational, ROBINS-I-applicable) comparison — a pooling strategy not pre-specified in the registered protocol.
