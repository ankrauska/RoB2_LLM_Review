## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

- **Design:** Individually randomized, open-label, parallel-group multicentre trial.
- **Comparison:** Preoperative contrast-enhanced MRI plus standard triple assessment versus standard triple assessment with no further imaging.
- **Result assessed:** Primary outcome at 6 months—repeat operation (further wide local excision or mastectomy) or pathologically avoidable mastectomy at initial surgery. Reported result: **153/816 (19%) versus 156/807 (19%); adjusted OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.75–1.24; p=0.77**.
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to MRI versus no MRI (intention-to-treat effect).

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?** **Yes.**  
  Allocation used “a minimisation algorithm incorporating a random element.” Under RoB 2, minimisation incorporating a random component is regarded as a random allocation method.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?** **Yes.**  
  Randomisation was administered through the University of Leeds Clinical Trials Research Unit’s “automated 24-hour telephone randomisation system.” This is a centrally administered allocation process, independent of recruitment personnel, and supports concealment until assignment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process?** **No.**  
  Baseline characteristics were closely balanced. In particular, age category was identical (23% younger than 50 years in both groups); breast-density categories were very similar (13% versus 13% for BI-RADS group 1); menopausal status was similar; and the authors state that characteristics were well balanced. There is no substantial imbalance in participant numbers or key reported prognostic factors suggesting a compromised randomization process.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The trial used randomised minimisation with a random element, allocation was conducted through a central telephone system, and the reported baseline characteristics do not indicate a failure of randomization. Therefore, the primary outcome result is at low risk of bias arising from the randomization process.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?** **Yes.**  
  The trial was explicitly open label. Participants knew whether they had MRI or no further imaging.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial?** **Yes.**  
  The trial was open label; surgeons, radiologists, research nurses, and the multidisciplinary team necessarily knew whether MRI had been performed and could use MRI findings in surgical planning.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?** **Probably no.**  
  There were departures from assigned imaging: 55 of 816 patients in the MRI group did not have an interpretable/used MRI result or did not receive MRI, and nine patients assigned to no MRI received MRI. However, the report does not establish that these departures arose because the trial context caused personnel or participants to undermine the protocol. Several specified reasons for non-receipt of MRI were technical failure, MRI unavailability before surgery, refusal, non-attendance, claustrophobia, or unsuitability for MRI. The protocol also explicitly required that MRI should not delay surgery. Surgical changes following MRI findings were part of the intended MRI strategy, not deviations from it.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?** **Yes.**  
  The article states that analysis was by intention to treat. The primary analysis included 816 participants allocated to MRI and 807 allocated to no MRI, irrespective of whether participants actually received MRI. The two persons shown in the flow chart as randomised after surgery were not included in the stated intention-to-treat population; because surgery had already occurred before assignment, they were not eligible to contribute to the prospective imaging comparison.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
Although the study was open label and there was incomplete receipt of allocated MRI plus limited cross-over, the primary outcome was analysed according to randomized allocation. The report does not show that deviations from imaging assignment arose because of the trial context or that they were differential protocol departures likely to bias the assignment effect. Therefore, the primary intention-to-treat result is at low risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions.

**Reporting limitation:**  
The flow diagram reports 1625 people randomised, whereas the text reports 1623 randomised and analysed (816 MRI; 807 no MRI). The diagram attributes the difference to two patients randomised after surgery. This inconsistency is documented, but the reported primary analysis includes the stated randomized intention-to-treat population of 1623.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?** **Yes.**  
  The primary analysis included 816/816 participants in the MRI group and 807/807 participants in the no-MRI group. The flow diagram reports primary-endpoint data unavailable for three MRI-group participants and four no-MRI-group participants, plus losses to follow-up of four and six participants, respectively. These participants were classified as not having a primary-endpoint event.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?** **Not applicable.**  
  The amount of unavailable primary-outcome information was very small in relation to the randomized sample and the number of observed primary events.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
At most, primary-endpoint information was unavailable for 7/816 (0.9%) participants in the MRI group and 10/807 (1.2%) in the no-MRI group, with similarly low and closely balanced proportions between groups. There were 309 observed primary events, far exceeding the number with unavailable outcome information. Consequently, the missing outcome data are too limited to make an important difference to the estimated intervention effect, regardless of the assumption that those lost to follow-up had no primary event.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?** **No.**  
  Repeat surgery and mastectomy were determined from clinical and surgical follow-up. The “pathologically avoidable mastectomy” component was assessed using MRI, histopathology, and criteria established by the independent Data Monitoring and Ethics Committee and agreed by the Trial Steering Committee. These are appropriate sources for determining the prespecified composite primary outcome.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?** **Probably no.**  
  Both groups had clinical follow-up at 6 and 12 months after surgery and then annually. Repeat surgical procedures were recorded for both groups. The same 6-month primary-outcome period and the same clinical follow-up approach applied to both randomized groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received?** **No information.**  
  The trial was open label. Although MRI quality-assurance rereading was undertaken by an independent radiologist masked to the original MRI findings, this concerned scan quality and interpretation, not ascertainment of the primary outcome. The article does not state that those determining the primary composite outcome, particularly the DMEC determining whether an initial mastectomy was pathologically avoidable, were blinded to randomized MRI assignment.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?** **Probably yes.**  
  The repeat-operation components are largely objective surgical events. However, the composite includes “pathologically avoidable mastectomy,” which required an adjudicative judgment relating MRI findings, surgical management, and histopathology. The determination could potentially be influenced by knowledge that MRI had been undertaken.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?** **Probably no.**  
  The report describes pre-established pathological criteria for defining an avoidable mastectomy, and the assessment was made by an independent DMEC. It does not provide evidence that outcome classification was influenced by awareness of intervention allocation. However, blinding of the adjudicators is not reported.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The principal component of the outcome—whether repeat surgery occurred—is objective and was obtained through follow-up in both groups. Nevertheless, the primary composite outcome included a component requiring adjudication of whether a mastectomy was pathologically avoidable. The report does not state that the DMEC or other outcome assessors were blinded to MRI assignment. Because knowledge of MRI status could potentially influence that adjudication, but no evidence shows that it did, this domain raises some concerns rather than high risk of bias.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available?** **No information.**  
  The paper identifies the primary outcome and describes the logistic-regression analysis adjusted for minimisation factors. It also gives the trial registration number. However, no date-stamped protocol or statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the precise primary-outcome analysis was finalized before unblinded outcome data became available was provided in the article materials assessed here.

- **5.2 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain on the basis of the results?** **Probably no.**  
  The primary endpoint is explicitly defined as a 6-month composite of repeat operation/further mastectomy or pathologically avoidable initial mastectomy. The reported numerical result corresponds to this defined primary endpoint. The article does not provide evidence that the reported primary endpoint was selected from several eligible primary-outcome measures or time points on the basis of its result.

- **5.3 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data on the basis of the results?** **No information.**  
  The article reports an adjusted logistic-regression result and also presents event counts. It does not provide a pre-specified statistical analysis plan or sufficient information to determine whether other eligible analyses—such as unadjusted analysis, alternative handling of the small number of unavailable outcomes, or alternative specifications of the composite—were performed and selectively not reported.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
There is no direct evidence that the reported primary result was selected because it was statistically favourable or otherwise noteworthy. However, the absence of an available date-stamped prespecified analysis plan means that it cannot be verified that the reported analysis was selected before unblinded data were available, nor can selection among multiple possible eligible analyses be excluded. Under RoB 2, this leads to some concerns.
