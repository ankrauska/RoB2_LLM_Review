# ROB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Enders J, et al. "High-Field Open versus Short-Bore Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Spine: A Randomized Controlled Comparison of Image Quality." PLoS ONE 2013.

**Result assessed:** Overall qualitative image quality score (primary comparative outcome) comparing short-bore vs. open MR imaging of the spine, analyzed via Mann-Whitney rank sum test.

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (as-randomized comparison, acknowledging cross-referral was per-protocol management of claustrophobic events).

---

## Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

Motivation: The allocation sequence was computer-generated (random) and allocation concealment was achieved using sealed envelopes, satisfying criteria for adequate concealment. However, randomization was explicitly **not stratified**, despite the trial drawing from a larger cohort with three different anatomical indications (cervicothoracic, thoracolumbar, whole spine) and known baseline variability (BMI, claustrophobia scores). Table 1 shows generally well-matched baseline characteristics (all P>0.05), consistent with chance, supporting adequate randomization. However, a more important concern is that **this specific subgroup of 93 spine patients was not itself the unit of primary randomization** — patients were randomized within a larger cohort of 174 patients across three anatomical regions (head, spine, shoulder), and the spine subset was analyzed post-hoc as one of several anatomical strata. This raises the possibility that the randomization process, while adequate at the level of the parent trial, was not specifically balanced for this outcome/subgroup, and the fact that 13 of 106 eligible spine patients did not undergo imaging (excluded due to claustrophobic events) after randomization introduces potential post-randomization attrition relevant to baseline comparability of the analyzed sample. This is reflected more directly in Domain 3, but it also raises some concern regarding whether the randomized comparison analyzed here fully reflects the original allocation. Given no evidence of baseline imbalance and adequate concealment/sequence generation, but incomplete certainty about the appropriateness of subgroup-level randomization integrity, a judgment of "Some concerns" is assigned.

---

## Domain 2: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

Motivation: Blinding of participants and personnel to intervention was **impossible** by design — patients and MR technologists were necessarily aware of which scanner (open vs. short-bore) was being used (an inherent feature of the intervention). This alone does not create high risk of bias, since deviations must additionally arise from trial context and affect the outcome in an unbalanced way.

However, in this trial, **substantial deviations from assigned intervention occurred**: 25 of 93 examinations (27%) were performed after **cross-referral** to the alternate scanner following a claustrophobic event, and 16 of these required conscious sedation. This is a considerable and clinically meaningful deviation from the intended (as-randomized) intervention, arising directly from the trial context (patient inability to tolerate the assigned scanner due to claustrophobia — the very condition under study). These cross-referred patients were **included in the primary analysis** under the group corresponding to the scanner in which they were eventually imaged, meaning the analyzed groups do not correspond strictly to the original random allocation. This constitutes a substantial post-randomization deviation, likely to differentially affect the two arms (claustrophobic events were reported to occur at different rates and directions between the two scanner types in the parent study), and is likely to affect the outcome (image quality), since sedated/anxious patients or motion artifacts could alter qualitative/quantitative scores. Although the authors performed subgroup analyses excluding cross-referred patients and found consistent results ("equally distributed results," P<0.0001), the primary reported analysis itself is at high risk because it does not reflect a pure intention-to-treat comparison, and the appropriateness of the primary analysis method (mixing per-protocol crossover patients into an ITT framework without adjustment) is questionable. Given deviations arose from trial context, were unbalanced in nature (asymmetric claustrophobia-driven crossover), and likely affected the outcome, and no appropriate statistical method (e.g., instrumental variable or sensitivity-adjusted analysis) was used to correct for this in the primary reported result, this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

---

## Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgment: Some concerns**

Motivation: Of 106 eligible patients with a clinical indication for spinal MR imaging, only 93 (87.7%) underwent MR imaging and were included in the analysis; 13 patients experienced a "claustrophobic event" preventing imaging in either scanner and were excluded from the image-quality analysis entirely (per Figure 1). This amounts to missing outcome data in slightly over 12% of the intended sample. Critically, this missingness is **highly likely to be related to the true value of relevant outcomes/patient characteristics** (these patients could not complete imaging due to claustrophobia, which is plausibly associated with different scanner tolerability, though the outcome being assessed here — image quality — was simply not measurable in these patients rather than differentially poor). No sensitivity analysis or imputation was performed to address this exclusion, and the flow diagram does not report the exact per-arm breakdown of these 13 exclusions in relation to original allocation. Since the amount of missing data is nontrivial (12%) but reasons are transparently documented (claustrophobic event preventing scanning) and are not likely to differentially bias the *image quality* comparison itself (since no image was produced, these participants contribute no outcome data rather than a systematically different value), the risk is not judged "High," but the lack of clarity on the per-group distribution of these exclusions and absence of sensitivity analysis supports "Some concerns."

---

## Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

Motivation: The method of measuring the outcome (qualitative scoring by two blinded examiners in consensus, and quantitative SNR/CNR/contour sharpness measurement by objective software-based methods) was appropriate and validated by prior literature (cited methodology for CNR/SNR and contour sharpness). Measurement/ascertainment methods were kept comparable between groups (same scoring scales, same anatomical ROIs, standardized approach), minimizing differential ascertainment. Critically, the outcome assessors performing both qualitative and quantitative image analysis were **explicitly blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity** at the time of analysis ("At the time of the analysis the examiners were blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity"). Given that outcome assessors were blinded and the outcome (image quality) is an observer-reported outcome involving judgement, but such judgement could not be influenced by knowledge of intervention received due to effective blinding, risk of bias in this domain is low. Quantitative measures (SNR, CNR, contour sharpness) are objective, software/formula-derived measurements, further reducing risk of differential measurement error.

---

## Domain 5: Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

Motivation: The trial was registered prospectively (NCT00715806) and a detailed trial protocol was published in advance (BMC Med Imaging 2011), which is a strength. The primary outcome of the *registered* trial was the reduction of claustrophobic reactions (reported in a separate 2011 PLoS ONE publication), while the *image quality comparison* reported in this specific paper is a **secondary/exploratory analysis** of a subset of the original cohort, using multiple qualitative parameters (overall quality, contrast, contour sharpness, noise, artifacts) and multiple quantitative parameters (SNR, CNR for multiple tissue interfaces, contour sharpness for multiple interfaces) across three anatomical regions and three sequence types. This creates substantial multiplicity of possible outcome measurements and analyses. While all pre-specified qualitative/quantitative parameters described in the protocol appear to have been reported comprehensively (with no evident selective omission — all sequences, all parameters, and all anatomical regions are presented systematically in Tables 3–4), the sheer number of comparisons performed (many dozens of P-values across parameters, sequences, and regions) raises the possibility of selective emphasis in the Discussion on the most favorable/statistically significant findings, without correction for multiplicity. Additionally, it is not fully verifiable from the available text whether the specific analysis methods (e.g., choice of Mann-Whitney test, specific ROI placement criteria) were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available, as the statistical analysis plan is not independently accessible for granular verification in this assessment. Given comprehensive reporting of pre-specified parameters but uncertainty regarding pre-specification of the exact analytic hierarchy and potential multiplicity concerns, "Some concerns" is judged, this ambiguity being reported as a limitation of this assessment rather than assumed as bias.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall judgment: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Per RoB 2 rules, an overall judgment of "High risk of bias" is warranted because the study was judged to be at **High risk of bias in Domain 2 (deviations from intended interventions)**, driven by substantial (27%) unaddressed post-randomization crossover between intervention arms due to claustrophobic events, without appropriate statistical correction in the primary reported analysis. This alone is sufficient to trigger an overall "High risk of bias" judgment. This is compounded by "Some concerns" in three additional domains (randomization process, missing outcome data, and selection of the reported result), which further diminishes confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported effect estimate for image quality comparison between the two MR scanner types.

**Note on limitations of this assessment:** This judgment is based solely on the information available in the published article, its supplementary protocol reference, and the linked trial registry record. Full statistical analysis plans, complete CONSORT flow data disaggregated by original randomized allocation (rather than by scanner ultimately used), and pre-specified per-protocol analysis plans for handling crossover patients were not independently accessible for verification, and this ambiguity is reported as a limitation rather than being resolved by assumption.
