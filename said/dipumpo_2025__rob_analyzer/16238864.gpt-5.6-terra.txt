## Scope of assessment

- **Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group pre–post trial.
- **Experimental intervention:** Brief leaflet-like motivational and implementation-intention intervention promoting the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables (RDIFV).
- **Comparator:** Questionnaire-focused control task, taking approximately the same time.
- **Result assessed:** Effect of assignment to the intervention versus control on **self-reported number of days meeting the RDIFV during the 1-week follow-up**, analysed using ANCOVA controlling for baseline RDIFV: *F*(1,143) = 6.83, *p* < .01; reported follow-up means 3.03 days (intervention) versus 2.28 days (control).
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment to intervention** (intention-to-treat effect).
- **Sources available for this assessment:** The article and its tables only. No protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial registry record, or other prespecified source was provided. This is an important limitation particularly for Domains 1 and 5.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states that intervention and control booklets were “placed in a single pile in an order determined by a sequence of random integers generated by a true random number generator.” This describes a sequence-generation process with a random component.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The study reports that booklets were placed in one pile and distributed in a lecture theatre and laboratory class. It does not state that booklets were identical in appearance, sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, distributed by an independent person, or otherwise protected from foreknowledge of the next allocation. Thus, allocation concealment cannot be confirmed from the report. However, the report does not provide direct evidence that the allocation sequence was known or manipulated; therefore, a high-risk judgement is not warranted on the available evidence.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No information.**  
  The article reports no statistically significant differences between groups in pre-intervention cognition and behaviour, age, or gender. However, the reported tests have degrees of freedom consistent with the follow-up/analysed sample rather than all 218 randomized participants. Therefore, the report does not provide a clearly documented baseline comparison for the full randomized population. No imbalance suggestive of failed randomization is reported, but the available information does not permit a fully secure assessment.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The random sequence itself was adequately generated. Nevertheless, allocation concealment was not described, and baseline comparability appears to have been assessed in a subset of participants rather than unequivocally in all randomized participants. Under RoB 2, absence of information about allocation concealment, without evidence of major baseline imbalance or explicit allocation manipulation, leads to **some concerns** rather than high risk.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Participants completed either the intervention material, which included persuasive messages and implementation-intention planning, or a control task asking questions about the questionnaire experience. These conditions were materially different, and participants necessarily knew the content they received.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably yes.**  
  The intervention and control booklets were distributed and completed in classroom settings. The report does not describe blinding of the researchers or personnel distributing/administering booklets. Given the visibly different contents of the two conditions, personnel delivering the study procedures were probably aware of allocation.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  Participants were asked not to discuss their booklets over the following week to reduce cross-condition contamination. However, the article does not report whether contamination, differential co-interventions, departures from assigned materials, or other trial-context deviations actually occurred.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The behavioural outcome was analysed by assigned experimental condition using ANCOVA controlling for the baseline outcome. The report does not state that participants were analysed according to treatment received or that non-adherent participants were excluded because of their intervention behaviour. The analysis therefore appears appropriate for assignment to intervention among participants with observed follow-up outcomes. The substantial missing outcome data are considered separately in Domain 3.

**Motivation for judgement**  
Participants and likely study personnel were unblinded. The report does not establish whether this resulted in trial-context deviations that affected the outcome. The analysis retained participants in their randomized groups among those with observed data, which is appropriate for this domain. However, because the study provides no information about whether unblinding led to contamination or other differential deviations from intended intervention, this domain raises **some concerns**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  A total of 218 participants completed the baseline booklet, but only 146 responded at 1-week follow-up. The overall attrition rate was therefore 33% (72/218 participants without follow-up outcome data). This is not “all or nearly all” participants.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The article reports no analysis correcting for potentially informative missing data, no multiple imputation, no inverse-probability weighting, and no sensitivity analyses assessing whether plausible outcomes among non-responders could alter the intervention effect.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  The authors report that participants who completed follow-up had significantly higher pre-intervention intentions, anticipated regret, and fruit and vegetable consumption than those who did not complete follow-up. Because baseline fruit and vegetable consumption and motivational variables are related to the follow-up behavioural outcome, this demonstrates that follow-up completion was associated with prognostic baseline characteristics.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  The observed association between attrition and baseline fruit and vegetable consumption, intention, and anticipated regret makes it likely that missingness was related to participants’ subsequent fruit and vegetable consumption. The report states that dropout rates did not differ significantly by condition, but this does not resolve the risk: when missingness is related to the outcome and the intervention may affect that outcome, complete-case analysis can yield a biased intervention effect even without a statistically significant between-group difference in overall dropout rates.

**Motivation for judgement**  
One-third of randomized participants did not provide the 1-week behavioural outcome. More importantly, attrition was demonstrably associated with baseline consumption and motivational characteristics related to the outcome. The study used an analysis based on participants with observed follow-up data and did not provide sensitivity analyses or an analysis addressing the potential impact of missingness. Under RoB 2, this combination warrants a judgement of **high risk of bias** due to missing outcome data.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  The outcome was measured using self-report items asking on how many days during the previous week participants consumed one piece of fruit, four serving spoons of vegetables, and the combined RDIFV. The article acknowledges that these were self-report measures and that biochemical validation or diary measures would be useful in future research. However, the report does not establish that the questionnaire method was invalid or incapable of detecting a plausible intervention effect. Therefore, the measurement method itself is not judged inappropriate.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  The same self-report behavioural questions were administered to both groups at the same 1-week follow-up interval. The article does not report different questionnaires, thresholds, follow-up schedules, or ascertainment procedures by condition.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Yes.**  
  For a self-reported dietary outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor. Participants were aware of whether they had received the fruit-and-vegetable persuasive and planning intervention or the control task.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.**  
  The outcome required participants to recall and report their own fruit and vegetable consumption. This is a participant-reported outcome and is therefore capable of being influenced by awareness of the assigned condition.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably yes.**  
  The intervention explicitly encouraged participants to make a “firm decision” to meet the RDIFV, stated that doing so was easy and within their control, and required them to plan purchases and meals. The primary outcome was a short-term self-report of the exact behaviour promoted by the intervention. No blinding, objective dietary biomarker, food diary validation, or independent outcome ascertainment was used. In this context, awareness of intervention assignment is likely to have influenced self-reported consumption.

**Motivation for judgement**  
Although both groups completed the same follow-up questionnaire, the outcome was subjective self-report by unblinded participants. The intervention directly targeted the socially desirable behaviour being reported and included explicit planning exercises around that behaviour. The article itself identifies reliance on self-report as a limitation and notes the absence of biochemical validation. In accordance with RoB 2, the likely influence of knowledge of intervention received on an unblinded participant-reported outcome leads to **high risk of bias** in outcome measurement.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial registry entry was available in the supplied material. The article describes hypotheses and analyses but does not provide evidence that the analysis plan was finalized before outcome data were unblinded.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The article reports three related behavioural outcomes at follow-up: days consuming at least one piece of fruit, days consuming four or more serving spoons of vegetables, and days consuming the combined RDIFV. The combined RDIFV outcome is directly aligned with the stated intervention aim and hypothesis. However, in the absence of a prospectively finalized protocol or analysis plan, it cannot be confirmed whether this particular measurement and time point were selected without reference to results.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The behavioural results were analysed using ANCOVA controlling for pre-intervention scores, but the article provides no prospectively specified plan confirming this analytic approach, the intended covariates, handling of missing data, or whether alternative analyses were performed. There is no direct evidence that the reported analysis was selected because it was favourable, but the absence of prespecified analysis intentions prevents a low-risk judgement.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The publication reports outcomes consistent with its stated hypotheses, and there is no direct evidence that the reported RDIFV result was selected from multiple analyses or measurements because of statistical significance or direction of effect. However, no protocol, registry record, or prespecified statistical analysis plan was available to verify this. RoB 2 therefore supports a judgement of **some concerns**, rather than low risk or high risk, for selective reporting of this result.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation**  
The result is at **high risk of bias** because at least one domain is judged at high risk:

1. **Missing outcome data:** 33% attrition, with follow-up completion associated with baseline fruit and vegetable consumption and motivational variables, and no sensitivity or bias-adjusted analyses.
2. **Measurement of the outcome:** unblinded participants self-reported the exact dietary behaviour promoted by the intervention, with no objective or independently assessed outcome measure.

There are additionally **some concerns** regarding allocation concealment, possible deviations from intended intervention in an unblinded study, and lack of a prespecified analysis plan. Therefore, according to the specified RoB 2 overall-judgement rules, the assessed 1-week self-reported RDIFV result is at **high risk of bias**.
