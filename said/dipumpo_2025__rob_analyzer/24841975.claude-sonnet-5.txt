# RoB 2 Assessment: CLAVELA Trial (Elacytarabine vs Investigator's Choice in Relapsed/Refractory AML)

**Preliminary considerations:**
- Design: Individually-randomized parallel-group trial
- Experimental: Elacytarabine; Comparator: Investigator's choice (7 regimens)
- Outcome assessed: Overall Survival (primary endpoint)
- Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)
- Result assessed: HR 0.97 (95% CI, 0.77 to 1.22); median OS 3.5 vs 3.3 months

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- 1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? **Probably yes.** Randomization was conducted via a centralized Interactive Web Response System (IWRS), stratified by intensity of prior therapy and remission status, with a block size of four. This implies a random component, though the specific method of sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated) is not explicitly described.
- 1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed? **Yes.** Central randomization via IWRS is a robust concealment mechanism, minimizing risk of foreknowledge of assignment.
- 1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization? **Yes.** Table 1 shows a statistically significant imbalance in age distribution: patients aged ≥70 years were significantly more common in the elacytarabine arm (30% vs 18%, P=.007). While block randomization with small block size (4) generally protects against major imbalance, this specific, clinically relevant prognostic imbalance (age is a major prognostic factor in AML) raises concern that it may not be entirely attributable to chance, or at minimum introduces confounding potential that was substantial enough for the authors themselves to perform an age-adjusted analysis.

**Motivation:** Although concealment was adequate (central IWRS) and sequence generation was probably random, the presence of a significant baseline imbalance in age (a key prognostic factor in AML, explicitly acknowledged by the authors who performed sensitivity analyses) raises concern about whether the randomization process was fully successful in producing balanced groups. This combination of adequate concealment/randomness with a concerning baseline imbalance maps to "Some concerns" per the RoB 2 algorithm (Table 3/4).

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention) — **Low risk**

**Signalling questions:**
- 2.1 Were participants aware of assigned intervention? **Yes** (open-label design).
- 2.2 Were carers/personnel aware of assigned intervention? **Yes** (open-label design).
- 2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of trial context? **Probably no.** The paper reports that only 6 patients in the control arm received treatment not according to the prespecified investigator's choice (e.g., substitutions like FLAG-Ida instead of MEC, or hydroxyurea instead of palliative care), and these appear to be clinical/logistical substitutions rather than deviations driven by trial-context-related behavioral effects (e.g., participant-driven crossover due to unmasking). There is no evidence of systematic contamination or non-protocol interventions driven by awareness of assignment.
- 2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used? **Yes.** The primary analysis was intention-to-treat, including all 381 randomized patients (191 elacytarabine, 190 control), consistent with ITT principles.
- 2.7 N/A given 2.6 = Yes.

**Motivation:** Despite the open-label design (unavoidable given the nature of comparing an infusional novel agent against heterogeneous salvage regimens, including oral/palliative options), there is no evidence that trial-context-driven deviations (e.g., recruitment/engagement effects) meaningfully affected outcome ascertainment for OS, an objective and non-manipulable endpoint. The small number of protocol deviations (6/190, ~3%) is unlikely to have biased the ITT effect, and the analysis appropriately included all randomized patients. Because OS is an objective, hard endpoint unlikely to be influenced by lack of blinding, and the analysis was properly ITT, this domain is judged **low risk**.

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Low risk**

**Signalling question:**
- 3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? **Yes.** The Patient Disposition (Figure 2) indicates that of 191 elacytarabine patients, only 1 (1%) was lost to follow-up, and of 190 control patients, 4 (2%) were lost to follow-up/withdrawn. All other patients had a documented status (alive at study end or event/death). The analysis explicitly states that patients confirmed alive at cutoff were censored at that date, and those lost to follow-up before cutoff were censored at their last known-alive date — a pre-specified and transparent approach to handling the (very small) amount of missing data.

**Motivation:** For the primary outcome of overall survival, outcome ascertainment (death vs censoring) was available for nearly all randomized patients (only 1% and 2% lost to follow-up in the two arms respectively). This proportion is far too small to have had an important impact on the treatment effect estimate for OS, particularly with 300+ events observed as pre-specified. Therefore, this domain is judged **low risk** of bias.

---

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk**

**Signalling questions:**
- 4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? **No.** Overall survival was measured objectively as time to death from any cause, using Kaplan-Meier methodology with a clearly defined cutoff date (300th event), which is an entirely appropriate and standard method.
- 4.2 Could measurement/ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? **No.** Death is an objective, unambiguous endpoint not subject to differential ascertainment procedures between arms; there is no indication that survival status was determined using different methods or intensities of follow-up between the two arms.
- 4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? **Yes** (open-label design; unavoidable given the study design), but this is not judged to introduce bias given the nature of the outcome (see 4.4/4.5 reasoning below).
- 4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? **No.** All-cause mortality is an "observer-reported outcome not involving judgement" per RoB2 guidance categories — ascertainment of death does not require subjective interpretation and is not influenced by awareness of treatment assignment.

**Motivation:** Because the primary outcome (OS) is a hard, objective endpoint (all-cause mortality) that cannot reasonably be influenced by knowledge of treatment assignment, lack of blinding of outcome assessors (an inherent feature of this open-label, multi-regimen trial) does not introduce risk of bias for this specific outcome. This domain is therefore judged **low risk**.

---

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Low risk**

**Signalling questions:**
- 5.1 Were the data analysed in accordance with a pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? **Probably yes.** The trial registration (NCT01147939) pre-specifies OS as the primary endpoint, with clearly pre-specified secondary endpoints (response rate, relapse-free survival, safety). The statistical methods section describes a pre-planned sample size/event-driven analysis (300 events, 87% power to detect HR 0.70), an unambiguous a priori design feature indicating the analysis plan was fixed prior to unblinding.
- 5.2 Was the result likely selected, based on results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements? **No.** OS was measured and reported in a single, standard way (time to death from randomization), with no evidence of alternative outcome definitions being available and selectively suppressed.
- 5.3 Was the result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses? **No.** The primary OS analysis (log-rank test, Cox proportional hazards, ITT population) is reported straightforwardly, along with pre-specified sensitivity analyses (age-adjusted, SCT-censored) that are transparently presented as secondary/exploratory rather than substituted for the primary result.

**Motivation:** There is no indication of selective reporting for the primary outcome of OS; the trial registry and statistical design confirm a pre-specified, event-driven analysis plan for this endpoint, and results are reported comprehensively and consistently with this plan. This domain is judged **low risk**.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement — **Some concerns**

**Rationale:** Applying the RoB 2 algorithm for overall judgement:
- Domain 1 (Randomization process): **Some concerns**
- Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions): **Low**
- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data): **Low**
- Domain 4 (Measurement of outcome): **Low**
- Domain 5 (Selection of reported result): **Low**

Per RoB 2 rules, since the study raises "some concerns" in exactly one domain (randomization process, due to the significant and prognostically relevant baseline age imbalance) but is not judged to be at high risk of bias in any domain, the overall risk-of-bias judgement for this result (Overall Survival, elacytarabine vs. investigator's choice) is:

**➤ SOME CONCERNS**

**Limitation/ambiguity to note:** The exact method of random sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated randomization algorithm) was not explicitly described in the article beyond reference to a centralized IWRS system and stratified block randomization (block size = 4); this required a judgement of "probably yes" rather than a definitive "yes" for signalling question 1.1, based on the plausibility that a large multinational academic/industry-sponsored trial run through a validated IWRS would have used an adequate randomization algorithm. This is reported as a specific ambiguity rather than an assumption, in accordance with RoB 2 guidance regarding transparency of judgement-based responses.
