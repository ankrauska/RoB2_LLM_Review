# RoB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Effects of bright and blue light on acoustic reaction time and maximum handgrip strength in male athletes: a randomized controlled trial

**Design:** Individually-randomized, 3-arm parallel-group trial (BRIGHT vs BLUE vs CONTROL)

**Outcomes assessed:** (1) Reaction time (ms); (2) Maximum handgrip strength (kg)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

**Numerical results assessed:**
- Reaction time: BRIGHT vs CONTROL −1 ms (95% CI −8, 6); BLUE vs CONTROL 2 ms (95% CI −5, 9)
- Handgrip strength: BRIGHT vs CONTROL 0.9 kg (95% CI −1.5, 3.3); BLUE vs CONTROL −0.3 kg (95% CI −2.7, 2.0)

---

## Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- Sequence generation: "Participants were allocated randomly... using an online tool (www.randomization.com) which applies permuted block randomization with randomly varying block sizes of 3, 6, and 9." This is an adequate, truly random method (Signalling Q1.1 = Yes).
- Allocation concealment: "The list was transmitted by a non-involved person using serially numbered sealed opaque envelopes." This constitutes adequate allocation concealment (Signalling Q1.2 = Yes), as it meets minimal criteria (sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes handled by an independent party).
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 (referenced) shows similar group sizes (24/24/24) after exclusions, and reported baseline characteristics (age, VO2max, sport type distribution) appear comparable across groups with no indication of problematic imbalance (Signalling Q1.3 = No). No statistical tests indicating major imbalance are reported, and imbalances described are compatible with chance.

All three criteria for "Low risk" are met: adequate concealment, random sequence, and no evidence of problematic baseline imbalance.

---

## Domain 2: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Blinding of participants: The protocol explicitly states masking = "NONE." The article confirms: "Participants only knew the light condition they were exposed to, but not the other two light conditions" — implying participants were aware of their own assigned condition (Signalling Q2.1 = Yes).
- Blinding of personnel: The test supervisor (RK) was blinded to light condition ("The test supervisor (RK) was blinded to the light condition each participant was exposed to"), which mitigates some risk related to personnel administering the light exposure and outcome tasks (Signalling Q2.2 = Probably No, for the supervisor conducting testing; however, personnel setting up the light devices in the three separate rooms were presumably aware, as they had to configure the different light devices/rooms).
- Deviations arising from trial context: There is no explicit evidence that participants' knowledge of their assigned light condition led to non-protocol interventions, contamination, or behavioral deviations affecting reaction time or handgrip strength performance. The tasks are objective, short-duration physical/psychomotor tests conducted in a controlled laboratory setting immediately after exposure, minimizing opportunity for behaviorally-mediated deviations (Signalling Q2.3 = Probably No, but not explicitly addressed in the text — no information provided on whether participant awareness influenced effort/motivation differentially, which is plausible given psychological anticipation effects tied to unblinded self-administered lighting).
- Analysis: The paper reports that 72 out of 74 randomized participants were analyzed. Two participants were excluded due to "invalid measurements of peak oxygen uptake in the cardiorespiratory fitness test" — a baseline eligibility/fitness-screening test conducted **before** randomization-relevant intervention exposure, not related to the light intervention itself or to knowledge of group assignment. This exclusion is plausibly unrelated to intervention received and can be considered an appropriate post-randomization exclusion of ineligible participants (Signalling Q2.6 = Probably Yes, appropriate ITT-consistent analysis, as groups were analyzed as randomized with all available data at 24/24/24).

**Overall for this domain:** Because participants were aware of their assigned light condition (unblinded to own arm), and there is no explicit statement ruling out that this awareness (e.g., differential motivation/expectation regarding "bright light boosting alertness") could have influenced effort on a subjective/voluntary maximal-effort task (handgrip strength, and to some extent reaction time, both of which depend on participant motivation and alertness — the exact psychological pathway the intervention purports to modify), this raises "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk." The analysis approach itself was appropriate (ITT-consistent, appropriate exclusion), so this domain does not reach "High risk."

**Limitation:** The article does not report whether trial context (e.g., participant expectation of receiving a "performance enhancing" bright light) could have differentially motivated effort during the self-administered maximal-effort handgrip test — this ambiguity could not be resolved from the reported information.

## Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- Outcome data (reaction time and handgrip strength) appear to be available for all 72 analyzed participants (24 per group), with pre- and post-light exposure values reported in Table 2 for all three groups (Signalling Q3.1 = Yes).
- The two exclusions (one each in BRIGHT and CONTROL) were due to invalid VO2peak measurements in the baseline fitness screening test, unrelated to the reaction time/handgrip outcomes or their true values, and occurred prior to allocation-relevant intervention exposure. There is no indication of differential loss to follow-up between groups for the specific outcomes assessed in this report.
- No dropout after randomization/light exposure is reported; all remaining 72 participants appear to have completed both pre- and post-tests.

Since outcome data are available for essentially all randomized (and analyzed) participants with no evidence of missingness related to the true outcome value, this domain is judged as low risk.

---

## Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Method of measurement: The reaction time task (computer-based simple acoustic reaction time) and handgrip strength test (hydraulic hand dynamometer, mean of three trials) are objective, standardized, validated instruments; the method of measurement itself is not inappropriate (Signalling Q4.1 = No).
- Measurement comparability across groups: Both tasks were performed identically before and after light exposure in room-light conditions (~230 lx) across all three groups, using the same equipment and procedures (Signalling Q4.2 = No).
- Awareness of outcome assessors: While the test supervisor administering/scoring the tasks was blinded to allocation ("The test supervisor (RK) was blinded to the light condition each participant was exposed to"), the outcome assessor for both outcomes is effectively the **participant themselves**, since reaction time and handgrip strength are self-performed, effort-dependent measures. Participants were aware of their own assigned condition (Signalling Q4.3 = Yes, participants — the de facto outcome assessors for these effort-based outcomes — were aware of intervention received).
- Potential for influence: Both outcomes (reaction time, a psychomotor/vigilance measure, and handgrip strength, a maximal voluntary effort measure) could plausibly be influenced by participants' expectations regarding the light's purported alerting/performance effects, i.e., participants who believed they were receiving an "activating" bright/blue light might exert differential effort or attention compared to those in the room-light control condition (Signalling Q4.4 = Yes/Probably Yes, could be influenced).
- Likelihood of actual influence: The article does not provide direct evidence (e.g., a manipulation-check of participant expectations) that assessment was actually influenced by awareness of allocation, nor strong reason to believe it was substantially influenced, given the light exposure/testing paradigm is relatively novel to participants and beliefs about specific light effects on physical performance are not necessarily strong or widely held among athletes (Signalling Q4.5 = Probably No — no clear evidence of strong prior expectation effects, but cannot be ruled out).

**Overall for this domain:** Because the outcome assessor (participant) was aware of the intervention received, and the outcomes are subject to potential motivational/expectation-based influence, but there is no clear evidence that such influence actually occurred, the domain is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low" or "High."

**Limitation:** No blinding of participants was feasible or attempted for the light exposure itself (masking = "NONE" per protocol), and the study did not report any assessment of participants' beliefs/expectations regarding the light's effects on these specific self-performed outcomes, which prevents a more definitive judgement.

## Domain 5: Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- The trial was prospectively registered (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02203539), and the protocol explicitly pre-specifies both reaction time (ms) and maximum handgrip strength (kg) as secondary outcomes with the exact metrics reported in the article (Signalling Q5.1 = Yes — matches registered secondary outcomes).
- The article states: "All statistical methods used to compare the groups for reaction time and handgrip strength in the present report were pre-specified and registered," and analyses of covariance (adjusting for baseline outcome values) is a standard, singular, pre-specified approach — not one selected from multiple analyses post hoc (Signalling Q5.3 = No).
- There is a single measurement per outcome domain (single reaction time task with median reaction time computed; single handgrip strength protocol with mean of three trials), with no evidence of multiple alternative outcome measurements/instruments from which the reported one was selectively chosen (Signalling Q5.2 = No).
- Both primary comparisons (BRIGHT vs CONTROL; BLUE vs CONTROL) and additional group comparisons (BRIGHT vs BLUE) and dose-response (regression on lx) analyses are reported comprehensively and consistently, including non-significant results, without apparent selective omission.

Given close correspondence between the registered protocol outcomes/analysis plan and the reported results, with no evidence of selective reporting, this domain is judged as low risk of bias.

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**For both outcomes (Reaction time and Handgrip strength): SOME CONCERNS**

**Justification (per RoB 2 rules):**
- Domain 1 (Randomization): Low risk
- Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions): Some concerns
- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data): Low risk
- Domain 4 (Measurement of outcome): Some concerns
- Domain 5 (Selection of reported result): Low risk

Since the study is judged to raise "Some concerns" in two domains (Domain 2 and Domain 4) but is not judged to be at "High risk of bias" in any single domain, and the concerns — both stemming from the same underlying issue of unblinded participants who also serve as the effort-dependent outcome assessors — do not compound to a degree that substantially undermines confidence in the result (the outcomes are relatively objective, short-duration, standardized tests, and the effect estimates are precise with narrow, non-significant confidence intervals reported transparently), the overall judgement per the RoB 2 algorithm is:

**Overall: SOME CONCERNS**

**Stated limitation:** The unblinded nature of the light exposure intervention (masking = "NONE") is an inherent methodological constraint for this type of intervention (participants can visually perceive bright/blue vs. room light), and this ambiguity regarding potential motivational/expectation effects on self-performed, effort-dependent outcome measures (reaction time and handgrip strength) could not be fully resolved based on the information reported in the article.
