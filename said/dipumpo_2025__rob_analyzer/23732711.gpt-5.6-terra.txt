## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

- **Design:** Individually randomized, 2×2 factorial, community-based parallel-group trial.
- **Result assessed:** Effect of **daily broad-spectrum sunscreen application** versus **discretionary sunscreen use** on change in skin photoaging (microtopography grade) from 1992 to 1996.
- **Numerical result assessed:** Adjusted relative odds of higher microtopography grade over time, daily versus discretionary sunscreen: **0.76 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.98)** (Table 4, model 2).
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment to daily versus discretionary sunscreen use** (intention-to-treat effect).
- **Sources available for this assessment:** The uploaded journal article and its tables/figures. Although the paper cites an earlier design/protocol publication, that protocol and a statistical analysis plan were not available in the supplied material.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?** **Yes.**  
  The article states that “one of our study investigators, who had no knowledge of the participants, randomly assigned them by using a **computer-generated randomized list**.” This is a random method of sequence generation.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?** **No information.**  
  The report does not describe allocation concealment. It does not report central randomization, an independent allocation service, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another method demonstrating that recruiters could not know or predict forthcoming assignments. The statement that the investigator generating allocations “had no knowledge of the participants” does not establish concealment from personnel enrolling participants.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?** **Probably no.**  
  The daily and discretionary sunscreen groups had nearly identical numbers at baseline (442 and 444). The reported baseline distributions of sex, age, skin colour, sun reaction, occupational sun exposure, sunburn history, history of skin cancer, neck photoaging, sunscreen use, recreational activity, and smoking were broadly similar. The article reports no baseline differences in phenotype, sun exposure, or pretrial sunscreen use between sunscreen groups. No marked imbalance indicates a failure of randomization.

**Judgement: Some concerns**

The allocation sequence was explicitly computer-generated and the baseline data do not suggest a compromised randomization process. However, allocation concealment was not reported. Under RoB 2, lack of information on concealment, despite otherwise apparently balanced groups, gives **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.

**Direction of bias:** Not predictable from the available report.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?** **Yes.**  
  Participants allocated to daily sunscreen were instructed to apply the supplied sunscreen to the head, neck, arms, and hands every morning and reapply after sweating, bathing, or prolonged outdoor exposure. Participants allocated to discretionary use did not receive a placebo sunscreen because this was considered unethical. Therefore, participants could distinguish the sunscreen strategies.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial?** **Yes.**  
  The sunscreen intervention was an open behavioural intervention, and the trial report does not describe blinding of staff responsible for follow-up, adherence assessments, or sunscreen provision.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?** **Probably no.**  
  The article reports differences in sunscreen-use frequency consistent with the assigned strategies: by 1996, 77% of daily-use participants reported applying sunscreen at least 3 to 4 days per week, compared with 33% of discretionary-use participants. This difference reflects implementation/adherence to the assigned strategies, rather than a reported non-protocol deviation caused by the trial context.

  Importantly, other reported sun-protection behaviours were similar during follow-up: usual shade-seeking was reported by 54% versus 53%, and usual hat use by 63% versus 67%, in daily and discretionary groups, respectively. Reported weekend outdoor exposure was also similar (78% vs. 76% spending at least half of weekend time outdoors). The report provides no evidence of unbalanced non-protocol co-interventions arising because participants or trial personnel knew allocations.

- **2.4–2.5 Were such deviations likely to affect the outcome, and were they balanced?** **Not applicable**, because deviations arising from the trial context were not identified.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?** **Probably yes.**  
  The investigators state that “data were analyzed according to treatment as randomly allocated.” The main analysis used generalized estimating equations to compare change in ordinal microtopography grades over time according to allocated sunscreen group. Participants with missing outcome data are addressed in the missing-outcome-data domain. The paper did not report an as-treated or naïve per-protocol result as the primary sunscreen effect estimate.

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

Participants and trial personnel were aware of sunscreen allocation, but lack of blinding alone does not establish bias in this domain for the effect of assignment. The report does not identify deviations from the intended strategy caused by the trial context that were likely to affect photoaging and unbalanced between groups. Reported outdoor exposure, shade use, and hat use were similar between sunscreen groups. The primary analysis was by randomized allocation.

**Direction of bias:** Not applicable/not predictable.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?** **No.**  
  The photoaging analysis concerned 903 participants younger than 55 years. Good-quality skin replicas were available for 817 participants at baseline and 673 participants at follow-up. Of the 886 participants with at least one good-quality replica, only 604 contributed both baseline and follow-up replicas; 213 contributed a baseline replica only and 69 a follow-up replica only. Thus, a substantial proportion did not have outcome data at both relevant time points.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?** **Probably no.**  
  The authors conducted complete-case and multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses and state that results were consistent with the primary analysis. However, the supplied report does not provide the numerical results of these analyses, details of the imputation model, or a sensitivity analysis testing plausible departures from missing-at-random assumptions. Therefore, the report does not establish that missing data could not have materially biased the result.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?** **Probably yes.**  
  This possibility cannot be excluded. Participants contributing only one replica were more likely than those with two replicas to have severe neck photoaging and a history of 2 to 5 sunburns—characteristics relevant to photoaging. Thus, missingness could be related to underlying sun damage/photoaging status.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?** **Probably no.**  
  The article reports that missing follow-up microtopography grade was not associated with baseline microtopography grade. It also states that treatment allocation was not associated with missing grades. These findings lessen, but do not eliminate, concern that the unobserved follow-up outcome depended on the true outcome value. The report does not provide sufficient information to conclude that such dependence was likely.

**Judgement: Some concerns**

Outcome data were not available at both time points for a substantial number of participants. The missingness mechanism is not fully resolved: one-replica participants differed in severe neck photoaging and sunburn history, while baseline hand microtopography grade was not associated with missing follow-up grade. The available sensitivity-analysis description is insufficient to demonstrate robustness to plausible informative missingness. This creates **some concerns**, but the available evidence does not establish that missingness was likely to depend on the unobserved outcome strongly enough to justify a high-risk judgement.

**Direction of bias:** Not predictable from the available report.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?** **No.**  
  Photoaging was assessed from silicone replicas of the back of the left hand using the Beagley and Gibson microtopography scale. The article states that this measure had been validated against dermal elastosis and was a valid measure of photoaging up to age 70 years. It also reports high intra-rater and inter-rater repeatability (weighted kappa values 0.81 and 0.86, respectively).

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?** **No.**  
  The same standardized procedure was used to obtain skin replicas from the same anatomical site at baseline and follow-up. Participants in all groups were instructed not to use moisturizer or sunscreen on the day of replica collection. The report provides no indication that outcome collection differed by sunscreen allocation.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?** **No.**  
  The report explicitly states that the experienced assessors grading the skin replicas were “unaware of treatment allocation.”

- **4.4–4.5 Could assessment have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received, and was it likely to be influenced?** **Not applicable**, because outcome assessors were blinded.

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

The outcome was measured with a standardized and reported valid/reliable method, using comparable procedures across groups. Crucially, assessors grading the silicone replicas were blinded to treatment allocation. Therefore, there is no identified material risk of bias in measurement of the photoaging outcome.

**Direction of bias:** Not applicable.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available?** **No information.**  
  The article identifies change in photoaging from 1992 to 1996 as the primary outcome and cites a prior design publication. However, the underlying protocol and any dated statistical analysis plan were not available in the supplied material. Therefore, it cannot be verified whether the specific generalized estimating equation analysis, covariate adjustment, handling of missing data, or selected numerical result were specified before unblinded outcome data were available.

- **5.2 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?** **Probably no.**  
  The report defines photoaging using hand-skin microtopography grades measured at baseline and follow-up and identifies change from 1992 to 1996 as the target outcome. The article does not provide evidence that multiple eligible photoaging scales, time points, or alternative outcome definitions were measured and selectively reported on the basis of findings.

- **5.3 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?** **No information.**  
  Multiple analyses are described: unadjusted and adjusted models, complete-case analysis, and multiple imputation. The article reports the main models and states that sensitivity analyses were consistent, but the pre-specified hierarchy of analyses and full results of the sensitivity analyses are not supplied. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was favourable, but the absence of a pre-specified, dated analysis plan means this cannot be excluded.

**Judgement: Some concerns**

There is no direct evidence of selective reporting, and the primary photoaging outcome and target follow-up period are stated. Nevertheless, the supplied material does not include a prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan that permits verification of the chosen outcome analysis and handling of missing data. Under RoB 2, this leaves **some concerns** about selection of the reported result.

**Direction of bias:** Not predictable from the available report.
