## RoB 2 assessment

**Assessment target:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial comparing a single pre-exposure dose of **250 mg D-cycloserine (DCS)** with matching placebo in adults with social anxiety disorder.  
**Result assessed:** The prespecified-in-article primary result of **between-session habituation in speech-related subjective distress (SUDS)**, defined as a ≥5-point reduction in average SUDS from Visit 1 to Visit 2 (DCS 75% vs placebo 36%; χ²[1] = 4.69, *p* = .030).  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to DCS versus placebo.

Only the article provided was available; no trial protocol, statistical analysis plan (SAP), or registry record was available for this assessment.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The article states that participants “were randomly assigned” to DCS or placebo, but does not report how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, block randomization). Under RoB 2, a statement that a study was randomized without a method is insufficient to confirm that the sequence itself contained a random component.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  Allocation was performed by “a data manager who had no interaction with participants.” This indicates that allocation was managed separately from participant-facing study personnel. Participants received the assigned capsule only after recruitment, eligibility assessment, consent, and development of the exposure task. Although the precise allocation-concealment procedure is not described, the reported separation between the data manager and participant contact supports a judgment that allocation was probably concealed from those enrolling participants.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No information.**  
  The report states that groups did not differ on gender, ethnicity, medication use, baseline LSAS, baseline speech SUDS, or baseline Social Phobia Scale scores; age differed (*p* = .019), with a mean age of approximately 49 years in DCS and 38 years in placebo. However, the article does not provide a complete numerical baseline table, nor does it make fully clear whether all baseline comparisons were based on all 34 randomized participants or only the 30 participants who completed Visit 2. One nominally statistically significant difference among several comparisons, particularly in a small trial, is not by itself evidence that the randomization process failed. The available information does not demonstrate a baseline pattern suggesting manipulation or a failure of randomization.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The random-sequence-generation method is insufficiently described. However, allocation was reportedly performed by a data manager with no interaction with participants, which supports probable allocation concealment. The reported baseline information does not demonstrate an imbalance pattern that indicates a problem with randomization. According to the RoB 2 algorithm, no information about sequence generation, together with probable allocation concealment and no demonstrated problematic baseline imbalance, supports **low risk of bias** in this domain. The unreported sequence-generation method remains a reporting limitation.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  DCS and placebo were administered in “identical-appearing capsules.” The adequacy of blinding was tested after Visit 1: participants were asked to guess their allocation, and they were not able to discern assignment (*ps* > .15).

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  Experimenters also could not discern treatment assignment based on Visit 1 (*ps* > .15). The data manager assigning capsules had no interaction with participants. Thus, personnel conducting the exposure procedure were probably blinded.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Not applicable.**  
  Because participants and intervention-delivery personnel were probably unaware of allocation, this question is not required under the RoB 2 algorithm.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The analysis included participants according to their assigned DCS or placebo group. Four participants who did not complete Visit 2 had no post-intervention SUDS data and were excluded. For this RoB 2 domain, an analysis excluding participants with missing outcome data is treated as a modified intention-to-treat analysis and is considered appropriate; the implications of missing data are assessed separately in Domain 3. The study did not report an as-treated analysis, a naïve per-protocol analysis, or exclusion based on adherence to the assigned capsule or exposure intervention.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The study used matching capsules, and both participants and experimenters failed to identify the assigned treatment better than expected from chance. There is therefore no evidence that awareness of allocation produced trial-context deviations from the intended intervention. Participants were analysed in their randomized groups; the exclusion of those without Visit-2 data is addressed as missing outcome data rather than as a deviation-from-intervention problem. Consequently, this domain is at **low risk of bias**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  Thirty-four participants were randomized (18 DCS; 16 placebo). Four participants did not return for Visit 2 and were excluded from all analyses: two in the DCS group and two in the placebo group. Thus, the primary between-session outcome was available for 30/34 participants (88%). For a subjective outcome in a small trial, missing Visit-2 outcome data for 12% of randomized participants cannot be regarded as so small that the missing outcomes could not have affected the estimated group difference.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The report provides no analysis that incorporates the missing outcomes, no sensitivity analysis evaluating plausible missing-data assumptions, and no analysis showing that the primary result is robust to the four missing Visit-2 outcomes.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  The primary outcome is self-reported subjective anxiety during a repeat speech task. It is possible that willingness or ability to return for the second speech could be related to anxiety, avoidance, improvement, or lack of improvement. One participant in each group stated a preference not to continue, and one participant in each group became unreachable. The report does not establish that these circumstances were unrelated to the unobserved Visit-2 SUDS values.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably no.**  
  Missingness was numerically balanced between groups (2/18 DCS and 2/16 placebo). The stated reasons were also similarly distributed: one unreachable participant and one participant preferring not to continue in each group. The report does not provide evidence that missingness was likely related to the unobserved anxiety outcome or that its relationship to the outcome differed by intervention group.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The study excluded four randomized participants because they lacked Visit-2 data. The missingness rate was not negligible for the small sample and subjective primary outcome, and no sensitivity analysis was reported. It remains possible that non-return for a repeat anxiety-provoking public-speaking task was associated with the unobserved anxiety outcome. Nevertheless, missingness and the reported reasons for missingness were balanced across groups, and the article does not establish that missingness was likely outcome-dependent. This leads to **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  The primary outcome was the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS), scored from 0 to 100, measured repeatedly during each speech. The article identifies SUDS as a standard scale in behavioural research and reports high internal consistency for the combined ratings (Cronbach’s α > .85). Nothing in the report indicates that the instrument was unsuitable for measuring subjective distress during a speech exposure.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  Both groups underwent the same laboratory-based speech-exposure procedure at Visit 1 and an identical situation at Visit 2. SUDS was collected at the same specified stages of the speech task for both groups. The report does not identify different measurement methods, thresholds, schedules, or outcome-ascertainment opportunities between DCS and placebo groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  For the SUDS outcome, participants themselves are the outcome assessors because they report their own distress. The participants received identical-appearing capsules and could not discern their allocation after Visit 1 (*ps* > .15). Therefore, participants were probably unaware of whether they had received DCS or placebo.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The outcome was measured using the same reported SUDS procedure in both groups, and the measure was suitable for the intended construct of subjective anxiety during public speaking. Most importantly, the outcome assessors—the participants—were probably blinded to intervention assignment. Therefore, the risk of differential measurement error due to knowledge of DCS versus placebo assignment is **low**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data producing this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The article identifies categorical between-session habituation as the primary outcome and states the threshold used (a ≥5-point reduction in average SUDS across visits). However, no dated protocol, SAP, or trial-registry record is reported or available. Therefore, it cannot be verified that this analysis was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The study measured SUDS repeatedly during each speech, calculated average speech SUDS, defined a categorical SUDS response, and measured a broader Social Phobia Scale outcome. The article does not provide a prospectively dated document specifying the selected SUDS summary, response threshold, or hierarchy of outcome measurements. There is no direct evidence that the reported primary categorical SUDS result was selected because of its findings; however, the available report does not exclude selection among multiple eligible SUDS measurements or summaries.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The paper reports several analytic approaches: a chi-square test for categorical SUDS response, repeated-measures MANCOVA for average SUDS and Social Phobia Scale scores, follow-up ANCOVAs, and additional analyses incorporating medication use, antidepressant use, treatment guesses, and age. No protocol or SAP establishes in advance which analyses, covariates, handling of missing data, or SUDS outcome metrics were intended to be primary. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was chosen on the basis of its magnitude, direction, or statistical significance, but this cannot be assessed definitively.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The article itself describes the categorical SUDS outcome as primary, but this alone does not establish that the analysis was pre-specified before outcome data were available. The absence of a protocol, registration record, or SAP prevents confirmation that the selected measurement and analysis were planned in advance and prevents a full assessment of possible selective reporting. There is no evidence sufficient to judge high risk; however, the lack of verifiable prospective analysis intentions produces **some concerns**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgment  
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation for overall judgment:**  
The trial is at low risk of bias for the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and measurement of the primary subjective-distress outcome. However:

1. **Missing outcome data** raise some concerns because 4/34 randomized participants (12%) did not provide the Visit-2 outcome and were excluded, with no sensitivity analysis assessing the effect of the missing data; and  
2. **Selection of the reported result** raises some concerns because no prospectively dated protocol, SAP, or registry record was available to verify that the primary SUDS measurement and analysis were specified before unblinded data were available.

No individual domain is judged at high risk of bias. The concerns in Domains 3 and 5 do not, on the information reported, substantially lower confidence enough to warrant an overall high-risk judgment. Therefore, applying the specified RoB 2 rule, the overall judgment is **Some concerns**.
