## RoB 2 assessment

**Target result assessed:** progression-free survival (PFS) after random assignment, comparing **maintenance paclitaxel plus gemcitine (PG)** with **observation** among patients with metastatic breast cancer who achieved disease control after six induction cycles of PG. The reported primary result was median PFS 7.5 versus 3.8 months; hazard ratio 0.73 (95% CI 0.55–0.97; *P*=0.026).

**Effect of interest:** effect of **assignment** to maintenance PG versus observation (intention-to-treat effect).

**Sources assessed:** published article, reported tables/figures, and the provided ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT00561119). No statistical analysis plan or registry-posted results were available.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article explicitly states that “the random permutation method was used for random assignment.” This is a random sequence-generation method. Randomization was performed in a 1:1 ratio and stratified by visceral disease, prior adjuvant taxane therapy, response to induction PG, and hormone-receptor status.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  The report states that “registration and random assignment were coordinated centrally at the KCSG data center.” Central coordination supports allocation concealment from staff recruiting participants at the participating centres. The article does not describe the exact central randomization procedure or explicitly state that assignment was released only after irreversible enrolment; therefore, the response is “probably yes,” rather than unequivocally “yes.”

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  The groups had the intended near-equal sizes (116 versus 115). Baseline characteristics were generally similar. One difference was reported for hematogenous lung metastases (31.9% in the maintenance arm versus 16.5% in the observation arm; *P*=0.029). However, numerous baseline variables and metastatic sites were compared, and this isolated nominally significant difference is not, by itself, an excess of imbalance beyond that compatible with chance. There was no extreme imbalance in an identified key prognostic factor, no marked imbalance in group size, and no pattern indicating failure or manipulation of randomization.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The random sequence was explicitly generated using random permutation, randomization was centrally coordinated, and the available baseline table does not show imbalance sufficient to indicate a defective randomization process. The incomplete procedural detail on concealment does not materially undermine the otherwise centrally administered randomization process.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The ClinicalTrials.gov record specifies no masking. Participants assigned to maintenance chemotherapy necessarily knew they were receiving chemotherapy, while those assigned to observation received no treatment.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  This was an open-label comparison of continued intravenous chemotherapy with observation. Treating clinicians and other personnel necessarily knew the allocated strategy.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  The protocol intended maintenance PG to continue until progression, unacceptable toxicity, or withdrawal of consent. Nevertheless, 33 of 116 maintenance-arm participants discontinued study drugs without documented progression “at the physician’s discretion”; the reported median number of post-randomization cycles in these participants was 12. In the observation arm, six participants discontinued at physician discretion. These discontinuations were unbalanced between groups. However, the article does not report why physicians discontinued treatment, whether these decisions reflected trial-context effects, or whether the same treatment changes would have occurred outside the trial. RoB 2 requires evidence or a strong reason to conclude that deviations arose because of the trial context for the assignment-effect assessment. Such evidence is not reported.

- **2.4–2.5 Were deviations likely to affect PFS and were they balanced between groups? — Not assessed further.**  
  Because it is not established that the relevant deviations arose because of the trial context, these questions do not determine the judgment under the assignment-effect pathway.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The article states: “All randomly assigned patients were included in the efficacy analysis according to the intent-to-treat principle.” The PFS analysis included all 231 randomized participants in their assigned groups. No crossover to maintenance PG occurred in the observation group after progression. The use of intention-to-treat analysis is appropriate for the effect of assignment.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The analysis was intention-to-treat and therefore appropriate. However, the trial was unblinded, and substantial discontinuation of maintenance PG at physician discretion occurred in the maintenance arm. The report does not establish whether these deviations resulted from the trial context, so the open-label design combined with incomplete information about the mechanism and implications of those deviations raises some concerns, but does not establish high risk of bias under RoB 2’s assignment-effect framework.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for PFS available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants? — No information.**  
  PFS was defined from randomization to documented progression or last visit, and participants were censored at the last clinical contact if lost to follow-up or at the analysis cutoff date. However, the article does not report the number censored in each group, the numbers with PFS events, the numbers remaining at risk over time, or a complete accounting of PFS outcome availability at the data cutoff. The discontinuation table does not establish whether participants who stopped protocol treatment continued protocol follow-up and contributed PFS data.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No sensitivity analysis for informative censoring or missing PFS data was reported. No method specifically intended to demonstrate robustness of the PFS effect estimate to missing outcome data was presented.

- **3.3 Could missingness in PFS depend on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  In a metastatic-cancer study, withdrawal, treatment discontinuation, deterioration, or loss to follow-up may be related to participants’ disease status or impending progression. The study censored participants at last clinical contact but did not provide information sufficient to exclude informative censoring.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in PFS depended on its true value? — Probably no.**  
  The report does not show differential loss to follow-up, differential censoring rates, or reasons for censoring that clearly demonstrate that missingness depended on true progression status. Although patient withdrawal and other discontinuation categories were reported, these are not equivalent to missing PFS data, because the report does not state whether follow-up continued after treatment discontinuation.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The information provided is insufficient to verify that PFS data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants, and no sensitivity analysis addresses the effect of censoring or missing PFS data. Because progression-related health status could plausibly affect follow-up, some concern is warranted. High risk is not assigned because the report does not demonstrate differential or informative censoring, and it does not provide enough information to establish that missingness was likely related to the true PFS outcome.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring PFS inappropriate? — No.**  
  Disease response and progression were assessed according to RECIST version 1.0, with radiographic studies performed every six weeks. RECIST-based radiographic assessment is an appropriate measurement method for progression in this trial.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of PFS have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  The report specifies radiographic assessment every six weeks and does not report different imaging schedules, different progression thresholds, or different ascertainment procedures between treatment groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Yes.**  
  The trial was open-label, and “PFS and the duration of response were assessed by investigators.” There is no report of blinded independent central radiology review. Therefore, investigator outcome assessors were aware of allocation.

- **4.4 Could assessment of PFS have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.**  
  Although imaging was scheduled and RECIST criteria were used, investigator-assessed progression involves interpretation of radiographic findings and determination/documentation of progression. In an open-label comparison of active chemotherapy with observation, knowledge of allocation could influence these assessments.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of PFS was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  The article reports scheduled radiographic assessments every six weeks and use of RECIST criteria, which limits subjectivity. The article provides no evidence that assessments were actually influenced by allocation, no discrepancy in assessment schedule, and no evidence of differential progression ascertainment. Nonetheless, absence of blinded central review prevents a low-risk judgment.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
PFS was measured with scheduled imaging and RECIST criteria, supporting standardized outcome ascertainment. However, progression was assessed by unblinded investigators, rather than by blinded independent assessors. Since PFS assessment can involve judgment, allocation knowledge could have influenced the result; the report does not provide evidence that it did so. This supports “some concerns,” rather than high risk.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced the PFS result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The trial registry identifies PFS as the primary outcome, and the article identifies PFS after randomization as the primary endpoint. However, the registry does not provide a sufficiently detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan for the reported numerical PFS result. No dated statistical analysis plan was available. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that the reported analysis—including the selected Cox-model specification, covariates, handling of censoring, and presentation of the hazard ratio—was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

- **5.2 Was the PFS result likely selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — Probably no.**  
  PFS was explicitly designated as the primary endpoint in both the article and registry. The article consistently reports PFS from random assignment, with progression assessed by RECIST and radiographic evaluations scheduled every six weeks. There is no evidence that multiple eligible PFS definitions or time points were measured and that only a favorable one was selected for reporting.

- **5.3 Was the PFS result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The article reports Kaplan–Meier/log-rank analyses and states that treatment effects were adjusted using a multivariable Cox model with preselected covariates. However, the detailed pre-randomization and pre-unblinding analysis plan is unavailable, and the report does not provide enough information to determine all analyses conducted or whether the reported numerical PFS result was selected from multiple eligible analyses. There is no affirmative evidence of selective reporting.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
PFS was clearly pre-specified as the primary outcome, which reduces concern about selective choice of outcome domain. However, no detailed, date-stamped statistical analysis plan was available, and the registry record does not document the full analysis strategy. Consequently, selective reporting cannot be excluded, although it is not demonstrated. The appropriate judgment is “some concerns,” not high risk.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgment — **High risk of bias**

The study is at low risk of bias for the randomization process, but it has **some concerns in four domains**:

1. deviations from intended interventions;
2. missing PFS outcome data/informative censoring;
3. measurement of investigator-assessed open-label PFS; and
4. selection of the reported result.

Under RoB 2, an overall judgment of **high risk of bias** is appropriate when a study has some concerns in multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result. Here, the concerns are not isolated: the unblinded comparison, unblinded investigator assessment of PFS, incomplete information on follow-up/censoring, and lack of a sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan together materially reduce confidence in the reported PFS estimate.
