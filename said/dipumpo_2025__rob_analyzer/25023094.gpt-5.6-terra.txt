## RoB 2 assessment

**Result assessed:** effect of **active therapeutic ultrasound plus the usual physiotherapy protocol** versus **sham (inactive) ultrasound plus the same usual physiotherapy protocol** on **pain at discharge** (after 25 sessions; 20 days after ultrasound had ceased), principally the VAS pain improvement result reported in Table 3.  
**Effect of interest:** effect of **assignment to intervention** (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Information sources available for this assessment:** the published article and its tables/flowchart only. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial-registry record was provided.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Probably yes.**  
  The article describes allocation by “**sex-stratified randomization**” and states in the abstract that participants were “assigned by simple random selection.” These statements support the presence of random allocation. However, the report does not state how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, or random permuted blocks).

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned? — No information.**  
  The article states that “**the allocation sequence was blinded through the use of sealed envelopes**.” However, it does not report that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, opened sequentially, or opened only after irreversible participant enrolment. Under RoB 2, merely stating that sealed envelopes were used is insufficient to establish adequate allocation concealment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  The groups had equal sample sizes (27 per group), equal sex distributions (10 men and 17 women per group), and no statistically significant baseline differences in age or the reported pre-treatment pain and mobility measures. Some mobility variables had borderline p-values (extension p = 0.054; left side bending p = 0.050), but these isolated differences do not by themselves demonstrate an imbalance beyond that compatible with chance. The reported baseline information therefore does not suggest a problem with randomization.

**Judgement and motivation**

There are **some concerns** because allocation concealment was not adequately documented. The use of “sealed envelopes” alone does not demonstrate that enrolment personnel could not foresee or manipulate forthcoming allocations. The report supports random allocation and does not show concerning baseline imbalance, but it does not provide enough information to judge concealment as adequate.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  The study is described as involving “**blinding of patients**,” and the control group received sham ultrasound with the ultrasound device switched off. This supports participant blinding.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention? — Yes.**  
  The article explicitly acknowledges that “**the blinding of the physiotherapist who applied the treatment was not possible**.” Thus, the treating physiotherapist was aware of allocation.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The treatment protocol was explicitly described for both groups. The only planned between-group difference during the first 10 sessions was active versus inactive ultrasound; both groups then received the same interventions without ultrasound for 15 sessions. The article reports no crossovers, unplanned co-interventions, differential implementation failures, or other departures from the intended protocol that arose because the therapist knew allocation.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The authors state that “**the analysis was done by intention to treat**.” In addition, the flowchart and methods state that no participant was lost or dropped out. The reported analyses compare the randomized groups.

**Judgement and motivation**

Risk of bias is **low** for the effect of assignment to active versus sham ultrasound. Although the treating physiotherapist was unblinded, the report provides no evidence that this knowledge led to differential deviations from the protocol or differential co-interventions. Participants were reported to be blinded, both groups received the same non-ultrasound components of treatment, no attrition occurred, and the analysis was stated to be intention-to-treat.

A limitation is that the report does not provide a direct assessment of whether participant blinding was successfully maintained. However, the described sham procedure and explicit statement that patients were blinded support a low-risk judgement for this domain.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Yes.**  
  The trial randomized 54 participants, 27 to each group. The article states: “**During the study no patient was lost or dropped out**.” The participant flowchart likewise shows the 27 participants in each randomized group progressing through the intervention and final data collection.

**Judgement and motivation**

Risk of bias is **low** because the report indicates complete follow-up and outcome assessment for all randomized participants. There is no evidence of missing pain data, differential dropout, exclusion owing to missing data, or imputation methods that could introduce bias.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  Pain was measured using a visual analogue scale (VAS). The article also used pressure algometry at specified anatomical points. Nothing in the report indicates that the VAS was unsuitable for measuring pain in this population.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  Pain measurements were obtained at the same scheduled assessment points for both groups: before session 1, before session 6, before session 11, and at completion of session 25. The same instruments and assessment procedures were described for both groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  For the VAS outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor. The article reports patient blinding. It also states that the physiotherapist who assessed outcomes was blinded (“**blind assessor**”), which is relevant to the algometry and mobility outcomes.

**Judgement and motivation**

Risk of bias is **low** for measurement of the VAS pain outcome. The pain outcome was measured using the same method and schedule in both groups, and participants were reported to be blinded to active versus sham ultrasound. Thus, knowledge of assignment was not expected to differentially influence participant-reported VAS pain.

The limitation is that the article does not report a formal test of the success of patient blinding. Nevertheless, it explicitly reports patient blinding and a sham-ultrasound comparator, with no evidence that outcome measurement differed between groups.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial-registration record is available in the supplied material. The article does not state that the analysis plan was finalized before outcome data were unblinded.

- **5.2 Is the assessed VAS result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the pain domain? — No information.**  
  The study assessed pain repeatedly: before treatment, before the sixth session, before the eleventh session, and at discharge. It also assessed pain using both VAS and algometry at four anatomical sites. The main results tables report the between-group improvement results after 10 sessions and at discharge, but do not provide the results for the assessment before session 6. In the absence of a pre-specified plan, it cannot be determined whether the discharge VAS result was selected from multiple measured time points or pain measures based on its result.

- **5.3 Is the assessed result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses? — No information.**  
  The report does not provide a pre-specified analytic hierarchy or statistical analysis plan. It presents improvement indices and uses different statistical tests according to data distribution, but there is insufficient information to determine whether alternative eligible analyses—such as final values versus change scores, alternative handling of non-normal data, or adjusted analyses—were performed and selectively reported.

**Judgement and motivation**

There are **some concerns** about selective reporting because no pre-specified analysis plan is available and the study assessed pain using multiple measures and multiple time points. The report does not establish that the discharge VAS analysis was selected independently of the observed findings.

This judgement is **not high risk** because the article does not provide direct evidence that the reported discharge VAS result was selected on the basis of its statistical significance, direction, or magnitude. The limitation is insufficient information, rather than documented selective reporting.
