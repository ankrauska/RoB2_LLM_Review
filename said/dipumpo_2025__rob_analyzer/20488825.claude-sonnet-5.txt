# ROB2 Assessment Report
## Simvastatin Treatment in Relapsing-Remitting Multiple Sclerosis (Togha et al., 2010)

**Outcome assessed:** Total relapse rate over 1-year follow-up (primary outcome)
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- 1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? **Probably yes.** The article states "A computer-based blocking method (four blocks) was used for the randomization." This indicates a random component in sequence generation.
- 1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed? **No information.** The article states "Separate physicians were assigned to randomly allocate patients into two groups" but provides no detail on the mechanism of concealment (e.g., sealed envelopes, central allocation, pharmacy-controlled dispensing). No information is given on whether the enrolling physicians could foresee upcoming allocations, which is a particular concern given the "four blocks" method was used (fixed block size = higher predictability risk), and no mention is made of whether block size was concealed from the allocating personnel.
- 1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem? **No.** Table 1 shows no statistically significant differences in sex, age, disease duration, prior relapses, prior IFNb duration, baseline EDSS, or baseline Gd+ lesions (all p>0.05). Differences appear compatible with chance.

**Motivation:** Sequence generation appears random (probably yes), but there is no information regarding allocation concealment. Given the fixed block size of four (a relatively small, potentially predictable block size) and no description of a concealment mechanism, this is judged as "no information" for concealment. Per the RoB2 algorithm, when sequence generation is Y/PY but concealment is NI, and baseline imbalance is not suggestive of a problem, the default judgment is "Some concerns." No baseline imbalance was noted, which is reassuring but insufficient alone to rule out concealment issues.

**Limitation:** The trial report does not specify the method of allocation concealment (e.g., use of sealed opaque envelopes, central pharmacy allocation, etc.), constituting an ambiguity that could not be resolved from the text provided.

---

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions:**
- 2.1 Were participants aware of assigned intervention? **No/Probably no.** The study is described as double-blind (also registered as "quadruple" masking including participant, care provider, investigator, and outcomes assessor in the trial registry). Medications were "given in similar packages."
- 2.2 Were carers/those delivering the intervention aware? **No/Probably no.** Same blinding as above; separate physicians handled randomization while treating neurologists were blinded.
- 2.3–2.5 (Not applicable given No to 2.1/2.2)
- 2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used? **Yes.** The article states, "The analyses were performed according to intention-to-treat methods, and patients who withdraw from the study were followed for the whole study period." Per-protocol and LOCF analyses were also presented as sensitivity analyses, but the primary reported ITT analysis is appropriate.
- 2.7 Not applicable (appropriate analysis was used).

**Motivation:** The trial was double-blind (quadruple-masked per registry), medications were identically packaged, and neurologists performing clinical/EDSS assessments were blinded to allocation. No evidence of trial-context-driven deviations (e.g., non-protocol interventions) that could differentially affect outcome ascertainment is reported. An ITT analysis was used as the primary analytic approach. This supports a low risk of bias judgment for the effect of assignment to intervention.

---

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- 3.1 Were data available for all/nearly all randomized participants? **No.** Of 85 randomized (43 simvastatin, 42 placebo), 14 patients withdrew prematurely (2 simvastatin + 4 placebo due to two relapses; 1 placebo due to chronic exacerbation; 2 for personal reasons; 5 non-cooperators in first 2 months). For the primary relapse outcome, the Mann-Whitney analysis used n=40 (simvastatin) and n=36 (placebo), indicating missing data for approximately 9/85 (~11%) participants in the primary analysis. For MRI outcomes, missingness was substantially higher: 11 patients refused MRI entirely (4 placebo, 7 simvastatin) and 8 more refused IV contrast (2 placebo, 6 simvastatin), disproportionately affecting the simvastatin group.
- 3.2 Is there evidence the result was not biased by missing data? **Probably no/No information.** While the authors report "patients who withdraw from the study were followed for the whole study period," and used LOCF for the 3-month interval attack-rate outcome, no formal sensitivity analysis addressing plausible values under missing-not-at-random assumptions was presented for the primary outcome.
- 3.3 Could missingness depend on its true value? **Yes.** Most premature withdrawals occurred due to "two relapses" (i.e., directly linked to the outcome variable itself) or "chronic exacerbation" — a disease-activity–related reason. This creates a mechanistic link between missingness and the true value of the outcome (relapse status).
- 3.4 Is it likely that missingness depended on its true value? **Probably yes, but likely balanced in direction.** Withdrawals for relapse/exacerbation were numerically higher in the placebo group (4 relapse-related placebo withdrawals + 1 exacerbation = 5) versus simvastatin (2 relapse-related withdrawals), suggesting differential missingness related to the outcome, favoring retention of "better outcome" patients disproportionately in the simvastatin arm's dropouts profile, though numbers are small.

**Motivation:** Although the overall dropout proportion (14/85, ~16%) is not extreme, the reasons for missingness are directly tied to the outcome being measured (relapses/exacerbations), and the distribution of these outcome-related dropouts differs numerically between arms (more in placebo). This raises the possibility that missingness depended on its true value in a manner that could affect the effect estimate, though the small number of affected patients (6 relapse/exacerbation-related dropouts total) limits the likely magnitude of bias. This does not reach the threshold of "likely" material bias sufficient for a "High" judgment, but the absence of sensitivity analyses addressing this specific concern precludes a "Low" judgment.

**Limitation:** The article does not report a sensitivity analysis modeling the impact of different assumed outcomes for participants who withdrew due to relapse, which would have permitted a more definitive judgment.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions:**
- 4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? **No.** Relapses were defined using a clear, pre-specified operational definition ("any new or worsening neurological deficit in the absence of high body temperature that lasted more than 24 h after 1-month stability"), a standard and appropriate method for MS relapse ascertainment.
- 4.2 Could measurement/ascertainment differ between groups? **No.** All patients were evaluated by neurologists on an identical schedule (every 3 months) regardless of group assignment; the same diagnostic criteria for relapse applied to both arms.
- 4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of intervention received? **No.** The four neurologists performing follow-up evaluations and relapse assessments are explicitly stated to be "blinded to the patients' assignment to treatment group." MRI was evaluated by radiologists "blinded to the patients' treatment group."
- 4.4/4.5: Not applicable, as assessors were blinded.

**Motivation:** Outcome assessment (relapse determination, EDSS scoring, and MRI reading) was performed by clinicians and radiologists explicitly blinded to treatment allocation, using a standardized and clinically validated relapse definition applied uniformly across groups. This supports a low risk of bias in outcome measurement.

---

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- 5.1 Was the outcome analyzed in accordance with a pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded data were available? **No information.** The trial is registered (NCT00668343), and the registered primary outcome is "attack number" over 1 year, consistent with the primary outcome reported in the article. However, no full pre-specified statistical analysis plan (SAP) was available for review, and the registry entry provides only minimal detail on planned secondary analyses (EDSS, MRI outcomes) or covariate-adjustment strategy.
- 5.2 Was the result likely selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements? **No information/Probably no** for the primary relapse outcome (single, clearly defined outcome). However, for EDSS and MRI outcomes, multiple analytic approaches were reported (unadjusted means, MANOVA-adjusted estimates, per-protocol analysis, LOCF analysis), and the article emphasizes the significant unadjusted EDSS difference (p=0.037) prominently in the abstract before noting it became non-significant after adjustment (p=0.07), raising some concern about presentation emphasis, though both results were transparently reported.
- 5.3 Was the result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses? **Probably no for primary outcome, but concern for secondary outcomes.** Multiple analytic strategies (ITT, per-protocol, LOCF) were all reported for the relapse outcome, with results directionally consistent (all significant, p=0.01, 0.004, 0.03 respectively) — this transparency reduces concern for this specific outcome. However, the absence of an accessible trial protocol/SAP limits verification that no other outcomes, timepoints, or subgroup analyses were measured but not reported.

**Motivation:** The primary outcome (relapse number) matches the registered outcome, and multiple concordant analyses (ITT, per-protocol, LOCF) were transparently reported, supporting adequate reporting for this specific result. However, the lack of an available detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan, combined with the pattern of reporting an initially significant EDSS finding that lost significance after adjustment, and the absence of information about whether other outcome measures/timepoints were collected but not reported, precludes a "Low risk" judgment.

**Limitation:** No statistical analysis plan or detailed protocol document was accessible to confirm that all pre-specified analyses were reported in full and that no additional eligible analyses were selectively omitted.

---

### 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall judgment: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Applying the Cochrane RoB2 algorithm: no domain was judged "High risk" individually; however, three of the five domains (Domain 1 – Randomization process; Domain 3 – Missing outcome data; Domain 5 – Selection of the reported result) were each judged "Some concerns." Per RoB2 guidance (Table 1), "'Some concerns' in multiple domains may lead the review authors to decide on an overall judgement of 'High' risk of bias for that outcome," particularly when these concerns compound to substantially lower confidence in the robustness of the result. Here:
- Uncertainty about allocation concealment (Domain 1) leaves open the possibility of selection bias at enrollment;
- Missing outcome data disproportionately linked to the outcome itself (relapse/exacerbation-driven withdrawals) (Domain 3) creates a plausible pathway for bias in the primary relapse-rate estimate;
- The absence of a fully verifiable pre-specified analysis plan (Domain 5), combined with a discrepant EDSS result (significant unadjusted, non-significant adjusted) prominently reported in the abstract, raises concern about selective emphasis of findings.

The convergence of these three "Some concerns" domains, particularly given that the primary outcome (relapse rate) is directly implicated in both the randomization/concealment uncertainty and the missing-data mechanism, substantially lowers confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported significant relapse-rate benefit (p=0.01). Therefore, the overall judgment for this result is **High risk of bias**.
