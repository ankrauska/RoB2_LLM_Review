## RoB 2 assessment

**Result assessed:** Effect of assignment to **Wellness Recovery Action Planning (WRAP)** versus **wait-list/usual care** on the primary psychiatric-symptom outcome, assessed using the **Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI) Positive Symptom Total** over baseline, post-intervention, and 6-month follow-up. The reported effect was the condition-by-time interaction from mixed-effects random regression: **estimate −1.16; P = .027**.

**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment to intervention** (intention-to-treat effect).

**Design and sources used:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial. The article, participant-flow figure/tables, and the supplied ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT01024569) were assessed.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**
- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.** The article states that randomization was undertaken by Survey Research Laboratory staff using “a **random allocation sequence programmed into CAPI administration software**.”
- **1.2 Was allocation concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned? — Yes.** The same passage states that the procedure “allowed for **complete allocation concealment up to the point of assignment**,” and that both interviewers and participants could not know the assigned condition before assignment.
- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.** The article reports no statistically significant baseline differences between groups. Table 1 shows broadly comparable distributions of age, sex, ethnicity, diagnoses, living situation, site, and baseline service use. There is no marked imbalance in group sizes (251 analysed WRAP participants versus 268 analysed control participants) or in a key prognostic characteristic that is clearly incompatible with chance.

**Motivation:**  
Random allocation and allocation concealment were explicitly described and were implemented by staff and software separate from participant interview procedures. The reported baseline data do not provide evidence that the randomization process was compromised. Therefore, the requirements for low risk of bias in this domain are met.

---

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**
- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention? — Yes.** Participants allocated to WRAP attended an 8-week peer-led course, whereas control participants were on a waiting list and continued usual services. Blinding of participants was not feasible.
- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of assigned intervention? — Yes.** WRAP facilitators necessarily knew whom they were delivering the intervention to.
- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.** The intervention was delivered with high reported curricular fidelity (mean 91.3%). Although only 233/276 participants allocated to WRAP received the intervention and attendance averaged 5.05 of 8 sessions, these are deviations in receipt/adherence rather than evidence that the trial context caused non-protocol intervention changes that undermined the assigned-group comparison. Control participants continued usual services as intended.
- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.** The principal analysis was a mixed-effects random regression analysis using participants’ randomized study condition and longitudinal observations. The report explicitly describes this as an intention-to-treat design and states that no participants were excluded for reasons other than loss to follow-up.

**Motivation:**  
The trial was necessarily open-label for participants and intervention providers. However, under RoB 2, awareness alone does not establish bias in the effect of assignment. The report does not identify trial-context deviations from intended intervention that were both outcome-relevant and imbalanced between groups. Attendance/nonreceipt of WRAP does not itself bias an assignment-effect estimate when participants remain analysed in their randomized groups. The mixed-effects analysis retained the assigned intervention groups and was appropriate for the intention-to-treat effect, subject to the separate assessment of missing outcome data.

A relevant limitation is that control participants attended peer-led support groups during follow-up (41.9% between baseline and post-intervention, and 44.9% between post-intervention and 6-month follow-up). However, the report identifies these as services available to controls rather than a trial-induced deviation from the intended wait-list/usual-care strategy. The authors reran analyses adjusting for peer-support exposure and reported no substantial change. This does not support a bias judgement in this domain.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**
- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.** Of 555 randomized participants, only 519 were included in the analysed baseline sample. For the BSI Positive Symptom Total, outcome data were available for 224 WRAP and 234 control participants at post-intervention, and 220 WRAP and 228 control participants at 6 months. Thus, 56/276 (20.3%) WRAP-assigned and 51/279 (18.3%) control-assigned participants did not provide the 6-month BSI outcome, relative to the randomized sample.
- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.** The article states that mixed-effects random regression was selected in part because it can address missing observations. However, no sensitivity analysis was reported to show that the estimated intervention effect was robust under plausible assumptions about the unobserved BSI outcomes. Nor does the report establish that the modelling assumptions adequately removed potential bias from missingness.
- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.** It could. Reported reasons for loss to follow-up included “death or ill health,” moving away, and formal withdrawal. In a trial of psychiatric symptom severity, ill health and withdrawal can plausibly be associated with unobserved psychiatric symptom burden.
- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably yes.** The outcome is self-reported psychiatric symptoms in people with severe and persistent mental illness. The reported reasons for missingness include death or ill health, which are outcome-relevant circumstances. Missingness was also greater in the WRAP group before analysis: 25/276 versus 11/279 participants were reported as lost to follow-up. Although completion percentages among the analysed baseline cohort were described as not statistically different, the report does not provide outcome-specific reasons for missing BSI data by group or analyses demonstrating that missingness was unrelated to psychiatric symptoms.

**Motivation:**  
Substantial outcome data were unavailable by follow-up, particularly when assessed against the full randomized population. The mixed-effects model does not, by itself, demonstrate that missing outcome data could not bias the result; it relies on assumptions about the missing-data process. The study did not provide sensitivity analyses addressing missing-not-at-random mechanisms or evidence that missingness was unrelated to the unobserved BSI outcome. Given the clinical context and reported reasons for attrition, missingness is likely related to participants’ underlying health status and potentially their psychiatric symptoms. This meets the RoB 2 criteria for **high risk of bias** due to missing outcome data.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**
- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.** The BSI is a specified self-report instrument used to measure psychiatric symptoms. The report describes it as a research instrument with high concordance with clinician assessment. No evidence was reported that the instrument itself was invalid or incapable of detecting plausible effects.
- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.** Both groups completed structured interviews at the same scheduled time points using the same assessment approach.
- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Yes.** For this participant-reported outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor under RoB 2. Participants knew whether they had attended WRAP or were on the wait list. The blinding of telephone interviewers does not blind the participant-outcome assessor.
- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.** BSI symptom severity is based on participants’ reports of how much they were bothered by symptoms during the previous week. Such reporting can be influenced by expectations, perceived benefit, engagement with facilitators, or disappointment/expectation associated with wait-list assignment.
- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.** WRAP is a highly interactive peer-led intervention explicitly intended to promote recovery, wellness, self-management, competence, and hope. The comparator was a wait-list condition, not an attention-matched or placebo intervention. The authors themselves identify the lack of an attention-control placebo and reliance on participant self-report uncorroborated by clinicians or objective observers as limitations. These circumstances make influence of knowledge of assignment on self-reported psychiatric symptoms likely.

**Motivation:**  
The BSI Positive Symptom Total is a subjective participant-reported outcome. Participants were unblinded by the nature of the intervention and control conditions. Although interviewers were usually blinded—participants revealed their condition in only 4% of follow-up interviews—this does not resolve the issue because the participants themselves supplied the symptom ratings. In an unblinded comparison of an active, peer-supported recovery intervention with a wait-list control, participant expectations and reporting are likely to differ by assigned group. Under RoB 2, this constitutes **high risk of bias in measurement of the outcome**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**
- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.** The article states that “all outcomes, hypotheses, and statistical analyses presented here were prespecified” in the grant proposal. However, the proposal and a dated statistical analysis plan were not supplied. The ClinicalTrials.gov record was first posted in December 2009, after the study had started (December 2007) and after the actual primary completion date (September 2009). It therefore cannot verify that the reported analysis plan was finalized before unblinded outcome data became available.
- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the psychiatric-symptom domain? — No information.** The BSI could yield multiple eligible measures, including the Global Severity Index and Positive Symptom Total; the article reports both. It also reports the outcome at multiple time points. The registry specifies the BSI Positive Symptom Score as a primary outcome, but it does not give sufficient detail on the exact analysis, handling of repeated measurements, or whether all planned BSI outcomes and time-point contrasts were to be reported. No dated protocol or SAP permits confirmation that the reported result was not selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements.
- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.** The article reports mixed-effects random regression models and supplementary reruns controlling for exposure to peer-led support groups. The available sources do not include a sufficiently detailed pre-unblinding analysis plan specifying the model, covariates, outcome metric, missing-data handling, or whether the BSI Global Severity Index versus Positive Symptom Total was the primary analysis. Thus, the possibility of selecting among eligible analyses cannot be excluded.

**Motivation:**  
There is no direct evidence that the reported BSI Positive Symptom Total result was selected because it was favourable. Therefore, a high-risk judgement is not supported. Nevertheless, the pre-specified analysis intentions cannot be verified from a protocol or statistical analysis plan finalized before access to unblinded data. The trial registry was retrospectively posted and is insufficiently detailed to resolve the selection issue. Accordingly, this domain raises **some concerns**.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**  
The result is judged at **high risk of bias** in at least two domains:

1. **Missing outcome data:** substantial follow-up data were unavailable, with attrition related to circumstances such as death or ill health; no sensitivity analyses establish robustness to plausible missing-data assumptions.
2. **Measurement of the outcome:** psychiatric symptom severity was participant-reported in an unblinded WRAP-versus-wait-list comparison. Since participants themselves were the outcome assessors, blinded interviewers do not prevent bias from knowledge of assigned intervention; influence on symptom reporting is likely.

The trial is at low risk of bias from randomization and deviations from intended interventions, while selection of the reported result raises some concerns because a prospectively finalized detailed analysis plan was not available. In accordance with RoB 2 overall-judgement rules, the presence of high risk of bias in one or more domains results in an **overall high risk of bias** judgement for the assessed psychiatric-symptom result.
