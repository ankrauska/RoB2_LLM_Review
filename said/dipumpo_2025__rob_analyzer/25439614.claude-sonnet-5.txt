# Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) Assessment

**Study:** Diabetic Retinopathy Clinical Research Network — "Intravitreal Ranibizumab for Diabetic Macular Edema with Prompt vs. Deferred Laser Treatment: 5-year Randomized Trial Results"

**Result assessed:** Mean change in best-corrected visual acuity letter score from baseline to the 5-year visit, ranibizumab + prompt laser vs. ranibizumab + deferred laser (intention-to-treat/longitudinal model estimate).

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), based on the longitudinal model using all available visit data.

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

The article states that participants were "randomly assigned" and refers to the original 2010 publication for full methodological detail on sequence generation and allocation concealment. However, the present paper (the 5-year report under evaluation) does not itself describe the method of random sequence generation, nor the allocation concealment mechanism (e.g., use of a central web-based randomization system, permuted blocks, stratification factors). No baseline comparison table for the originally randomized cohorts (180 vs. 181 eyes) is presented in this article; the only baseline table provided (Table 1) compares 5-year completers vs. non-completers within each arm, not the two randomized groups at entry. This precludes an assessment of baseline imbalance suggestive of randomization problems from the information contained in this report.

Because this is recognized as a large, well-established multicenter network (DRCR.net) trial, a degree of "probably yes" judgement regarding random sequence generation and concealment could be inferred from the reputation and design rigor typical of this network; however, in strict adherence to the information reported in this specific document, insufficient details are given. This leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk," since no information is available in this paper to confirm adequate allocation concealment or absence of baseline imbalance in the originally randomized groups.

**Limitation to report:** The article under review does not itself present the randomization methods or baseline balance table for the originally randomized ranibizumab + prompt vs. deferred laser groups; this information was referenced only via citation to an earlier publication and could not be independently verified from the text provided.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgment: Some concerns**

- **Blinding of participants and treating personnel:** The intervention (prompt vs. deferred laser) is not blindable to treating clinicians, since the decision of whether/when to apply laser is inherently known to the treating physician and likely to the participant as well (participants assigned to "deferred" laser may have been told laser was withheld). The article explicitly states masking applied only to "visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians," not to participants or treating physicians. Thus, participants and personnel administering treatment were aware of group assignment.

- **Deviations arising from trial context:** Because unmasked treating physicians decided on retreatment (injections/laser) according to protocol-specified criteria based on VA and OCT, there is potential that clinical decision-making (equipoise, expectation of efficacy) influenced the pattern and number of retreatments differently between groups — indeed, the deferred group received a higher median number of injections (17 vs. 13) and more visits. The authors themselves discuss this as instrumental to interpreting the observed vision benefit in the deferred group ("observed difference in visual acuity...may be related to a greater number of ranibizumab injections..."). This is a per-protocol-permitted difference, not necessarily a deviation inconsistent with protocol, but the lack of blinding of caregivers introduces the possibility of differential clinical management influenced by knowledge of allocation, beyond what is specified.

- **Analysis method:** The analysis used is a longitudinal model including "all available visit data" for all randomized eyes (adjusting for baseline VA), consistent with an ITT-type approach rather than a naïve per-protocol or as-treated analysis. This supports low risk of bias from the analytic method itself.

Given lack of blinding of personnel/participants, plausible differential treatment intensity between groups (which is protocol-driven rather than a trial-context artifact, but not verifiable as fully unbiased), and an appropriate ITT-oriented statistical analysis, the domain is judged as "Some concerns."

**Limitation to report:** The report does not explicitly discuss whether deviations from protocol-specified retreatment criteria occurred or whether such deviations were balanced between groups; this ambiguity limits certainty regarding this domain.

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

- Excluding deaths, only 76% of prompt-laser and 74% of deferred-laser originally randomized eyes completed the 5-year visit — meaning roughly a quarter of the ITT population lack primary outcome data at 5 years. This is a substantial proportion, well above the "small enough to make no important difference" threshold, and differs between arms.
- The paper explicitly reports "some imbalances between completers versus non-completers" in prognostic factors (age, prior PRP, lens status, DR severity), which is direct evidence per Table 1 that missingness may plausibly be related to characteristics associated with outcome — i.e., missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome or on prognostic factors correlated with outcome.
- Although the authors used a longitudinal mixed model incorporating all available visit data (a method intended to reduce, but not necessarily eliminate, bias from missing data under a missing-at-random assumption) and performed adjusted analyses for identified imbalances, the authors themselves acknowledge in the Discussion: "it is not possible to know whether incomplete data were related to unobserved factors that could have affected the results in either direction," an explicit acknowledgment that the risk of bias due to missingness could not be excluded.
- Sensitivity/attrition-adjusted analysis restricted to 5-year completers (Figure 2) is reported to show "similar" results, providing some reassurance, but this is a complete-case analysis itself and does not fully resolve the concern, since figures were not accompanied by formal statistical comparison in the text provided.
- The reason for missing data was mainly discretionary non-consent to extension follow-up rather than dropout for outcome-related reasons (e.g., adverse events), which somewhat mitigates concern, but the documented baseline imbalances between completers and non-completers, combined with a ~25% loss to follow-up, are suf­ficient to raise high risk of bias under RoB 2 criteria (large proportion of missing data + evidence that missingness could relate to prognostic factors linked to the outcome, without definitive statistical evidence ruling out impact).

**Limitation to report:** The exact reasons for the ~24-26% loss to 5-year follow up (beyond "chose not to consent to extension") are not detailed by group, and no formal sensitivity analysis for missing-not-at-random scenarios (e.g., worst-case/best-case imputation) was reported, limiting the ability to rule out bias from missing outcome data.

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

- The primary outcome, best-corrected visual acuity, was assessed by certified visual acuity examiners who were reported as masked to treatment assignment ("masking of the visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians" is explicitly listed as a strength of the study).
- Visual acuity assessment via standardized electronic ETDRS (E-ETDRS) protocol is a well-validated, appropriate, objective measurement method with minimal subjective judgment, and was applied identically in both arms.
- Because outcome assessors (VA examiners, OCT technicians) were blinded to intervention assignment, and the outcome measurement method did not differ between groups, and the measurement approach itself is not vulnerable to the type of subjective interpretation that could be swayed by knowledge of assignment even if unblinding occurred incidentally, this domain is judged as low risk.

**Limitation to report:** The report does not explicitly describe the methods used to preserve masking of outcome assessors (e.g., separation of examiners from treating physicians), nor does it formally test/verify successful blinding, but no indications of unmasking are reported.

---

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

- The trial's outcome measures (mean VA change, ≥10/≥15 letter improvement or worsening, OCT CSF thickness) appear consistent with those reported in earlier publications of this same trial (1-year, 3-year reports), suggesting a pre-specified consistent outcome/analysis framework across the trial's lifespan, which is reassuring.
- However, this article does not reference or provide access to a trial registry entry, protocol, or statistical analysis plan explicitly confirming that the 5-year longitudinal model (with specific adjustment covariates), and the truncation of VA changes at ±30 letters, were pre-specified prior to unblinded data availability. The truncation approach ("visual acuity changes were truncated to ±30 letters to minimize the effects of outliers") is a data-handling decision not clearly justified as prespecified, and could in principle influence the magnitude of the estimated between-group difference, though the direction/impact is not obviously favoring either group.
- Multiple statistical models are presented (unadjusted for baseline VA only, and adjusted for multiple additional confounders), and both are reported with concordant results (P=0.09 vs P=0.15), which reduces (but does not eliminate) concern about selective reporting, since discordant results were not selectively suppressed.
- No explicit statement confirms whether a full analysis plan was finalized before unblinded data were available for this 5-year extension analysis specifically (as opposed to the original 3-year study).

Given the absence of explicit protocol/SAP verification within the document, but with reassuring consistency of outcome reporting across time points and multiple concordant sensitivity analyses, the judgment is "Some concerns" rather than "Low" or "High."

**Limitation to report:** No trial registry, protocol, or statistical analysis plan was provided or cited with sufficient detail in this article to confirm that the specific 5-year longitudinal analysis approach (including outlier truncation rules and covariate adjustment sets) was pre-specified before outcome data were unblinded.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall judgment: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Per RoB 2 rules, an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" is warranted because the study is judged to be at **high risk of bias in the domain of missing outcome data** (Domain 3) — approximately a quarter of originally randomized participants lack 5-year outcome data, with documented baseline imbalances between completers and non-completers that were not fully resolved by adjusted analyses, and the authors themselves acknowledge inability to exclude bias from unobserved confounding related to missingness. This alone is sufficient to classify the result as "High risk of bias" per Table 1 of the RoB 2 framework ("High risk of bias in at least one domain").

This overall high-risk judgement is further reinforced by "Some concerns" identified in multiple other domains (randomization process, deviations from intended intervention, and selection of the reported result), which cumulatively lower confidence in the reliability of the reported 5-year treatment effect estimate, even though none of these other domains individually reached "high risk."

**Summary implication:** While the 5-year findings (numerically favoring deferred over prompt laser for visual acuity gain) are broadly consistent with earlier (1- and 3-year) results from this trial and are biologically/clinically plausible, the substantial loss to follow-up combined with documented baseline differences between completers and non-completers, and incomplete transparency regarding pre-specification of analysis methods, warrant caution in accepting the magnitude and statistical robustness of the reported between-group difference at face value.
