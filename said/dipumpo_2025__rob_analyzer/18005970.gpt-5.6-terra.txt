## RoB 2 assessment

**Assessment target.** This assessment concerns the **effect of assignment at Phase 2 in the Trento Faculty**, comparing **intensive clinical tutoring** (n=41) with **routine tutoring** (n=41), on the main outcome: **students making fewer than two wrong diagnostic hypotheses** after four weeks of clinical learning. The effect of interest is the **intention-to-treat effect** (effect of assignment to tutorial strategy), because the groups were randomized and outcomes are reported by assigned group.

The article also presents an “inter-trial” comparison between Trento and Udine (e.g., OR 3.75, 95% CI 1.77–7.88). That comparison is **not a randomized comparison of tutorial strategies**, because students were not randomized between faculties/campuses. It should not be assessed as an RCT result using RoB 2; it would require assessment as a non-randomized comparison (e.g., ROBINS-I).

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?** **Probably yes.**  
  The report states that, within each campus, students were “randomised with a randomisation table” into intervention and control groups. This supports the presence of a random allocation process, although the method used to generate the table is not described.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?** **No information.**  
  The article does not report allocation concealment. There is no description of central randomization, independent allocation, sealed opaque sequentially numbered envelopes, or another method preventing tutors, investigators, or students from foreseeing the next allocation.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?** **Probably no.**  
  The article reports that there were no significant differences between trial and control groups within each campus for the characteristics summarized in Table 2. However, detailed baseline data separately for the Trento intensive-tutoring and routine-tutoring groups are not fully presented. Nothing reported indicates an imbalance sufficiently extreme to suggest failure of randomization.

**Motivation for judgment.**  
The stated use of a “randomisation table” supports random allocation, and there is no reported evidence of serious baseline imbalance. However, allocation concealment is entirely unreported. Under RoB 2, absence of information about concealment of the allocation sequence leads to **some concerns**, unless adequate concealment can otherwise be established. It cannot be established from this article.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?** **Yes.**  
  Students necessarily knew whether they were receiving intensive daily tutoring or routine tutoring during their clinical placement.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial?** **Yes.**  
  The tutor strategies required tutors and clinical staff to know and deliver the assigned educational approach.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?** **Probably no.**  
  The study describes the assigned tutorial approaches, their duration, tutor roles, and intended content. It also states that “during the study, none of the teaching practices changed in either of the degree courses.” The lack of standardization of routine tutoring is explicitly part of the defined routine-tutoring strategy, rather than a reported post-randomization deviation from it.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?** **Probably yes.**  
  The Phase 2 results include all 41 students allocated to intensive tutoring and all 41 allocated to routine tutoring in Trento. The reported comparisons and regression analyses use the allocated groups. There is no report that students were analysed according to a different strategy than that to which they had been randomized, or that eligible randomized students were excluded from this Phase 2 analysis.

**Motivation for judgment.**  
This was necessarily an unblinded educational intervention, but lack of blinding alone does not establish bias due to deviations from intended interventions for the intention-to-treat effect. The article does not report deviations arising because of the trial context that were likely to affect diagnostic-reasoning outcomes and were unbalanced between groups. The routine strategy’s less structured nature was part of the comparator as defined by the investigators. The reported group totals indicate analysis according to randomized allocation. Therefore, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

**Important limitation.**  
The report does not provide detailed fidelity data—for example, the number of daily debriefings actually delivered or attendance at each session. This limits certainty regarding implementation, but does not itself document a deviation from the allocated intervention or an inappropriate analysis.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?** **Probably no.**  
  The main outcome was the number of mistaken diagnostic hypotheses generated in response to a simulated nursing case. The article describes a common standardized case scenario, a professional panel that defined the standard problems, and independent evaluation of student responses against that standard. No evidence is presented that the measurement method was incapable of identifying differences in diagnostic errors.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?** **Probably no.**  
  The same Phase 2 nursing case and the same outcome-assessment approach were used for both Trento groups. The allotted time was also the same: 20 minutes to formulate a diagnostic hypothesis, identify relevant data, and identify additional data required.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?** **No.**  
  The article states that “two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students.” It further states that data were processed blind and that the identities of the schools were revealed only afterwards. This supports blinded outcome assessment.

**Motivation for judgment.**  
The outcome was measured using the same simulated-case process in both randomized groups, and the student responses were evaluated by blinded researchers against a predefined professional standard. Since outcome assessors were blinded and outcome ascertainment was comparable between groups, the risk of differential outcome measurement bias is **low**.

**Important limitation.**  
The article acknowledges that it did not investigate the types or seriousness of students’ diagnostic errors. This limits interpretation of the educational or clinical importance of the error count, but it does not demonstrate differential measurement error between randomized groups.
