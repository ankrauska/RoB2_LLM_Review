# ROB 2 Assessment Report

**Preliminary Note:** This study is described as a "double pragmatic randomised trial," but on close reading of the Methods, the actual result being assessed (the **inter-trial analysis**: comparing Trento intensive tutoring/laboratory group vs. Udine groups, OR 3.75) is **not** a randomized comparison — it is a comparison between two different, non-randomized campuses/faculties with different baseline educational traditions. Only the **intra-faculty** comparisons (intervention vs. control group within Trento; intervention vs. control within Udine) were generated by actual randomization ("randomised...into two different groups"). Given that the headline result reported in the abstract (OR 3.75; 95% CI 1.77–7.88) is the **inter-trial (non-randomized, between-campus) comparison**, this assessment focuses primarily on that result, as it is the result emphasized as the main finding, while noting that the intra-trial randomized comparisons showed **no significant differences** (Trento: OR 1.84, 95% CI 0.62–5.53; Udine: OR 2.98, 95% CI 0.86–10.74).

Because RoB 2 is designed for randomized comparisons, I address both scenarios below but the overall judgement pertains to the **inter-trial result**, since this is the result on which the paper's main conclusions and title rest.

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **HIGH RISK**

- **Signalling Q1.1 (Was the allocation sequence random?):** For the *intra-faculty* comparisons, students were allocated "with a randomisation table" — Yes/Probably Yes for those specific comparisons.
- However, the **primary comparative result reported (inter-trial, OR 3.75)** compares students from **two entirely different faculties (Trento vs. Udine)**, which were **not randomized at all** — allocation to "Trento" or "Udine" was determined by which university the student had already enrolled in, not by randomization.
- **Signalling Q1.3 (Baseline imbalance):** Table 2 shows statistically significant differences between the Trento and Udine cohorts in previous work experience duration (p<0.05), voluntary work experience (p<0.01), and clinical training arrangements (p<0.01). These are substantial, non-chance imbalances indicative of fundamentally different populations and contexts, not attributable to randomization at all.
- **Judgement:** Because the compared groups (Trento vs. Udine) were never randomized to their tutorial strategy — students were simply enrolled in different pre-existing faculties with pre-existing different educational traditions, curricula history, and tutor cadres — this domain is at **High risk of bias**. This is a fundamental design flaw amounting to a confounded, non-randomized between-subjects comparison being interpreted as if it arose from randomization.

*Limitation/ambiguity to report:* The article labels the overall design as a randomized/pragmatic trial, but randomization only occurred within each faculty, not between faculties. The paper's own major conclusion is drawn from the between-faculty (non-randomized) comparison, creating ambiguity as to which "trial" the RoB2 tool should strictly apply.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **HIGH RISK**

- **Awareness of assignment (2.1, 2.2):** Neither students nor tutors could be blinded to which tutorial strategy they were receiving (laboratory/intensive tutoring vs. weekly/routine tutoring) — this is inherent to an educational intervention and expected/unavoidable, but must still be assessed.
- **Deviations arising from trial context (2.3–2.5):** The study explicitly reports that, during the trial, the Trento faculty experienced budget "cut-backs," which **reduced the number of tutors and reduced student exposure to intensive tutorials, replacing them partially with routine tutoring** ("Student exposure to intensive tutorials was reduced and routine tutorials by ward staff...were introduced"). This is a substantial, unplanned deviation from the intended intervention that occurred **during the trial itself**, was **directly related to trial context/logistics**, and **likely affected the outcome** (accuracy of diagnostic reasoning) — and it was **not balanced between groups** (it specifically affected the Trento arms).
- **Appropriate analysis (2.6, 2.7):** There is no evidence of an adjusted/instrumental-variable or per-protocol-appropriate analysis accounting for this deviation; a straightforward comparison of outcomes was performed despite the documented protocol departure.
- **Judgement:** The combination of unblindable, non-concealed group assignment plus a documented, outcome-relevant, unbalanced deviation from intended intervention (the tutoring cutbacks in Trento) that was not appropriately statistically handled places this domain at **High risk of bias**.

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **SOME CONCERNS**

- Of 179 enrolled students, 144 (80.4%) were deemed eligible and included; the flow diagram (Figure 1) tracks numbers through phases 0, 1, and 2, and final group sizes are reported (e.g., Trento intensive tutoring n=41, routine tutoring n=41; Udine weekly tutorials n=39, routine tutoring n=23).
- The 35 excluded students were excluded primarily for pre-specified eligibility criteria (language ability, prior work experience, attendance) rather than post-randomization attrition — this is not "missing outcome data" bias in the strict RoB2 sense, since exclusion occurred before randomization.
- However, there is **no explicit reporting of how many students completed all three phases (0, 1, 2)** or whether the denominators used in Table 3/Table 4 (e.g., n=41 in each Trento subgroup) represent 100% of those originally randomized to that subgroup, or whether some students were lost between Phase 1 and Phase 2 (e.g., due to absence during the case exercises). The paper does not present a clear accounting of missing data for each of the three testing time-points.
- No sensitivity analyses for missing data are reported.
- **Judgement:** Given the reasonably complete apparent accounting of participants through the flow diagram, but with insufficient explicit detail on outcome-data completeness at each of the three testing phases and no discussion of missing data handling, this domain is judged as **Some concerns** (not high, since there is no specific evidence that missingness was substantial or related to outcome).

---

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **SOME CONCERNS**

- **Appropriateness of method (4.1):** The outcome (number of "wrong" diagnostic hypotheses on a standardized simulated case) was defined against a panel-derived standard (15 nurses, Cohen's K>0.80), which is a reasonably appropriate and pre-defined method.
- **Differential measurement across groups (4.2):** The same case/instrument was used for all students in all phases, so measurement method itself did not differ between groups.
- **Blinding of outcome assessors (4.3):** The paper states "Two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students," and "data were processed blind; only after this had been done were the identities of the Schools...revealed" — this indicates the outcome assessors were blinded to school/group identity at the point of scoring, which is a strength.
- **Could assessment be influenced by knowledge of intervention (4.4/4.5):** Because the essay-style hypothesis lists were scored against a standard by blinded raters, direct assessor bias appears well controlled. However:
  - The outcome itself is generated by the **student** (self-generated hypothesis lists), and the **students themselves were fully aware of which tutorial strategy they received** — this raises the possibility that motivation, effort, or response behavior in completing the case exercise could be influenced by intervention received (e.g., students confident from intensive daily tutoring may approach the case differently than those with routine tutoring). This is a subjective, self-generated outcome not amenable to full blinding at the level of data generation (only at the level of scoring).
- **Judgement:** Given blinded scoring but an inherently subjective, self-generated outcome where the "assessor" (student generating the hypotheses) was necessarily aware of intervention assignment, and no clear evidence that this awareness did not influence performance, this domain is judged as **Some concerns**.

---

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **SOME CONCERNS**

- There is no mention of a published pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial registration; no protocol is referenced or cited as being finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.
- Multiple outcome constructs and analyses are reported: average number of hypotheses, average number of errors, dichotomized "wrong hypotheses" above/below median cut-off (with the median cut-off itself derived post hoc, "the median values of error in Phases 1 and 2 were regarded as reference 'cut-off' points" — this is explicitly a **post hoc data-derived threshold**, which raises the possibility that the specific dichotomization was chosen based on the observed data distribution rather than pre-specified).
- Multiple statistical comparisons are reported (intra-trial and inter-trial, univariate and multivariate, several secondary outcomes on perception/confidence), increasing the possibility of selective emphasis on statistically significant findings (e.g., the abstract highlights the significant inter-trial OR 3.75 while downplaying the non-significant intra-trial randomized comparisons).
- No trial registry or protocol was identified/available for comparison in the article to verify whether the specific reported results correspond to pre-specified analyses.
- **Judgement:** Absence of a pre-specified, time-stamped analysis plan, combined with a post-hoc derived cut-off for the primary dichotomous outcome and multiple candidate analyses/outcomes reported, without clear indication that selection was avoided, leads to a judgement of **Some concerns**.

*Limitation to report:* No protocol or SAP was available for verification; judgement is based solely on the published report.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement — **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Rationale:** Per the RoB 2 decision rules, an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" is warranted if the study is at high risk of bias in at least one domain, or has "some concerns" in multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result. In this study:

- Domain 1 (Randomization process) is **High risk**, because the primary/headline comparative result (inter-trial, OR 3.75, 95% CI 1.77–7.88) is derived from a **non-randomized comparison between two different university faculties** with documented significant baseline imbalances.
- Domain 2 (Deviations from intended intervention) is also **High risk**, due to a documented, unplanned, unbalanced mid-study reduction in intervention intensity (tutor cutbacks in Trento) that likely affected the outcome, without appropriate statistical correction.
- Domains 3, 4, and 5 each carry **Some concerns**, further compounding uncertainty.

Given both the High-risk judgements in two independent domains and the multiple "Some concerns" in the remaining domains, the overall result must be judged **High risk of bias**. This means the reported headline effect (that intensive tutoring/laboratory strategies reduce diagnostic reasoning errors compared to routine/weekly tutoring) cannot be considered a reliable causal estimate attributable to randomization; it is much more plausibly confounded by pre-existing, non-randomized differences between the two nursing faculties, their student populations, and an unplanned mid-study change in intervention delivery.

**Stated ambiguity/limitation for transparency:** The authors themselves acknowledge that "generalisation of the results is more limited than predicted for a pragmatic experimental study" and prioritize intra-faculty (properly randomized) comparisons — which showed **no statistically significant differences** between tutorial strategies within either faculty. This internal inconsistency between the properly randomized (null) results and the non-randomized (positive) inter-faculty result reported prominently in the abstract is a critical source of ambiguity that materially affects interpretation of this study's risk of bias and should be considered a major limitation of the evidence presented.
