# ROB 2 Assessment Report: "Can We Modify Maladaptive Attributions for Fatigue?" (Harris & Carney)

**Preliminary Notes:** This is an individually-randomized parallel-group trial (2×2 design). The outcome assessed here is the primary outcome (NumberAttributionScore/attribution change on the LAT), analyzed as effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), comparing Fatigue Information (FI) vs. Control information conditions. The result assessed is the group × time interaction (F(1,86)=5.17, p=.026), and similarly for FrequencyScore and ImportanceScore.

---

## 1. Bias Arising from the Randomization Process — **Some Concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- Was the allocation sequence random? **Yes** — "a Microsoft Excel random number generator was used to randomize participants into the two conditions."
- Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled? **No information** — no description of concealment procedures (e.g., who generated the sequence, whether it was administered independently of the person enrolling/testing participants).
- Did baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization? **No** — Groups did not differ significantly on age, ethnicity, sex distribution, or baseline ISI, MFI, BDI-II, DBAS-16, or LAT scores.

**Motivation:** Although true randomization was used and baseline balance was demonstrated, the complete absence of information regarding allocation concealment prevents a "Low risk" judgment. There is no description of whether the same investigator who generated the sequence also enrolled/tested participants, raising the possibility (though unconfirmed) that assignment could have been anticipated or influenced. Per the RoB2 algorithm, "no information" on concealment combined with no baseline imbalance issues yields "Some concerns."

**Limitation noted:** The paper does not report any methodology for concealing allocation from the study investigator prior to enrollment.

---

## 2. Bias Due to Deviations from Intended Interventions — **Some Concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? **Yes** — by design, participants read/received information sheets specific to their condition; blinding of participants to condition content was not possible given the nature of the intervention (educational information).
- Were carers/those delivering the intervention aware of assignment? **Yes** — "the study investigator read the information sheet alongside each participant individually," meaning the investigator delivering the intervention was necessarily unblinded.
- Did deviations from intended intervention arise because of trial context? **No information** — no evidence of contamination, co-intervention, or trial-context-driven deviations is reported; the intervention was a single, one-time information session delivered as intended.
- Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? **Yes** — All 88 included participants (after data-quality exclusions unrelated to intervention) were analyzed in their randomized groups; no post-randomization exclusions based on adherence occurred.

**Motivation:** Lack of blinding of participants and the investigator delivering the intervention is unavoidable and expected in this type of single-session psychoeducational study; this is inherent to the design rather than indicative of a flaw in trial context. There is no evidence that deviations from intended intervention (e.g., cross-contamination between conditions, failure to deliver the assigned information) occurred. However, because participants and the deliverer of the intervention were both aware of group assignment, and no explicit description confirms that this awareness did not influence engagement with the outcome measure (LAT), a judgment of "Some concerns" is warranted rather than "Low."

**Limitation noted:** The paper does not explicitly address whether investigator awareness of group assignment could have influenced how the LAT was administered or explained to participants.

---

## 3. Bias Due to Missing Outcome Data — **Low Risk**

**Signalling questions:**
- Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? **Yes** — Of 93 initially enrolled, 5 were excluded for reasons unrelated to the intervention or outcome (2 underage, 1 incomplete demographics, 2 incomplete outcome measures in a way that precluded scoring). The final analyzed sample (n=88; 44 per group) reflects equal balance across groups with no differential dropout between FI and control conditions reported.

**Motivation:** The exclusions were minimal (5/93 ≈ 5.4%), occurred prior to group comparison, were evenly distributed (44 in each group), and were not related to the outcome or attributable to differential engagement with either intervention. There is no evidence that missingness depended on the true value of the outcome or intervention assignment. This supports a "Low risk" judgment.

**Limitation noted:** No CONSORT flow diagram was provided, and exclusion reasons per group (FI vs. control) are not explicitly broken down, which limits complete verification, though the overall pattern does not suggest bias.

---

## 4. Bias in Measurement of the Outcome — **Some Concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? **No** — The List of Attributions Task (LAT) was designed for the study purpose and captured self-generated attributions, rankings, and importance ratings; while novel/unvalidated, it directly targeted the construct of interest.
- Could measurement/ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? **No** — Both groups completed the identical LAT pre- and post-intervention, administered in the same format.
- Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by participants? **Yes/Probably yes** — The self-reported outcome assessor here is the participant themselves (a participant-reported outcome), who was inherently unblinded due to the nature of the information they received. Additionally, coding of LAT responses into sleep-related/non-sleep-related categories was done initially by a blinded volunteer, but discrepant items (12/870, 1.38%) were resolved through discussion between the volunteer and the (non-blinded to hypotheses, though blind to random assignment) study investigator.
- Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? **Yes** — Because this is a participant-reported outcome directly related to the content of the information just received, knowledge of intervention condition could plausibly influence how participants generated and categorized their own attributions.
- Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? **Probably no/Unclear** — The authors explicitly address and attempt to rule out this concern (demand characteristics/priming), arguing that if priming explained the FI results, a parallel priming effect toward sleep-related attributions should have appeared in the control group, which was not observed. However, this is a post-hoc argument rather than a design feature (e.g., a distractor task or delay was not used to reduce demand characteristics).

**Motivation:** Because the primary outcome is a participant-reported measure directly following disclosure of condition-specific information, and the participant (the outcome assessor) was necessarily unblinded, there is clear potential for the assessment to be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received (demand characteristics/priming). The authors' rebuttal (based on absence of a symmetric effect in controls) is reasonable but not definitive, as it does not rule out an asymmetric susceptibility to priming across information types. The initial blinded coding of open-ended responses by an independent rater partially mitigates concern for the NumberAttributionScore variable, but does not resolve concern regarding whether participants' generation and ranking behavior itself was shaped by expectancy. This results in a judgment of "Some concerns" rather than "High risk," since there is no strong independent evidence that influence definitely occurred, but the potential clearly exists and cannot be excluded from the design.

**Limitation noted:** No steps (e.g., a delay between intervention and outcome assessment, or a cover story to mask study hypotheses) were taken to reduce demand characteristics for this self-reported, hypothesis-transparent outcome.

---

## 5. Bias in Selection of the Reported Result — **Some Concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- Was the data analyzed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? **No information** — There is no mention of a trial registration, published protocol, or pre-specified statistical analysis plan for this study.
- Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements? **No information** — The study defines three outcome variables (NumberAttributionScore, FrequencyScore, ImportanceScore) all derived from a single measure (LAT), and all three are reported consistently with results (all significant), which does not suggest selective suppression of alternative measurements. However, without a registered protocol, it is not possible to confirm no other variables/timepoints were collected and omitted.
- Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? **No information** — No alternative statistical models (e.g., adjusted vs. unadjusted) are mentioned, and the ANOVA approach appears consistently applied across all three outcome variables.

**Motivation:** There is no trial registration or published/available protocol/statistical analysis plan referenced anywhere in the article, making it impossible to verify whether all measured outcomes and analyses were reported. However, there is no internal evidence (e.g., inconsistent subscale reporting, unusual composite constructions, or discrepant outcome definitions across the paper) suggestive of active selective reporting — all three pre-specified-sounding dependent variables are reported with full descriptive and inferential statistics, and results are internally consistent. This pattern of "no information" without any red flags leads to a default judgment of "Some concerns" rather than "High risk," per the RoB2 decision algorithm.

**Limitation noted:** Absence of a registered protocol or published statistical analysis plan is a key ambiguity that limits the ability to fully rule out selective outcome/analysis reporting.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement — **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Summary of domain judgments:**
1. Randomization process: Some concerns
2. Deviations from intended interventions: Some concerns
3. Missing outcome data: Low risk
4. Measurement of the outcome: Some concerns
5. Selection of the reported result: Some concerns

**Rationale:** Per the RoB2 rules, an overall judgment of "High risk of bias" is warranted when a study has "some concerns" for multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result, even in the absence of any single domain rated as "High risk." In this study, four of the five domains (randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result) were each judged to raise "Some concerns," driven predominantly by: (a) lack of reported allocation concealment procedures, (b) unavoidable and unaddressed lack of blinding of participants/investigator combined with a self-reported, hypothesis-transparent outcome susceptible to demand characteristics, and (c) absence of a registered trial protocol or statistical analysis plan to verify non-selective outcome reporting. The convergence of these concerns — particularly the compounding of unblinded delivery with an unblinded, self-generated outcome measure directly tied to the intervention content — substantially undermines confidence in the reported effect (the group × time interaction for attributional change). Therefore, the overall risk-of-bias judgment for this result is **High Risk of Bias**.

**Note on ambiguity/limitations:** This assessment is limited by the absence of a published trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial registry entry, which precluded verification of pre-specification for outcomes and analyses (Domain 5) and full assessment of allocation concealment (Domain 1). These ambiguities are reported as limitations of this assessment rather than assumptions of malpractice by the study authors.
