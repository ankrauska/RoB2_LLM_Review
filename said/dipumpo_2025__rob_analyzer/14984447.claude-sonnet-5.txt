# RoB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Facilitating access to glucometer reagents increases blood glucose self-monitoring frequency and improves glycaemic control (insulin-treated diabetic patients, single-blind, control-matched RCT)

**Outcomes assessed:** (1) SBGM frequency; (2) HbA1c
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), as analysis is described as using "intention-to-treat population, using last observation carried forward"

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgement: Some concerns**

- Sequence generation: The article states patients were "randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion" and were "stratified in three categories of income" prior to randomization. No description of the actual method used to generate the random sequence (e.g., computer-generated, random number table) is provided. This is insufficient to confirm a truly random component was used (Signalling Q1.1: No information/Probably yes at best).
- Allocation concealment: No information is provided on how allocation was concealed from the enrolling personnel/research nurse. There is no description of use of sealed envelopes, central randomization, or any concealment mechanism (Signalling Q1.2: No information).
- Baseline balance: Table 1 shows the two groups were well-matched on all reported baseline variables (age, sex, education, income, diabetes type, duration, insulin use, HbA1c, glucose), with no statistically significant differences reported. This suggests no problem arising from randomization based on observed data (Signalling Q1.3: No).

**Motivation:** Absence of information on both sequence generation and allocation concealment methods, combined with adequate baseline balance, leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" per the RoB 2 algorithm (no information on concealment + no evidence of baseline imbalance problem = Some concerns).

**Limitation noted:** The report does not specify the actual randomization procedure (e.g., random number generation method) or concealment mechanism; this ambiguity could not be resolved without additional protocol documentation.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgement: Some concerns**

- Participants were aware of their allocated group (whether they received free strips or had to purchase them) — blinding of participants to this allocation is not feasible in this study design (Signalling Q2.1: Yes).
- Carers/study personnel (research nurse) were also aware of group assignment, since they physically provided or withheld free strips (Signalling Q2.2: Yes).
- Deviations from intended intervention arising from trial context: The very intervention under test (free vs. paid strips) inherently could influence health-related behaviour (SBGM frequency) through the trial context itself — but this IS the intervention being tested, not a deviation from it. However, there is a serious concern regarding the very high, differential dropout rate (48% in C group vs. 19% in NC group), suggesting differential engagement/motivation between groups possibly related to trial context (Signalling Q2.3: Probably yes — the differential attrition suggests trial engagement differed markedly between groups).
- These deviations (differential dropout, and possible differential contamination with self-purchase of strips in the C group, which was permitted and reportedly occurred) could have affected the outcome and were not balanced between groups (Signalling Q2.4/2.5: Probably yes/No — unbalanced).
- Analysis method: Authors report using intention-to-treat analysis with "last observation carried forward" (LOCF) for missing data (Signalling Q2.6: Probably yes for principle of including all randomized patients, though LOCF has its own limitations addressed in Domain 3).

**Motivation:** Lack of blinding of participants and personnel is unavoidable given the nature of the intervention (cost/access to reagents), and the study appropriately did not attempt to blind these groups to their allocation for the intervention itself. However, the substantial and differential dropout patterns, and the fact that C group patients were permitted to purchase additional strips (potentially diluting the contrast between groups, or conversely reflecting differential trial engagement), raise concerns that deviations related to trial context/engagement affected the outcome unequally between groups. This does not reach "high risk" because the overall analytic approach still followed ITT principles, but the substantial and unbalanced non-adherence to follow-up plausibly affected the result.

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgement: High risk of bias**

- Outcome data were NOT available for all or nearly all randomized participants. Of 62 randomized patients (31 per group), only 25/31 (81%) in the NC group and 16/31 (52%) in the C group completed the full 12-month study — an overall dropout rate of 19% (NC) vs. 48% (C) (Signalling Q3.1: No).
- The dropout rates are large, differ substantially between intervention groups, and the trial authors themselves speculate that "patients in the C group who found the cost of the reagent strips to be problematic may well have dropped out of the study" — i.e., missingness plausibly related to disease control/testing behaviour and to the true value of the outcome (glycaemic control/HbA1c) (Signalling Q3.3: Yes — missingness could depend on outcome).
- The differential dropout (48% vs 19%) between groups is itself evidence that reasons for missingness likely differ by group and may be related to outcome (worse glycaemic control/frustration in C group), which the authors themselves discuss as a plausible explanation (Signalling Q3.4: Yes — likely that missingness depended on its true value).
- Although the authors state they used "last observation carried forward" (LOCF) as an ITT approach, this method is explicitly flagged by RoB 2 guidance as inadequate to correct for bias arising from informative missingness, and does not constitute valid evidence that the result is unbiased (Signalling Q3.2: No — LOCF is not evidence of absence of bias).
- Sample size/power was also compromised: the authors explicitly note the power fell from 0.8 to 0.64 for the HbA1c and SBGM frequency outcomes due to attrition.

**Motivation:** The very high and markedly unequal dropout rates between groups (19% vs. 48%), combined with the authors' own acknowledgement that dropout was likely related to dissatisfaction/cost issues (plausibly correlated with worse glycaemic control), constitute strong evidence that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value and differed by intervention group. The use of LOCF does not adequately address this. This domain is judged **high risk of bias**.

---

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

- Outcome measurement method: HbA1c was measured using a validated laboratory method (DCA 2000 Analyser) and glucose via YSI2300 analyser — objective, standardized, and appropriate methods (Signalling Q4.1: No).
- Measurement/ascertainment did not differ between groups: Both groups underwent identical assessment protocols at the same 2-monthly intervals, with the same devices (Signalling Q4.2: No).
- Outcome assessor blinding: The research nurse who measured HbA1c/glucose and downloaded glucometer memory "did not provide any feedback to the patient concerning glucometer readings" and logged the results by patient number, with no analysis until study completion. Although the nurse was not blinded to group assignment (she personally dispensed or withheld free strips), the outcome itself (HbA1c, glucose) is an objective, laboratory-measured value not susceptible to subjective judgement or influenced by knowledge of group assignment (observer-reported outcome not involving judgement) (Signalling Q4.3/4.4: Aware, but assessment unlikely to be influenced given objective nature of outcome).

**Motivation:** HbA1c and glucose are objective, laboratory-based measurements with essentially no scope for assessor judgement or subjective bias, regardless of blinding status of the assessor. The methodology minimizes risk of differential measurement error between groups.

---

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgement: Some concerns**

- No trial registration, published protocol, or statistical analysis plan is referenced or available in the article to verify whether outcome measures and analyses were pre-specified before unblinded data were available (Signalling Q5.1: No information).
- The primary outcomes (SBGM frequency, HbA1c, random glucose, average glucometer glucose, insulin dose) appear consistently reported across the results/discussion without evidence of selective omission of pre-specified endpoints; however, without a protocol it is impossible to confirm that no alternative analyses, timepoints, or subgroup definitions were selectively reported (Signalling Q5.2/5.3: No information).
- Multiple statistical analyses are described (General Linear Model with LOCF-ITT, and separately a "repeated measures ANOVA" restricted to completers) without clear pre-specification distinguishing which was the primary/planned analysis, raising some concern about potential selective emphasis on the ITT vs. completer analysis.

**Motivation:** In the absence of a registered protocol or statistical analysis plan, it is not possible to confirm that the reported results were not selected, on the basis of the findings, from multiple eligible analyses or outcome definitions. This constitutes "Some concerns" rather than "High risk," as there is no direct evidence of selective reporting, only an inability to rule it out.

**Limitation noted:** No trial registry entry, protocol, or SAP was available for review; this ambiguity limits certainty regarding pre-specification of outcomes and analyses.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall judgement: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Rationale:** Per RoB 2 rules, an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" is warranted if the study is at high risk of bias in at least one domain. This study was judged to be at **High risk of bias in Domain 3 (missing outcome data)**, driven by the large, differential, and outcome-related dropout between the two intervention groups (19% vs. 48%), which the study authors themselves acknowledge as plausibly related to the outcome under study. This alone is sufficient to classify the overall result as high risk of bias.

Additionally, "Some concerns" were identified in three further domains (randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and selection of the reported result), which independently would also support an overall judgement no better than "high risk of bias" given the cumulative impact on confidence in the result, per the RoB 2 rule that multiple domains with "some concerns" can substantially lower confidence in the result.

**Summary limitation statement:** The principal drivers of concern in this assessment are: (1) lack of reported detail on randomization sequence generation and allocation concealment; (2) substantial and unequal attrition between groups, plausibly related to the outcome of interest; and (3) absence of a publicly available trial protocol/registration to verify pre-specification of outcomes and analyses. These ambiguities could not be resolved from the published article alone and are reported here as specific limitations of this risk-of-bias assessment rather than assumptions.
