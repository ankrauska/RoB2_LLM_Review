Based on the meticulous evaluation of the provided randomized controlled trial using the RoB 2 tool framework, here is the detailed risk of bias assessment:

**1. Bias arising from the randomization process**
**Judgment:** Some concerns
**Motivation:** The study utilized "computerized block randomization," which constitutes an adequate random sequence generation method. However, allocation concealment was described simply as being "concealed in envelops" without specifying whether these envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, and sealed, which leaves potential vulnerabilities in concealment. Additionally, while age and sex distributions were similar, there was a statistically significant baseline imbalance between the groups regarding the affected side (P = 0.015). Although this may be due to chance, the combination of limited details on the envelope concealment method and the baseline imbalance raises some concerns.

**2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (Effect of assignment to intervention)**
**Judgment:** Some concerns
**Motivation:** The study is described as a "single-blind trial" where participants were stated to be blind to the allocation; however, the control group received no device (only standard care) compared to the TENS and IFT groups, making true blinding of participants to the intervention received virtually impossible. Furthermore, the principal investigator acted as the therapist administering all treatments, meaning personnel were not blinded. Because subjective responses and physical engagement are required in physical therapy, awareness of the intervention could potentially lead to deviations from the intended interventions. However, an appropriate Intention-to-Treat (ITT) analysis was conducted, as all 105 randomized participants were analyzed in their assigned groups. The lack of blinding warrants a judgment of some concerns.

**3. Bias due to missing outcome data**
**Judgment:** Low risk of bias
**Motivation:** The CONSORT flow chart and the text confirm that exactly 105 participants were randomized and all 105 participants completed the study. There were no dropouts or losses to follow-up at any of the measurement time points (baseline, immediately after the first treatment, before the eighth treatment, and 1 week after the eighth treatment). Therefore, outcome data were available for all randomized participants, completely eliminating the risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

**4. Bias in measurement of the outcome**
**Judgment:** High risk of bias
**Motivation:** The outcomes assessed in the study were pain intensity (using a Visual Analog Scale, a patient-reported subjective outcome) and cervical range of motion (an observer-reported outcome involving physical assessment). The authors explicitly state, "The principal investigator acted as both outcome assessor, taking all study measurements, and as therapist administering all study treatment." Consequently, the outcome assessor was completely aware of the intervention each participant received. Given that the outcomes are highly subjective and dependent on assessor judgment or participant reporting (who were also practically unblinded), it is highly likely that the assessment of the outcomes could have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention received. 

**5. Bias in selection of the reported result**
**Judgment:** Low risk of bias
**Motivation:** The study was prospectively registered as a clinical trial with the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry. The reported outcomes (pain intensity and cervical range of motion) are standard, expected measures for myofascial pain syndrome and were reported for all pre-specified time points. There is no evidence to suggest that the numerical results were selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements or multiple eligible analyses based on the statistical significance or direction of the findings. The data were analyzed and reported comprehensively across all groups.

**6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement**
**Judgment:** High risk of bias
**Motivation:** While the trial had complete follow-up data (Low risk in Domain 3) and no obvious selective reporting (Low risk in Domain 5), it is judged to be at a High risk of bias overall. This is strictly driven by Domain 4, because the individual delivering the physical therapy interventions was the exact same person assessing the highly subjective outcomes (pain and range of motion), without any blinding. In accordance with RoB 2 rules, a high risk of bias in at least one domain automatically leads to an overall judgment of High risk of bias for the result.
