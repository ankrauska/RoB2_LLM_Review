# ROB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Moritz et al., "A randomized controlled trial of internet-based therapy in depression" (Deprexis trial, NCT01401296)

**Outcome assessed:** Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) total score at 8 weeks (primary outcome)

**Numerical result assessed:** ITT ANCOVA, F(1,167)=5.51, p=.02, d=.36 (per-protocol); ITT result p=.03, d=.36

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgement: Some concerns**

- **Sequence generation (1.1):** The article states participants were "randomly allocated to the treatment or wait-list group in consecutive order. No stratification was applied." No further detail is given on the actual method used to generate the sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random number tables). The phrase "in consecutive order" raises ambiguity — this could be compatible with simple/block randomization but could also suggest a systematic assignment process. **Response: No information / Probably yes** (benefit of the doubt given registration as an RCT, but no explicit description).
- **Allocation concealment (1.2):** No description is provided of how the sequence was concealed from the researcher assigning participants (e.g., no central randomization service, no sealed envelopes described). Given the online/automated nature of the trial, concealment may have been inherent to the software system, but this is not explicitly reported. **Response: No information.**
- **Baseline imbalance (1.3):** Table 1 shows no statistically significant differences between groups on any demographic, medical, or psychopathological variable (all p>.1). This is compatible with successful randomization and does not suggest a problem. **Response: No.**

**Motivation:** Because there is no information on the concealment method (signalling question 1.2 = "No information") combined with no information on the precise randomization method beyond "consecutive order," but no evidence of baseline imbalance, this maps to "Some concerns" per the RoB2 algorithm (no information on concealment + baseline differences compatible with chance → Some concerns).

**Limitation/ambiguity reported:** The paper does not specify the actual mechanism of random sequence generation (e.g., computer algorithm, random number table) nor the method of allocation concealment. This ambiguity could not be resolved from the text provided.

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgement: Some concerns**

- **2.1 Participant awareness:** Yes — this is an open-label, unblinded trial; participants clearly knew whether they were in the immediate-access (Deprexis) or wait-list group (they were informed of their allocation and given/promised access codes accordingly).
- **2.2 Personnel/carer awareness:** Yes — there were no "carers" delivering therapy in a traditional sense (self-help intervention), but the research team was aware of group assignment (they sent different emails and different vouchers to each group).
- **2.3 Deviations from intended intervention arising from trial context:** The authors note the wait-list group "improved substantially" and speculate this could be due to "patients' use of treatments other than Deprexis" (contamination with non-protocol interventions) or "spontaneous remission." This raises the possibility that non-protocol interventions occurred, but the paper does not report data confirming or quantifying this systematically for both groups during the 8 weeks (e.g., no report of concurrent psychotherapy or medication changes during the trial window, only at baseline). **Response: No information.**
- **2.4/2.5:** Not able to be assessed given lack of information on whether such deviations were balanced.
- **2.6 Appropriate analysis:** Yes — both ITT (using multiple imputation) and PP analyses were conducted and reported; the ITT analysis with multiple imputation is aligned with intention-to-treat principles and appropriately analyzes participants according to randomized group.
- **2.7:** Not applicable, since appropriate ITT analysis was used.

**Motivation:** Lack of blinding of participants is inherent to this self-help, open-label design and is not, by itself, sufficient for a "high risk" judgement unless deviations from the protocol due to trial context are shown to have influenced the outcome differentially. Since the trialists themselves flag the unexpected wait-list improvement as potentially due to non-protocol interventions but provide no data to confirm or refute whether such non-protocol treatment-seeking behavior differed by group, this introduces genuine uncertainty. Because an appropriate ITT analysis was nonetheless used, the domain is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "High."

**Limitation/ambiguity reported:** No systematic monitoring or reporting of non-protocol treatment-seeking (e.g., new psychotherapy, medication changes) during the 8-week trial period is provided, despite the authors' own speculation that this may explain unexpected control-group improvement.

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgement: Some concerns**

- **3.1 Outcome data available for all/nearly all participants:** No — completion rate was 81.9% (approximately 18% missing), which is not "nearly all." Missingness differed slightly by group (85.7% wait-list vs. 78.1% Deprexis, per Table 1), though this difference was not statistically significant (χ²=2.06, p>.1).
- **3.2 Evidence that the result was not biased by missing data:** The authors performed multiple imputation (SAS PROC MI) for the ITT analysis, which is an appropriate statistical method to address missing outcome data, and the ITT results were "essentially the same" as PP results, suggesting some robustness. However, no explicit sensitivity analysis exploring departures from missing-at-random assumptions was reported, and the imputation model's covariates are not described in detail. **Response: Probably yes**, but with limited justification/detail.
- **3.3/3.4:** Analysis was found to differ between completers (higher baseline quality-of-life subscales) and non-completers (t=2.12, p=.03 and p=.05), indicating that dropout was non-random and related to baseline (and plausibly outcome-related) characteristics. This raises some concern that missingness could be related to the true outcome value, though the direction/magnitude of impact is unclear, and rates did not differ significantly by group.

**Motivation:** While multiple imputation was used and results were largely consistent between PP and ITT analyses, the modest imbalance in completion rates between groups, combined with evidence that non-completers differed systematically from completers on some baseline variables (suggesting missingness related to prognostic/outcome-related factors), and the absence of a detailed justification of the imputation model or dedicated sensitivity analysis, prevents a "Low risk" judgement.

**Limitation/ambiguity reported:** The precise variables included in the multiple imputation model are not reported, limiting the ability to assess whether the imputation approach adequately accounted for missingness related to the true outcome value.

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: High risk of bias**

- **4.1 Appropriateness of the outcome measure:** The BDI is a well-validated appropriate self-report measure of depression severity. **Response: No.**
- **4.2 Could measurement differ between groups:** No indication that the instrument/administration method differed between groups (same online survey, same instrument, same timing). **Response: No.**
- **4.3 Outcome assessors aware of intervention received:** This is a self-report outcome; the "outcome assessor" is the participant themselves. Participants were necessarily aware of their allocation status (immediate access vs. wait-list), since they were informed of group assignment and either used or awaited the program. **Response: Yes.**
- **4.4 Could assessment of outcome be influenced by knowledge of intervention received:** Yes — for participant-reported outcomes such as the BDI, knowledge of receiving the "active" intervention versus being on a wait-list can plausibly influence self-reported symptom ratings (e.g., via expectancy, demand characteristics, disappointment in the control group).
- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received:** This is a comparison against a no-treatment/wait-list control (not an active comparator or placebo), which the RoB2 guidance explicitly identifies as a higher-risk scenario for influence of unblinded self-report outcomes. Additionally, participants had explicit expectations about the program (37.8% expected benefit at baseline, per Table 3), and the trial's own discussion acknowledges that "skepticism prevailed at baseline" and expectancy effects could shape retrospective symptom reporting. This strongly suggests it is likely that self-report of the primary outcome was influenced by knowledge of the assigned condition.

**Motivation:** Because the primary outcome is entirely self-reported (participant is the outcome assessor), blinding of this outcome was impossible given the open-label wait-list design, and the comparator was an inactive wait-list condition (not an active or placebo comparator), the risk that knowledge of allocation influenced self-reported depression scores is high. This maps directly to a "High risk of bias" judgement per the RoB2 decision algorithm for participant-reported outcomes assessed as likely influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

**Limitation/ambiguity reported:** No independent/blinded clinician-rated outcome (e.g., HDRS) was used to corroborate self-report findings; the authors themselves acknowledge this limitation in the Discussion, citing lack of face-to-face clinician contact by design.

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgement: Some concerns**

- **5.1 Pre-specified analysis plan:** The trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01401296), and the protocol registry specifies BDI as the primary outcome and WHOQOL-BREF as a secondary outcome. However, the trial registry entry (as provided) does not clearly pre-specify the DAS and RSE as secondary outcomes, nor does it pre-specify the moderator analyses (baseline severity, treatment status, medication status) or the subgroup analysis based on externally-verified diagnoses, all of which are prominently reported in the results. There is no accessible statistical analysis plan (SAP) date-stamped prior to unblinding. **Response: No information / Probably no.**
- **5.2 Selection from multiple eligible outcome measurements:** The BDI was "segregated into three subscales" post hoc based on a published factor-analytic structure (Shafer, 2006) rather than a pre-specified plan; this is reported alongside the total score. No evidence of selective suppression of alternative depression measures. **Response: Probably no**, but with uncertainty due to limited documentation.
- **5.3 Selection from multiple eligible analyses:** Multiple additional exploratory analyses (moderator analyses, subgroup analyses by diagnostic verification, correlation with session number) are reported, and it is unclear whether these were pre-specified or exploratory post hoc analyses conducted after seeing unblinded data. There is no clear evidence of results being suppressed based on direction/significance, but the absence of a detailed, date-stamped SAP prevents full assurance.

**Motivation:** The trial was registered prospectively with primary/secondary outcomes specified, providing some reassurance against gross selective outcome reporting. However, the numerous exploratory/moderator analyses (by symptom severity, treatment status, medication, diagnostic verification) are not clearly pre-specified in the available registry information, and no statistical analysis plan is referenced. This lack of clarity about whether the primary reported result and its accompanying subgroup analyses were prespecified before unblinded data access leads to "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

**Limitation/ambiguity reported:** No statistical analysis plan (SAP) was available for review; several exploratory/moderator analyses are not verifiable against a pre-registered protocol, so it cannot be conclusively determined whether these were planned a priori or driven by inspection of results.

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall judgement: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Justification:** Per the RoB2 decision rules, an overall "High risk of bias" judgement is warranted if the study is at high risk in at least one domain, OR if there are "some concerns" in multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result. In this trial:

- Domain 4 (Measurement of the outcome) was judged **High risk of bias** on its own, driven by the combination of (a) an entirely self-reported primary outcome, (b) impossibility of blinding participants in an open-label wait-list design, and (c) use of an inactive (wait-list) comparator, which the RoB2 framework identifies as a scenario with high potential for outcome-assessment bias due to expectancy effects.
- This alone is sufficient to classify the overall result as **High risk of bias**.
- This is compounded by "Some concerns" in three additional domains (randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, and selection of the reported result), further lowering confidence in the robustness of the reported treatment effect.

**Summary statement:** The reported BDI effect size (d=.36, ITT) for Deprexis versus wait-list control should be interpreted with substantial caution. The principal driver of concern is the unblinded, self-report nature of the primary outcome combined with an inactive control condition, which creates a plausible mechanism for differential reporting bias favoring the experimental intervention that cannot be ruled out by the available data.
