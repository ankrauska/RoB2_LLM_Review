# RoB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Effects of Supporting Community-Living Demented Patients and Their Caregivers: A Randomized Trial

**Outcome assessed:** Time to institutionalization (period in community care) — primary outcome, assessed via Cox regression (Group and Group*time interaction)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect); this is an unblinded, non-pharmacological, complex intervention trial

---

## 1. Bias Arising from the Randomization Process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- **Sequence generation (1.1):** The allocation sequence was generated using sealed, nontransparent envelopes prepared by the dementia family care coordinator (53 intervention, 47 control), described as randomized. There is a random component described ("drawing one envelope"), so this is judged **Probably Yes**.
- **Allocation concealment (1.2):** This is a major concern. The envelopes were prepared and held by the **dementia family care coordinator**, who was also the person delivering the intervention and had a vested interest in the study outcomes. Although envelopes were "sealed" and "nontransparent," they were not described as sequentially numbered, and there is no description of an independent third party managing allocation. Critically, the last 14 patients from Kuopio were deliberately allocated at an **unequal 2:1 ratio** (10 intervention: 4 control) *after* the initial 86 patients had already been recruited, explicitly to "give more patients the opportunity to receive support from the dementia family care coordinator." This constitutes clear evidence that the randomization/allocation process was manipulated mid-study based on non-chance considerations, raising concern that concealment and integrity of sequence generation may have been compromised, at least for this subgroup. This is judged **Probably No** for adequate concealment.
- **Baseline imbalance (1.3):** Baseline characteristics (Tables 2 and 3) show no statistically significant differences between groups in age, gender, diagnosis, MMSE stage, Barthel Index, living arrangement, or caregiver characteristics. However, there is a numerical imbalance in dementia severity (36% severe in intervention vs. 24% severe in control) that, while not statistically significant, is notable given the paper's own conclusion that severity modifies the treatment effect. This is compatible with chance (P=.244).
- **Overall reasoning:** Although baseline balance appears compatible with chance, the explicit, self-reported mid-study change in allocation ratio (introduced by the same individual who held the allocation envelopes and delivered the intervention) is a clear methodological deviation that raises genuine concern about the integrity of the randomization/allocation concealment process, even though the authors argue post-hoc that this did not bias results. This admission constitutes an important limitation reported by the authors themselves.

## 2. Bias Due to Deviations from Intended Interventions (Effect of Assignment to Intervention)

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- **Awareness of assignment (2.1, 2.2):** This trial was **not blinded**. Patients, caregivers, the dementia family care coordinator, and treating physicians were all fully aware of group assignment, given the nature of the intervention (personal case management, training courses, and 24-hour telephone availability). Blinding of this type of intervention is not feasible.
- **Deviations arising from trial context (2.3, 2.4, 2.5):** Because participants and personnel were aware of allocation, the domain requires examination of whether trial-context-related deviations occurred and affected the outcome. The intervention itself was delivered with substantial variability ("frequency of contacts varied from once a month to five times a day") and was explicitly tailored to "problematic situations." There is no information provided regarding whether comparable non-protocol/ad hoc support was sought or received by control participants outside the trial's own healthcare system (contamination risk is plausible, as control patients had access to usual municipal services that could vary regionally and temporally). No information is given about the balance of non-protocol interventions between groups.
- **Analysis approach (2.6, 2.7):** The primary analysis includes institutionalization and death using Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression on data from **all 100 randomized patients**, with no losses to follow-up reported ("There were no dropouts from the study during the 2-year follow-up"). This is consistent with an ITT approach for the two endpoints tracked (institutionalization and death).
- **Overall reasoning:** The critical concern here is the **decision to stop the study/change enrollment ratio partway through** in a way that was influenced by an unblinded coordinator's discretion, combined with the fact that key subgroup analyses (by dementia severity) were presented as exploratory/post-hoc without correction, potentially influenced by knowledge of outcomes as data accrued. Furthermore, the crucial finding that the treatment effect appeared time-dependent (early institutionalization delayed, but converging by 24 months, formally represented by a significant Group*time interaction term) suggests that the unblinded, intensive, and highly variable-intensity intervention likely influenced the timing (and possibly threshold) of institutionalization decisions in ways that could reflect coordinator/caregiver behavior change rather than a pure disease-modifying effect. Given the substantial potential for unblinded personnel/participants to differentially influence a subjectively-timed, decision-based outcome (institutionalization, determined by a multidisciplinary panel who were reported as "blind to the allocation" but "generally unaware" the patient was in a study — an important caveat, addressed further in Domain 4), and lack of information to rule out that these deviations affected/biased the outcome, this domain is judged **High risk**.

## 3. Bias Due to Missing Outcome Data

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- The report explicitly states: **"There were no dropouts from the study during the 2-year follow-up"** (confirmed in Figure 1, the study flow diagram, which accounts for all 100 randomized patients at both 1-year and 2-year follow-up as either institutionalized, deceased at home, or still at home).
- Outcome data (institutionalization status/timing) were available for **100% of randomized participants** in both groups.
- Deaths in community care were appropriately handled as censoring events in the Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression analyses, which is a statistically appropriate way of handling this competing risk, consistent with an a priori stated analysis plan ("times were censored at death if the patient died in community care before institutionalization").
- **Conclusion:** With complete outcome ascertainment for all randomized individuals, there is no meaningful risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

## 4. Bias in Measurement of the Outcome

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- **Appropriateness of measurement method (4.1):** The primary outcome (time to institutionalization) was ascertained via official records from the Social Insurance Institution's register, an objective administrative data source. This method is appropriate and not likely to introduce differential measurement error itself.
- **Comparability of measurement across groups (4.2):** No evidence that ascertainment method differed by group; both groups' institutionalization dates were drawn from the same national registry.
- **Awareness of assessors (4.3):** The decision to institutionalize was made by "a group of experts (usually a chief physician, head nurse, and social worker)." The paper states these groups "were blind to the allocation and generally unaware that a patient was even participating in the study." This is reassuring; however, it is not entirely certain because:
  - The dementia family care coordinator, who was fully aware of allocation, was actively involved in "assistance with arrangements for social and healthcare services," which plausibly included direct engagement with these very decision-making bodies for intervention patients (e.g., advocating for continued community support, arranging alternative solutions, or contrarily facilitating institutionalization). This creates a substantial risk that even if the formal decision-making panel was nominally "blind," the case coordinator's interactions with the health/social care system for intervention patients only could have systematically influenced the timing or threshold for institutionalization decisions (a decision-based outcome, category 4 in RoB2 guidance: "outcomes that reflect decisions made by the intervention provider/system").
  - No information is given confirming that these panels were unaware of the coordinator's involvement in individual cases, especially given the small size of the municipalities involved (populations as small as 2,300–75,000), where it is plausible that panel members were aware, informally, of coordinator involvement even without knowing formal "trial" status.
- **Likely influence (4.4, 4.5):** Given the plausibility that assessment/decision could be influenced by the intervention (unblinded coordinator advocacy), but with the authors' explicit (if somewhat reassuring but unverifiable) statement of assessor blindness/unawareness, this is judged as a situation where knowledge **could have** influenced the outcome assessment, but there is insufficient evidence that it was **likely** to have done so systematically. This corresponds to **"Some concerns."**

## 5. Bias in Selection of the Reported Result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- **Pre-specified analysis plan (5.1):** No trial registration, published protocol, or statistical analysis plan is referenced or available in the article (this study predates mandatory trial registration norms — recruitment 1993–1995). There is no way to verify whether the Cox regression model (including the Group*time interaction term) was pre-specified or was constructed post hoc after observing that the proportional hazards assumption was violated and/or after seeing a converging pattern of curves. The text states "a time-dependent Cox regression analysis" was used "because the assumption of proportional hazards was not met in the data" — this is a legitimate, transparent methodological adjustment, but its post hoc nature (a decision made in response to observed data patterns) cannot be excluded, and no information is available to rule out that the specific modeling choice (time interaction) was selected because it produced a favorable/significant early effect.
- **Selection among multiple outcome measures/analyses (5.2, 5.3):** The study presents institutionalization data at 6, 12, and 24 months, the overall survival curves, a subgroup analysis by dementia severity, and a Cox model with a time interaction term. Multiple statistical comparisons are shown (e.g., chi-square tests at 1 year and 2 years for institutionalization and death separately, in addition to the survival analysis). Subgroup analyses by severity of dementia (Figure 2a–d) are described as "for descriptive purposes," suggesting they were not pre-specified as a primary confirmatory analysis, and the finding that severely demented patients "benefited most" is emphasized in the abstract/conclusions — a result that has the hallmarks of a data-driven, exploratory finding being elevated in prominence, without correction for multiple subgroup testing.
- **No information** is available on trial registration or a priori statistical analysis plan to confirm these analyses were specified in advance.
- **Overall reasoning:** There is no clear evidence of a pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available, and there are multiple analyses (survival at different time points, subgroup analyses by severity, and the choice of a time-varying Cox model) that could have been selected based on results. However, there is no definitive evidence of outcome-based selective reporting (e.g., no evidence that unfavorable results were suppressed) — this leads to a judgment of **Some concerns** rather than High risk.

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Rationale (per RoB 2 rules):**
Per the RoB 2 algorithm, an overall judgment of "High risk of bias" is warranted if the study is at high risk of bias in **at least one domain**, or if there are "some concerns" in multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result.

In this study:
- **Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions) was judged High risk of bias**, driven by the unblinded, highly variable-intensity intervention, the unexplained mid-study change in allocation ratio motivated by non-random considerations, and the plausibility that trial-context-related behaviors (by the coordinator, caregivers, or decision-making panels) affected the timing of the primary, judgment-based outcome (institutionalization).
- This alone is sufficient to classify the overall risk of bias for this result as **High**, per RoB 2 rules.
- This High risk of bias judgment is further compounded by "Some concerns" identified in **three additional domains** (Domain 1: randomization process; Domain 4: outcome measurement; Domain 5: selection of the reported result), which independently would have supported an overall "High risk of bias" judgment given the cumulative effect of concerns across multiple domains substantially lowering confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported treatment effect.

**Key limitations explicitly noted by the assessors (as required, without assumption beyond what is reported):**
1. The mid-study departure from a fixed 1:1 allocation ratio, controlled by the same individual delivering the intervention, is explicitly acknowledged by the study authors as a design flaw ("With hindsight, we think that a ratio of 1:1 should have been kept").
2. No trial protocol or statistical analysis plan is available/cited to confirm pre-specification of analyses, including the time-dependent Cox model and subgroup analyses by dementia severity.
3. Although the outcome (institutionalization) was assessed by panels described as "blind to allocation" and "generally unaware" of study participation, this claim cannot be independently verified, and the coordinator's direct engagement with social/health services on behalf of intervention patients creates plausible opportunity for indirect influence on this outcome.
4. The intervention could not be blinded due to its nature (case management/support), which is an inherent and unavoidable limitation of this type of trial design, appropriately reflected in the risk-of-bias judgment rather than being treated as a flaw unique to this study's conduct.
