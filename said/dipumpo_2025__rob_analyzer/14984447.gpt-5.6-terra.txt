## RoB 2 assessment

**Result assessed:** the effect of assignment to **free glucometer test strips (no-cost group, NC)** versus **having to purchase further strips (control group, C)** on **glycaemic control measured by HbA1c over the 12-month follow-up**. The reported result is the between-group HbA1c comparison analysed with the General Linear Model (“overall, HbA1c was significantly lower in the NC group vs. the C group, *P* < 0.002”).

**Effect of interest:** effect of assignment to the intervention (intention-to-treat effect).

**Sources available for this assessment:** the article text, Table 1, and Figures 1–3 provided. No protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial registry record, or other prespecified analysis document was available.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

* **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The report states that patients “were randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion to two groups” after stratification into three income categories. However, it does not state how the allocation sequence was generated. There is no description of a computer-generated sequence, random-number table, random permuted blocks, minimization, coin tossing, or another method with a random component.

* **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The article does not describe who generated the allocation sequence, who enrolled participants, who allocated treatment, or any allocation-concealment mechanism. There is no report of central randomization, pharmacy-controlled allocation, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or an equivalent procedure.

* **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  Table 1 reports closely similar baseline characteristics between groups. The groups each included 31 participants and were similar in age, sex, education, income rank, health insurance, diabetes type, diabetes duration, years receiving insulin, baseline self-monitoring frequency, insulin injections, HbA1c, and random glucose. The article states that insulin dose was slightly higher in the control group but that this difference was not statistically significant. The reported baseline information does not identify an imbalance clearly beyond that expected by chance.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The balance of reported baseline characteristics provides no indication that randomization was compromised. However, neither random sequence generation nor allocation concealment is described. Under RoB 2, absence of information about allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement, even where baseline characteristics appear comparable. The appropriate judgement is therefore **some concerns**, rather than high risk, because there is no reported evidence that allocation was predictable, non-random, or manipulated.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

* **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Participants necessarily knew whether they received 100 test strips per month free of charge or instead received only one package of 50 strips and were instructed to purchase any additional strips. Although the report uses the phrase “patient-blinded,” the intervention itself could not practically be concealed from participants.

* **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably yes.**  
  The research nurse supplied strips, performed follow-up procedures, and downloaded meter data. The article does not explicitly state whether the nurse was blinded, but the nurse’s role in supplying strips makes awareness of group assignment probable.

* **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The intended intervention was differential access to test strips: free monthly strips in the NC group and participant-purchased strips in the C group. The report does not identify non-protocol co-interventions, differential clinical care, or failures of intervention implementation caused by the trial context. Physician visit frequency was explicitly intended not to change, and the nurse “did not provide any feedback” about meter readings, random glucose, or HbA1c.

* **2.4–2.5 Were such deviations likely to affect the outcome and balanced between groups? — Not applicable.**  
  No trial-context deviations from the intended intervention were identified.

* **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The article states that “efficacy data from the intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward, were analysed by General Linear Model with baseline values as co-variate.” Participants were analysed according to their randomized groups. The appropriateness of last-observation-carried-forward for missing outcome data is considered separately in Domain 3.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Participants and likely the research nurse were aware of allocation, but awareness itself does not establish bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention. The report does not document deviations from the protocol arising because participants were in the study, differential non-protocol interventions, or differential clinical management that would undermine the assigned comparison. The reported analysis was described as intention-to-treat and retained patients in their assigned groups, albeit using last observation carried forward. Therefore, for the effect of assignment, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

* **3.1 Were data for HbA1c available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  At 12 months, 25 of 31 NC participants remained, compared with 16 of 31 control participants. Thus, 6/31 (19%) participants in the NC group and 15/31 (48%) participants in the control group did not complete the study. The extent of missing final follow-up data was substantial, particularly in the control group.

* **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The authors used last observation carried forward (LOCF) for the stated intention-to-treat analysis. LOCF is a single-imputation method and does not establish that the result was protected against bias due to missing outcomes. No sensitivity analyses are reported to show that plausible values of missing HbA1c data would not materially alter the result. No analysis accounting appropriately for informative dropout is described.

* **3.3 Could missingness in HbA1c depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  It is possible that withdrawal or dropout was related to glycaemic control, self-monitoring behaviour, ability or willingness to purchase strips, or discouragement related to diabetes management. These factors may be associated with the HbA1c outcome. The authors explicitly acknowledge: “We cannot exclude the possibility that patients with intrinsically poorer glycaemic control became discouraged and therefore did less testing or dropped out of the study altogether.”

* **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in HbA1c depended on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  Attrition was strongly unequal: 48% in the control group versus 19% in the no-cost group. The authors specifically interpret the higher control-group dropout as potentially related to the cost of strips: patients in the control group “who found the cost of the reagent strips to be problematic may well have dropped out of the study.” The differential dropout occurred in the group required to purchase strips and is plausibly connected to self-monitoring and diabetes-management behaviour, which are related to the HbA1c outcome. Individual-level reasons for dropout and HbA1c values at dropout are not reported; this is an important limitation, but the magnitude and imbalance of attrition support a “probably yes” response.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Missing outcome data are substantial and markedly more frequent in the control group. The differential attrition is large enough to have a material impact on the reported HbA1c comparison. The use of LOCF does not remove this concern, because it assumes that the previous measurement adequately represents the unobserved subsequent HbA1c and does not address the plausible relationship between dropout and glycaemic control. The article provides no sensitivity analysis demonstrating robustness to missing data. In accordance with RoB 2, the result is at **high risk of bias due to missing outcome data**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

* **4.1 Was the method of measuring HbA1c inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  HbA1c was measured at baseline and at each follow-up visit using the DCA 2000 analyser. The article provides no information indicating that this measurement method was unsuitable for measuring HbA1c or unable to detect plausible between-group differences.

* **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of HbA1c have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The same stated HbA1c measurement procedure was used at each study visit for both groups. Both groups attended follow-up every two months. The report does not indicate different laboratory methods, thresholds, timing, or ascertainment procedures by allocation group.

* **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — No information.**  
  The article does not state whether personnel performing or recording HbA1c measurements were blinded to group assignment.

* **4.4 Could assessment of HbA1c have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  HbA1c was measured using an analyser. This is an objective laboratory measurement that does not require subjective judgement by the assessor in determining the numerical value.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The study used the same objective analyser-based HbA1c assessment in both groups and at the same scheduled follow-up intervals. Although blinding of laboratory personnel is not reported, lack of blinding is not expected to influence an automated biochemical HbA1c measurement. There is no evidence of different ascertainment procedures between groups. This domain is therefore judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

* **5.1 Were the data that produced the HbA1c result analysed in accordance with a prespecified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No protocol, statistical analysis plan, registry record, or dated prespecified analysis document is available. The article describes several analyses: a General Linear Model including group, time, and group-by-time interaction; an intention-to-treat analysis using LOCF; and a repeated-measures analysis of variance among completers. It cannot be determined whether the HbA1c result reported as *P* < 0.002 was specified before outcome data were examined.

* **5.2 Is the reported HbA1c result likely to have been selected on the basis of the results from multiple eligible outcome measurements? — Probably no.**  
  HbA1c was a stated endpoint, measured initially and at two-month intervals. The figure presents the HbA1c trajectory across follow-up, and the text describes the overall between-group finding as well as the time point at which the difference was “most apparent.” The available report does not provide clear evidence that a particular HbA1c time point was selectively reported instead of other eligible time points. However, the absence of a prespecified analysis plan limits certainty.

* **5.3 Is the reported HbA1c result likely to have been selected on the basis of the results from multiple eligible analyses? — No information.**  
  The methods report multiple analytic approaches, including ITT with LOCF and repeated-measures analysis restricted to completers. The report does not clearly identify which of these analyses generated each reported HbA1c estimate or *P* value, nor does it provide a prespecified basis for selecting one analysis over alternatives. There is no direct evidence that the selected analysis was chosen because it produced a favourable result, but the available information is insufficient to exclude this possibility.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The report lacks a protocol or prespecified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available. It also describes more than one potentially eligible analysis but does not clearly link the reported HbA1c finding to one uniquely prespecified analysis. The article does not provide direct evidence of selective reporting sufficient for a high-risk judgement. Nevertheless, the absence of prespecified analysis documentation and ambiguity about the reported analysis result in **some concerns** for this domain.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**  
The study is judged at **high risk of bias** because the HbA1c result is at **high risk of bias due to missing outcome data**. Attrition was substantial and severely imbalanced between randomized groups at 12 months: 19% in the no-cost group and 48% in the control group. The authors used LOCF, which does not demonstrate that missing data could not have biased the estimated intervention effect, and no robustness or sensitivity analyses were reported. The article itself recognizes that poorer glycaemic control could have contributed to reduced self-monitoring or dropout.  

In addition, there are **some concerns** about the randomization process because sequence generation and allocation concealment were not reported, and **some concerns** about selection of the reported result because no prespecified analysis plan was available and multiple analyses were described. Under the specified RoB 2 rules, high risk in Domain 3 requires an overall judgement of **high risk of bias**.
