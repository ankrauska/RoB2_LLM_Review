# RoB 2 Assessment Report

**Preliminary considerations**
- Study design: Individually-randomized parallel-group trial (3 arms: percentile, fixed criterion, random incentives)
- Experimental intervention: Percentile incentive schedule; Comparator: Fixed criterion incentive schedule (primary comparison assessed here, as specified by study hypothesis)
- Outcome assessed: Maintenance of smoking abstinence (breath CO < 3 ppm), specifically ability to sustain consecutive abstinent samples once abstinence criterion was first met
- Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat-type effect)
- Sources used: Journal article only (no protocol, SAP, or registry record identified/available)

---

**1. Bias arising from the randomization process — Judgment: SOME CONCERNS**

Motivation: The allocation sequence had a random component ("Random assignment to one of the three groups was accomplished by randomly assigning 2 participants to the percentile group, 2 to the fixed criterion group and 1 to the random incentives group from each group of 5 participants completing intake"), satisfying signalling question 1.1 (Yes). This is a permuted-block-like design stratified by intent to use medication and order of study entry. However, no information is provided about allocation concealment (signalling question 1.2 — No information): there is no description of a mechanism (e.g., central allocation, sealed envelopes) that would have prevented foreknowledge of assignment, and because the block structure (2:2:1 within groups of 5) is fixed and disclosed, there is meaningful potential for prediction of the final allocation(s) within each block by research staff enrolling participants sequentially.

Additionally, baseline data (Table 1) show a statistically significant imbalance between percentile and fixed criterion groups in education level (Vocational/Technical/Community College attendance: 53% vs 23%, χ²=8.46, p<0.01), a difference the authors themselves flag as unlikely due to chance. While this is only one variable among many compared and could represent a chance finding given multiple comparisons, its magnitude and the explicit p<0.01 threshold raise some concern about a possible imbalance related to the randomization/allocation process, though it is not a directly established key prognostic factor for the primary outcome (abstinence maintenance).

Given: (i) allocation concealment is not reported (No information), and (ii) there is a baseline imbalance that, while not proven to indicate problems, cannot be firmly ruled out as a chance finding — this combination corresponds to "Some concerns" per the RoB2 algorithm (No information on concealment + imbalance suggestive of a potential problem).

*Limitation noted*: No information on allocation concealment methods was reported in the article; this is a specific ambiguity limiting a definitive "Low risk" judgment.

---

**2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention) — Judgment: SOME CONCERNS**

Motivation: 
- Participants were aware of their assigned intervention group (they were explicitly told the nature of their contingency: percentile participants were told the CO value needed "changed to help them smoke less"; fixed criterion participants were told they could "only earn incentives for not smoking"). This constitutes lack of blinding of participants (signalling question 2.1 = Yes).
- Personnel/researchers delivering incentives were also aware of assignment (2.2 = Yes), as incentive delivery necessarily required staff to apply different schedules.
- This is inherent to the nature of the intervention (different behavioral contingencies) and cannot be blinded; this is not, by itself, indicative of high risk, but requires assessment of whether deviations arose from the trial context. The study does not report any non-protocol interventions, contamination, or switching between arms — participants remained in their assigned incentive schedule throughout. There is no evidence of deviations from intended intervention arising from trial context that influenced the outcome unequally across groups.
- The analysis was appropriate: data were analyzed by randomized group assignment for all participants; results are presented based on assigned condition, consistent with ITT principles. No participants appear to have been excluded from the assigned group analysis apart from the two early successes and one who declined participation (94 randomized, 93 analyzed — see missing data domain).
- However, no information is provided in the article as to whether deviations from intended intervention arising from the trial context (e.g., participants comparing notes, awareness of contingency structure differentially motivating behavior) were assessed or excluded. Given that awareness of intervention assignment was unavoidable and the trial context (behavioral economics of a shaping vs. non-shaping schedule) is inherently tied to the mechanism being tested, there is some uncertainty as to whether behavioral differences between groups reflect the true intervention effect or trial-context artifacts (e.g., participant frustration or differential understanding of contingencies, as acknowledged by the authors themselves: "we did not include any systematic way of measuring whether or not participants fully comprehended the incentive contingency").

This leads to a judgment of "Some concerns," reflecting unavoidable and unblinded intervention awareness combined with the authors' own acknowledged uncertainty about participant comprehension of contingencies, without clear evidence of a high-risk trial-context confound.

*Limitation noted*: The article does not report any formal assessment of participant comprehension of intervention contingencies, an ambiguity explicitly acknowledged by the study authors as a limitation.

**3. Bias due to missing outcome data — Judgment: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

Motivation: 
- Of 94 randomized participants, only 93 are described as being analyzed (36 percentile + 40 fixed criterion + 18 random = 94, but the text states "18 assigned to random incentives" vs. abstract stating 93 total — minor discrepancy, but regardless, substantial missing data existed during follow-up).
- Critically, average attendance rates were very low: mean delivered breath CO samples per participant (out of 60 scheduled visits) were 42 (SD=22), 40 (SD=24), and 52 (SD=19) for percentile, fixed criterion, and random groups respectively — meaning approximately 30-33% of visits were missed on average, with very large standard deviations indicating some participants missed the majority of visits.
- The key outcome analyzed in this domain (ability to maintain abstinence once initiated, i.e., "number of visits before first CO<3ppm," "total criterion samples," and consecutive abstinence duration) was calculated only among the subset of participants who achieved at least one abstinent sample (23/36 percentile; 25/40 fixed criterion; 8/18 random) — a substantial proportion of randomized participants (as high as 56% in random group) were excluded from these key analyses of abstinence maintenance because they never achieved the qualifying event.
- More importantly, missing visits (dropout / non-attendance) were not equally addressed: a missed visit reset the incentive value to $5.00 (equivalent to a non-abstinent sample), but for the purposes of the "maintenance" outcome measures, no information is given on how missed visits during a streak of abstinence were handled in the survival-type analysis (Figure 2) — it is unclear whether a missed visit was treated as a failure to maintain abstinence, treated as censoring, or excluded from the denominator, and this determination is likely to differ systematically between groups given differing overall attendance rates (H=8.57, p<0.02, indicating between-group difference in attendance).
- Reasons for missing outcome data (missed visits) are not reported, and it is plausible that missingness relates to smoking status (participants who resumed smoking heavily may be more likely to disengage/drop out), which would relate missingness to the true value of the outcome. This is a classic scenario in addiction/CM research where dropout is often outcome-related.
- No sensitivity analyses addressing the impact of missing/attendance data on the abstinence-maintenance outcome were reported.

Given: (i) data were not available for all/nearly all participants (attendance ~65-87%, with high variability), (ii) no evidence/sensitivity analysis demonstrating the result was robust to missing data, (iii) it is plausible that missingness depended on the true outcome value (relapse leading to disengagement), and (iv) reasons for missingness were not reported and could differ between groups — this meets the criteria for **High risk of bias**.

*Limitation noted*: The article does not report reasons for missed visits/attrition, nor how missing visits were handled within the consecutive-abstinence survival analysis (Figure 2), representing a significant ambiguity that could not be resolved from the text provided.

**4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — Judgment: LOW RISK OF BIAS**

Motivation:
- The outcome (breath CO level, dichotomized at <3 ppm to indicate abstinence) is an objective, validated biochemical measure (Vitalograph CO monitor), not an outcome involving assessor judgment (signalling question 4.1 = No, the method is appropriate and validated per cited literature, e.g., Javors, Hatch & Lamb, 2005).
- The method of measurement was identical across all three groups — the same CO monitor, same procedure (deep breath, hold 20 sec, expire over 20 sec), same threshold (<3 ppm) applied uniformly (signalling question 4.2 = No, measurement did not differ between groups).
- This is a fully automated, objective test with no room for subjective interpretation by either participants or research personnel; therefore, even though participants and staff were not blinded to group assignment, knowledge of intervention assignment could not have influenced the objective breath CO reading itself (signalling question 4.3/4.4: awareness of assignment existed, but this type of outcome — an automated biochemical test — is not susceptible to influence by that awareness).

This corresponds to Low risk of bias, as the outcome measurement is an objective, non-judgment-based observer-reported outcome unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention received, applied identically across all arms.

**5. Bias in selection of the reported result — Judgment: SOME CONCERNS**

Motivation:
- No trial registration, published protocol, or statistical analysis plan is referenced or available in the article/sources reviewed. This prevents verification of whether the analyses reported (time to first abstinent sample, total number of criterion samples, consecutive abstinence duration, total earnings, proportion of visits earning incentive) were pre-specified before unblinded outcome data were available, or were selected among multiple possible analyses (signalling question 5.1 = No information).
- Several outcome analyses are reported (time to first abstinence, number of criterion samples, consecutive maintenance curves via Kaplan-Meier-like proportions, total earnings, proportion of incentivized visits), and while these appear conceptually linked to the study's stated hypothesis, the absence of a pre-registered protocol makes it impossible to rule out selective emphasis on analyses/comparisons that supported the narrative (e.g., emphasis on the percentile vs. fixed-criterion comparison, de-emphasis of the random group in formal statistical testing "because their probability... was equivalent to the percentile incentive group" — a post hoc justification for excluding a group from formal comparison).
- The multiple pairwise statistical tests (Kruskal-Wallis followed by post hoc Mann-Whitney specifically between percentile and fixed criterion) were seemingly planned based on the primary hypothesis, which is reassuring, but without a registered protocol this cannot be confirmed with certainty.

Given no information is available to determine whether the reported analyses were selected from multiple eligible options based on the results, but there is no strong direct evidence of selective reporting either, this corresponds to a judgment of **Some concerns** per the RoB2 default algorithm (No information on pre-specification = Some Concerns).

*Limitation noted*: No trial registry entry, protocol, or SAP was available for review; this ambiguity precludes a definitive judgment of low risk in this domain.

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement: **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

Justification: Per the Cochrane RoB 2 decision rules, an overall judgment of "High risk of bias" is warranted if the study is at high risk of bias in at least one domain, or if there are "some concerns" in multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result. In this study:
- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data) was judged **High risk of bias** due to substantial and differential attendance/attrition (approximately 30% of scheduled visits missed on average, with large variability, unclear handling of missing visits within the key survival-style outcome, no reported reasons for missingness, and no sensitivity analyses).
- Additionally, "Some concerns" were identified in three further domains (randomization process, deviations from intended intervention, and selection of the reported result), driven by unreported allocation concealment procedures, an unexplained baseline imbalance, unavoidable but unaddressed lack of blinding combined with acknowledged uncertainty about participant comprehension of contingencies, and absence of a pre-registered analysis plan.

The presence of a single High-risk domain (missing outcome data) is sufficient, per RoB 2 rules, to classify the overall risk of bias for this result as **High**, and this is further reinforced by the accumulation of concerns across multiple other domains, which collectively and substantially lower confidence in the reported result (that fixed-criterion incentive schedules better maintain smoking abstinence than percentile incentive schedules, once initiated).
