## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

**Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group pilot RCT.  
**Experimental intervention:** Reaching training plus active tRNS.  
**Comparator:** Identical reaching training plus sham tRNS.  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to active versus sham tRNS (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Result assessed:** The post-training (4-week) arm-function result, principally MAS item 6 (MAS6), reported separately for the two allocated groups in Table 3.

The article reports four participants (two active tRNS; two sham tRNS), all of whom completed the intervention and follow-up. It does **not** report a group-level treatment-effect estimate, confidence interval, or the prospectively registered mixed-effects active-versus-sham comparison. Therefore, this assessment concerns the reliability of the reported comparative clinical result, not the descriptive feasibility finding that all four participants completed training.

Sources assessed: the article and the prospectively registered ANZCTR record (ACTRN12614000952640).

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states that an offsite investigator prepared the randomization “using a computer generated random number sequence (1:1).” The registry independently specifies that “a computer generated random number sequence” would be used.

- **1.2 Was allocation concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  The article reports that consecutively numbered opaque envelopes containing group allocation were collected from the offsite investigator by the tRNS intervener only **after initial assessment**. This supports concealment from participants, trainers, and assessors at recruitment and baseline assessment. The report does not state whether envelopes were tamper-proof, sealed, or opened only after irreversible enrolment; however, it does document an offsite allocator, numbered opaque envelopes, and retrieval after baseline assessment. On the available information, concealment was probably adequate.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  The published baseline table reports characteristics for all four randomized participants. The group sizes match the intended 1:1 allocation (two participants per group). Although baseline characteristics differ between individuals, the sample is extremely small and there is no reported imbalance that clearly indicates manipulation or failure of randomization. Differences in such a small sample are compatible with chance.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Random sequence generation was explicitly computer-generated, and allocation was managed offsite using consecutively numbered opaque envelopes after baseline assessment. No evidence indicates compromised allocation concealment or a baseline pattern suggesting a defective randomization process. The incomplete detail about envelope safeguards is a reporting limitation, but does not, in this setting, justify elevating the judgement above low risk.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — No / Probably no.**  
  Participants were told that they “may or may not feel the stimulation irrespective of whether they were receiving active or sham stimulation.” The stimulator was placed out of sight, and both groups had identical electrode placement. The article describes participants as blinded.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention? — Yes, in part.**  
  Training personnel and outcome assessors were reported as blinded. However, the independent tRNS intervener necessarily knew allocation because that person administered 2 mA active tRNS or 0 mA sham tRNS and maintained the separate stimulation log.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention arising because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The study reports that all participants completed all 12 sessions and that there were no adverse events. It does not report differential co-interventions, crossovers, failure to deliver the allocated stimulation condition, or deviations from the common reaching-training protocol attributable to knowledge of allocation.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — No.**  
  The protocol specified an intention-to-treat analysis and a non-parametric mixed-effects model using a two-group (active versus sham) by three-phase (0, 4, and 12 weeks) design, followed by between-group comparisons. The article did not report that analysis. Instead, it calculated and narratively described change within each individual case. Table 3 gives individual data, but no active-versus-sham effect estimate, no group-level analysis, no confidence interval, and no between-group comparison.

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of this analysis failure on the result? — Yes.**  
  The absence of the planned comparative analysis is material. With two participants per arm, individual within-person changes cannot estimate a reliable effect of assignment to active rather than sham tRNS. The article itself concludes that it was “not evident in this very small sample, that there was a benefit over and above reaching training alone,” but it provides no comparative analysis to support or quantify that conclusion.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
There is no evidence that participants or trainers deviated from the allocated intervention because of knowledge of assignment. However, the reported clinical result was not analysed using the prospectively specified intention-to-treat, active-versus-sham comparative approach. The paper presents four descriptive case trajectories rather than an estimate of the randomized comparison. This is not merely imprecision: it prevents a valid estimate of the effect of assignment to active tRNS versus sham tRNS for the assessed clinical result. Therefore, the risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions—specifically the analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment—is judged **high**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Yes.**  
  The article states that all four participants completed all 12 treatment sessions. Table 3 reports MAS6 values at baseline (T0), post-training (T1), and follow-up (T2) for all four participants. Thus, for the reported MAS6 outcome, data are available at each scheduled time point for every participant described in the article.

- **3.2–3.4 — Not applicable.**  
  Because outcome data were available for all reported randomized participants, no further missing-data concerns arise for the assessed MAS6 result.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
There is no attrition in the published cohort of four participants, and post-training and follow-up MAS6 data are tabulated for every participant. There is no indication of missing MAS6 data, imputation, differential loss to follow-up, or outcome censoring.

**Important reporting ambiguity:**  
The registry currently records a final sample size of eight, whereas the article reports four participants. The article does not explain this discrepancy. For the four participants included in the publication, MAS6 data are complete. However, the unexplained difference between the registry’s final sample size and the published sample raises concern about reporting completeness and is addressed under Domain 5 rather than as missing outcome data for the four published cases.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  MAS item 6 (upper-arm function) was specified as the article’s primary clinical outcome and was measured at baseline, post-training, and follow-up. No evidence is presented that MAS6 was unsuitable, invalid, or insensitive for the stated purpose.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  The same scheduled assessment time points and the same outcome measure were used for active and sham participants. There is no report of different assessment methods, different thresholds, or differential assessment opportunities between groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — No.**  
  The article states that study personnel involved in assessment and training, together with participants, were blinded to allocation for the duration of the study. Allocation was held by the tRNS intervener and revealed to the investigator team only after training and follow-up assessments were complete.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The outcome was assessed using the same instrument and schedule in both randomized groups, and the outcome assessor was reported to be blinded. On the information reported, knowledge of active versus sham allocation was not available to the assessors and therefore is unlikely to have influenced MAS6 measurement.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data producing this result analysed according to a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No.**  
  The registry was prospectively registered before enrolment. It specified:
  - a **primary outcome** of the composite score of MAS upper-limb items 6–8;
  - an intention-to-treat analysis;
  - a non-parametric mixed-effects model for active versus sham groups across three time points; and
  - between-group comparisons.

  The article instead identifies **MAS6 alone** as its primary clinical outcome, reports individual participant changes, and does not report the registered composite primary outcome or the planned mixed-effects/between-group analysis.

- **5.2 Was the reported result likely selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements? — No information / some concern.**  
  The protocol specified the MAS items 6–8 composite as the primary outcome. The article reports MAS6 in Table 3 but does not provide the planned composite outcome or the separate values for MAS items 7 and 8. The protocol also specified several additional clinical outcomes—spasticity, pain, quality of life, and other motor measures—that are not presented as numerical results in the paper. This establishes a discrepancy between prospective outcome intentions and published reporting. However, the article does not state why these outcomes were omitted or why MAS6 replaced the composite, so it cannot be established from the available records that selection occurred specifically because of the results.

- **5.3 Was the reported result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses? — Some concerns.**  
  A prospectively specified group-by-time mixed-effects analysis and between-group comparisons were not reported. Instead, individual descriptive change scores were reported. This is a clear analysis-plan discrepancy. There is insufficient evidence to establish that the alternative descriptive analysis was chosen based on its statistical direction, magnitude, or significance; nevertheless, the lack of adherence to the prospective plan raises concern.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The trial was prospectively registered, allowing direct comparison of the pre-specified and published analyses. The registry specified a composite MAS6–8 primary outcome and a group-level intention-to-treat comparative analysis. The publication instead presents MAS6 as the primary outcome and individual case-level changes, while omitting the registered composite result and planned comparative analysis. This raises **some concerns** about selective reporting. A high-risk judgement is not assigned because the available documents do not demonstrate that the choice of outcome or analysis was made on the basis of the observed results.

**Specific ambiguity and limitation:**  
The registry lists a final sample size of eight, but the article reports only four participants, without explaining whether additional participants were recruited, excluded, unpublished, or reported elsewhere. The registry’s inclusion criterion was 6–24 months after stroke, whereas the article reports recruitment of people 6 months to 5 years after stroke. These discrepancies further reduce confidence in complete reporting, but their reasons cannot be determined from the available material.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation for overall judgement:**  
The overall judgement is **high risk of bias** because Domain 2 is judged at high risk of bias. The published paper does not provide the prospectively planned intention-to-treat, active-versus-sham analysis and instead reports descriptive outcomes for four individual cases. Consequently, the paper cannot provide a reliable randomized estimate of whether active tRNS adds benefit beyond sham tRNS during reaching training.

In addition, Domain 5 has **some concerns** because the prospectively registered composite primary outcome (MAS items 6–8) and planned group-level analysis were not reported as specified. The study remains informative for its descriptive feasibility observations—completion of sessions and absence of recorded adverse events among the four published participants—but the randomized comparative clinical result is at **high risk of bias**.
