**1. Bias arising from the randomization process**
**Judgment:** Low risk of bias
**Motivation:** The study utilized a computer-generated randomization list to assign the procedure type, ensuring a random sequence generation. Allocation concealment was adequately maintained through a telephone inquiry system. There were no substantial imbalances in baseline characteristics between the two groups (e.g., age, gravidity, parity, Quetelet Index, and estimated uterine weight), indicating that the randomization process was successful and no significant baseline confounding was introduced.

**2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions**
**Judgment:** Low risk of bias
**Motivation:** Due to the nature of the interventions (abdominal versus laparoscopic-assisted vaginal hysterectomy), both the participants and the surgical personnel were aware of the assigned treatment. However, there is no evidence of deviations from the intended interventions that arose because of the trial context. There were no crossovers or conversions from LAVH to abdominal hysterectomy. The analysis appeared to include all randomized participants in their assigned groups for the primary outcome, adhering to intention-to-treat principles.

**3. Bias due to missing outcome data**
**Judgment:** Low risk of bias
**Motivation:** Data for the primary outcome (length of hospital stay) and other immediate postoperative clinical data appear to be available for all 48 randomized participants (28 in the LAVH group and 20 in the AH group). Although only 72.9% of the women answered the long-term postoperative questionnaire, the primary clinical outcomes and hospital stay data were not affected by this missingness, rendering the risk of bias due to missing outcome data low for the main results.

**4. Bias in measurement of the outcome**
**Judgment:** High risk of bias
**Motivation:** The primary outcome measure was the length of hospital stay. The study explicitly states, "Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital." Since the participants were not blinded to their intervention (having either a large abdominal incision or laparoscopic ports), their knowledge of the received intervention highly influenced their decision on when they felt ready to be discharged. The subjective determination of the primary outcome by unblinded participants makes it highly likely that the assessment of the outcome was influenced by the knowledge of the intervention received. 

**5. Bias in selection of the reported result**
**Judgment:** Some concerns
**Motivation:** While the length of hospital stay was pre-specified as the primary outcome for the sample size calculation, there is no reference to a publicly available, date-stamped trial protocol or statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available. Although it is unlikely that the primary outcome was selected from multiple eligible measurements or analyses based on the results, the absence of a verified pre-specified analysis plan raises some concerns regarding the potential for selective reporting of secondary subjective outcomes (e.g., the non-validated questionnaire).

**6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement**
**Judgment:** High risk of bias
**Motivation:** The study is judged to be at high risk of bias in at least one domain (Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome). The primary outcome (length of hospital stay) was determined entirely by the patients themselves ("Each patient decided for herself..."), who were unblinded to the surgical approach. This lack of blinding coupled with a subjectively decided primary outcome substantially lowers confidence in the objective validity of the result, directly driving the overall high risk of bias judgment.
