# ROB 2 Assessment: Oral Sodium Bicarbonate for Metabolic Acidosis in PD Patients

**Study design:** Individually-randomized, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial
**Experimental intervention:** Oral sodium bicarbonate 0.9g thrice daily
**Comparator:** Placebo (starch tablet)
**Outcome assessed:** Primary outcome — Overall SGA score (also considering hospitalization duration as secondary outcome)
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat, as explicitly stated: "Analyses were intention to treat irrespective of adherence to treatment regimen")

---

## 1. Bias Arising from the Randomization Process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Sequence generation: The paper states allocation was performed "by a computer-generated list" — this satisfies a random component (Signalling Q1.1 = Yes).
- Allocation concealment: The list "was used for packaging of the tablets and then maintained by a third party that was not involved in the conduction of the study." This suggests reasonable concealment; tablets were indistinguishable in appearance, packaging, labeling, and taste (Q1.2 = Probably Yes). However, no explicit detail is given about sequential numbering, opaque sealing, or the exact mechanism preventing foreknowledge of allocation by enrolling investigators, so full assurance per the "extended criteria" (Table 2 of RoB2 guidance) cannot be confirmed.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows no statistically significant differences between groups in most baseline characteristics. However, there are numerically notable imbalances in comorbidities of clinical relevance to the outcomes under study (e.g., congestive heart failure: 8 vs 12; peptic ulcer disease: 2 vs 7; cerebrovascular disease: 6 vs 1; diabetes as underlying cause: 8 vs 12). None reach conventional statistical significance, and the authors explicitly acknowledge and adjust for comorbidity (Charlson Index) in their analyses. This is compatible with chance given the small sample size (n=30/group), but the imbalance in heart failure and diabetes is clinically important as these could influence both nutritional status and hospitalization outcomes independently of bicarbonate treatment.

**Conclusion:** Allocation concealment appears adequate but not described in full detail; sequence generation was random; baseline imbalances are plausibly compatible with chance but are noted as a limitation by the authors themselves. This combination supports "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

## 2. Bias Due to Deviations from Intended Interventions (Effect of Assignment to Intervention)

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- **Blinding of participants and personnel:** Both patients and investigators were blinded ("Both patients and investigators were blinded"). Placebo tablets were identical in appearance, packaging, labeling, and had identical artificial mint flavor to mask taste differences — a robust blinding strategy (Q2.1 and Q2.2 = No/Probably No, participants/personnel unaware).
- **Deviations from intended intervention:** A small number of patients discontinued study medication due to adverse effects (4 in treatment group: dyspepsia/dizziness; 3 in placebo group: dyspepsia/skin rash). These discontinuations are plausible drug-related events consistent with the trial protocol (i.e., expected AEs leading to cessation) rather than deviations arising because of trial context/lack of equipoise. Importantly, these patients continued to have biochemical and nutritional follow-up per protocol, and were retained in the analysis (Q2.3 = No).
- **Analysis:** The paper explicitly confirms an intention-to-treat analysis was used ("Analyses were intention to treat irrespective of adherence to treatment regimen"), and patients who discontinued medication were still followed and included in outcome assessments (Q2.6 = Yes, appropriate analysis).

**Conclusion:** Successful double-blinding, minimal and protocol-consistent deviations, and confirmed ITT analysis support a low risk of bias judgment for this domain.

---

## 3. Bias Due to Missing Outcome Data

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- All 60 randomized patients (30/30) appear to be accounted for throughout follow-up. Even the 7 patients who discontinued study medication (4 treatment, 3 placebo) had their "biochemical tests and nutritional assessment...continued for 52 wk according to the study protocol" (Q3.1 = Yes/Probably Yes — outcome data available for nearly all randomized participants).
- Deaths occurred during the study (2 in treatment group, 5 in placebo group) and are explicitly reported with causes; these are appropriately handled in survival analysis (log-rank test) and do not represent unaccounted missingness for the SGA outcome, since it is unclear whether SGA data collection continued for patients up to the point of death/dropout — this is a minor ambiguity, but given the small numbers and the fact that mortality itself was a reported outcome (not silently missing), this does not raise substantial concern.
- No indication of differential missingness related to the true outcome value. No evidence of imputation methods used to inflate completeness artificially.

**Conclusion:** Data appear essentially complete for all randomized participants, supporting a low risk judgment. **Limitation/ambiguity to report:** The manuscript does not explicitly provide a CONSORT flow diagram or detailed accounting of exact numbers of SGA assessments completed at each time point for patients who died or withdrew before 52 weeks, introducing minor uncertainty.

## 4. Bias in Measurement of the Outcome

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- **Appropriateness of measurement method:** SGA is a validated tool with established clinical relevance (referenced to CANUSA study), and NPNA/biochemical measures used standard, accredited laboratory methods (Q4.1 = No, not inappropriate).
- **Comparability of measurement across groups:** All patients underwent identical follow-up protocols, visit schedules, and identical measurement methods regardless of group assignment (Q4.2 = No).
- **Blinding of outcome assessors:** SGA — the primary outcome — was performed by "two trained observers who were blinded from the treatment group allocation and biochemical results of the patients," with a documented high inter-rater reliability (Cohen's kappa = 0.84) (Q4.3 = No, assessors unaware).
- Anthropometric measurements were similarly performed by blinded trained observers.
- Because outcome assessors were blinded and outcome ascertainment methods were standardized and appropriate, knowledge of intervention could not have influenced assessment.

**Conclusion:** Given documented blinding of the SGA assessors and standardized objective measurement protocols across groups, this domain is judged as low risk of bias.

---

## 5. Bias in Selection of the Reported Result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- The study does not reference a publicly available, date-stamped, pre-registered trial protocol or statistical analysis plan (SAP). No trial registration number is provided in the article (this study predates widespread mandatory trial registration), making it impossible to confirm whether the analysis was conducted according to a pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded data were available (Q5.1 = No information).
- The primary outcome (SGA score) is clearly pre-specified in the Methods and reported fully and consistently with a pre-defined follow-up schedule (0, 12, 24, 36, 52 weeks), which reduces some concern.
- However, multiple additional nutritional parameters (NPNA, FEBM, anthropometric LBM, serum albumin) and multiple sub-item SGA scores (anorexia, weight loss, subcutaneous fat, muscle mass) are reported, several of which yielded non-significant results (e.g., FEBM and LBM "were not statistically significant after adjusting for Charlson Comorbidity Index," serum albumin difference "was NS"). While these are reported (reducing likelihood of outright suppression), the number of secondary/exploratory comparisons raises the possibility of selective emphasis on statistically significant findings (e.g., anorexia and weight loss subscales highlighted as significant while subcutaneous fat and muscle mass are not), without correction for multiple comparisons explicitly discussed beyond Bonferroni post hoc tests for the ANOVA.
- Hospitalization analysis: multiple hospitalization-related endpoints are analyzed (number of admissions, duration, cause-specific breakdown in Table 4), and the number of admissions results were non-significant (P=0.07) while duration was significant (P=0.02) — both reported, which is reassuring but again reflects a multiplicity of related outcomes.

**Conclusion:** Because there is no verifiable pre-registered protocol/SAP available to confirm the reported result was not selected based on the direction/significance from multiple eligible measurements or analyses, and because several nutritional sub-outcomes are analyzed with mixed reporting emphasis, this domain is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

**Stated limitation:** No trial registry entry or protocol/SAP was accessible for cross-verification; this ambiguity is reported as a limitation of this assessment rather than assumed.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall Judgment: Some concerns**

**Rationale (per RoB 2 rules):**
- Domain 1 (Randomization process): Some concerns
- Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions): Low risk
- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data): Low risk
- Domain 4 (Measurement of outcome): Low risk
- Domain 5 (Selection of reported result): Some concerns

No domain was judged to be at **High risk of bias**, but **two domains** (Randomization process and Selection of the reported result) raise **"Some concerns."** Per Table 1 of the RoB 2 framework: *"Some concerns, if the study is judged to raise some concerns in at least one domain for this result, but not to be at high risk of bias for any domain."* Since concerns exist in two domains but neither reaches "High risk," and there is no indication that these concerns compound severely enough to substantially lower confidence in the result to the level of "High risk" (the trial had robust blinding, ITT analysis, accredited lab methods, and low missing data), the overall judgment is:

### **Overall Risk of Bias: SOME CONCERNS**

**Key limitations to note explicitly (ambiguities, not assumptions):**
1. No accessible trial registry, published protocol, or statistical analysis plan was cited/available in the article to confirm a priori specification of outcomes and analyses.
2. Allocation concealment mechanism is described only briefly ("maintained by a third party"), without full procedural detail (e.g., sequential numbering, tamper-proof sealing).
3. Small clinically relevant (though not statistically significant) baseline imbalances in comorbid conditions (heart failure, peptic ulcer disease, cerebrovascular disease) exist between groups, which the study authors address by covariate adjustment but which could still introduce residual confounding given the very small sample size (n=60 total).
