# ROB 2 Assessment Report

**Preliminary note on applicability:** This study is a randomized trial, but it is fundamentally a **methodological/survey research trial** in which pharmacists were randomized to different survey delivery methods, questionnaire lengths, item placements, and were stratified by gender. The "intervention" is the survey administration method, and the "outcomes" are response rate, response time, and cost. Given the nature of the study, several RoB 2 domains (particularly blinding-related ones) require adaptation, as blinding of participants to delivery method is inherently impossible. The primary result assessed here is the **effect of delivery method (postal mail vs. email vs. hybrid) on response rate**, which was the key significant finding of the study.

---

## Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:** The report states that "the sample was stratified by delivery method, questionnaire length, item placement, and gender of respondent for a total of 300 observations within each subgroup." However, no information is provided about the actual method used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random number tables). There is no description of how sequence generation was performed, nor is there any information on allocation concealment (i.e., whether personnel enrolling/assigning pharmacists to conditions could foresee assignments). Additionally, comparison of baseline demographic characteristics across delivery-method groups (Table 1) reveals differences that raise concern: e.g., Non-Hispanic White representation was 86.3% in postal mail vs. 68.7% in email vs. 74.0% in hybrid; Black representation ranged from 3.2% (postal) to 26.4% (email); hospital practice site ranged from 19.0% to 35.0%. These are sizeable differences in prognostic/demographic factors relevant to survey response behavior, and no statistical testing or explanation of these imbalances is provided. Because sequence generation and allocation concealment methods are not reported, and because baseline imbalances are present without clear reassurance that they are due to chance (the databases for postal/hybrid vs. email were drawn from different commercial vendors, which is itself a potential source of systematic difference rather than a chance artifact of randomization), this domain is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk." The use of two different sampling databases (MMS vs. Statlistics) for different arms is a notable ambiguity that is reported here as a limitation of the assessment, since the article does not clarify whether randomization to email vs. postal/hybrid occurred prior to or after database-specific sampling, which could itself be a source of confounding rather than a "true" randomization artifact.

---

## Domain 2: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**Judgment: Low risk**

**Motivation:** This domain, as conceived in RoB 2, addresses deviations from assigned intervention due to lack of blinding of participants/personnel and inappropriate analysis. In this trial, the "intervention" is the mode of survey delivery, which cannot be blinded (participants necessarily know whether they received a postal letter, an email, or a postcard/hybrid approach) — however, this is an inherent, expected feature of this type of methodological trial rather than a source of bias, since awareness of allocation does not create a "co-intervention" or non-protocol crossover risk in the traditional clinical sense. There is no evidence of contamination between groups (e.g., a pharmacist assigned to email switching to postal mail), and the delivery protocols described (timing of follow-up mailings/emails, reminder postcards) were implemented consistently and as intended within each arm. The analysis was conducted according to the assigned group (i.e., an intention-to-treat-like approach, analyzing all who were part of each assigned delivery arm), and appropriate statistical tests (chi-square, F-tests) were applied to the assigned groups. No evidence suggests failures in implementation of the assigned delivery method within arms. Therefore, this domain is judged as low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

---

## Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgment: High risk**

**Motivation:** The outcome (response rate) was explicitly defined as the percentage of eligible respondents returning **completed** questionnaires "with no missing data for any questions." This is a critical issue: many surveys were returned but excluded from the numerator because they had incomplete data (the article reports "a high prevalence (9.9%) of responses dropped because of incomplete data" for postal mail and 9.4% for email). This means that outcome data (in the sense of a fully completed questionnaire) were missing for a substantial fraction of respondents who did engage with the survey, and this exclusion was directly related to how respondents interacted with the delivery method — i.e., missingness in the outcome plausibly depends on its true value and/or on the intervention group. Specifically, according to Figure 1, of 526 postal surveys returned, only 474 were "complete" (52 dropped, ~9.9%), and of 180 email surveys returned, only 163 were complete (17 dropped, ~9.4%); for hybrid, 82 returned vs. 73 complete (9 dropped, ~11%). Because the rate and reasons for incompleteness could differ systematically between delivery methods (e.g., web-based surveys may allow easier skipping of questions, paper surveys may be returned with unanswered sensitive questions such as income), and because no sensitivity analysis was performed to determine whether the results would be robust to inclusion of partially completed surveys, there is a real risk that the classification of "response rate" (restricted to complete cases only) introduces bias into the delivery-method comparison. The authors explicitly acknowledge this as a limitation ("absence of non-response analysis and missing-data response analysis"). Given that (1) outcome data (complete responses) were not available for all participants who engaged with the survey, (2) there is no evidence that this exclusion was unrelated to the true outcome or delivery method, and (3) no sensitivity analysis was conducted, this domain is judged as **High risk of bias**.

---

## Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:** The outcome (response rate, response time) is objectively defined and ascertained (receipt of a returned, completed questionnaire, dated), which minimizes subjective judgment in outcome assessment — this is closer to an "observer-reported outcome not involving judgement" category. There is no separate "blinded outcome assessor," as receipt/completion status was recorded via administrative/database processes (postal tracking, online submission timestamps) rather than by human judgment susceptible to bias from knowledge of allocation. However, a concern arises regarding **measurement comparability across intervention groups**: the method of ascertaining a "complete" response could differ structurally between delivery modes. For the email/hybrid (web-based) survey, the platform "did not require" a response to every question to proceed, meaning missing-item detection and the resulting classification of "complete" vs. "incomplete" may function differently online than on paper, where any blank field would be manually identified by data-entry staff. This raises the possibility that measurement/ascertainment of the outcome (completeness) differed systematically between intervention (delivery method) groups — a methodological asymmetry inherent to the different modes rather than random error. This is a signal for "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk," since the measurement procedure to classify a completed response may not be strictly comparable across arms, though there is no evidence of intentional or knowledge-driven manipulation of assessment (it is a structural/administrative difference, not an assessor-bias issue in the classic sense).

## Domain 5: Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:** There is no mention of a publicly registered trial protocol or pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before data collection/unblinding. The study reports response rate, response time, and cost outcomes across four classification factors (delivery method, questionnaire length, item placement, gender), with multiple possible cross-tabulations (as detailed exhaustively in Table 2). While the reported analyses (chi-square for rates, F-tests for times) appear to have been applied comprehensively and symmetrically across all four classifications (not selectively reported only where significant), the absence of a pre-registered protocol or statistical analysis plan makes it impossible to confirm that: (a) the definition of "response rate" as restricted to fully completed questionnaires was pre-specified rather than chosen post hoc (e.g., an alternative definition including partially completed surveys could have been used, and the article itself notes the "combined response rate" including incomplete data as an alternative metric that was reported only secondarily/differently); and (b) no other outcome measures or analyses were conducted but not reported. Given the lack of a pre-specified, time-stamped analysis plan and the existence of an alternative outcome definition (combined response rate including incomplete surveys) that is presented differently in the Discussion than in the Results, there is ambiguity that cannot be resolved from the article alone. This is reported as a limitation of the current assessment. Judgment: **Some concerns**.

---

## Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Following the RoB 2 algorithm for reaching an overall judgement:
- Domain 1 (Randomization process): Some concerns
- Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions): Low risk
- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data): **High risk**
- Domain 4 (Measurement of outcome): Some concerns
- Domain 5 (Selection of reported result): Some concerns

Because Domain 3 (bias due to missing outcome data) was judged to be at **High risk of bias** — driven by the explicit exclusion of incompletely-filled surveys from the primary "response rate" outcome, differential and unexplained rates of incompleteness across delivery methods, and absence of any sensitivity analysis — the overall result for the key comparison (effect of delivery method on response rate) must be judged **High risk of bias**, per the RoB 2 rule that a "High" judgement in any single domain drives the overall judgement to "High," independent of the ratings in other domains. This is further compounded by the presence of "Some concerns" in three additional domains (randomization process, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result), which substantially lowers overall confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported effect estimates.

**Stated limitations/ambiguities identified during this assessment (as required, without assumption):**
1. No description of the method of allocation sequence generation or allocation concealment procedure was provided in the article.
2. Use of two different commercial sampling databases (MMS for postal/hybrid; Statlistics for email) for different randomized arms is not explained, and it is unclear whether this could have introduced a systematic (non-chance) source of baseline imbalance rather than a true randomization artifact.
3. No trial registration, protocol, or statistical analysis plan was referenced or made available, precluding confirmation of pre-specification of outcome definitions and analyses.
4. The definitional restriction of "response" to only fully-completed questionnaires, combined with differential/unexplained dropout due to incompleteness across arms, could not be further clarified from the text.
