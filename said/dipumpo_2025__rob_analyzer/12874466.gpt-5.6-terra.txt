## RoB 2 assessment

**Study:** *Oral Sodium Bicarbonate for the Treatment of Metabolic Acidosis in Peritoneal Dialysis Patients: A Randomized Placebo-Control Trial.*

**Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial (30 participants per group).

**Experimental intervention:** Oral sodium bicarbonate 0.9 g three times daily for 12 months.

**Comparator:** Matched placebo (starch tablet).

**Result assessed:** Effect of assignment to oral sodium bicarbonate versus placebo on the **primary outcome, overall Subjective Global Assessment (SGA) score**, assessed longitudinally from baseline to 52 weeks, with the reported primary analysis being repeated-measures ANOVA adjusted for Charlson Comorbidity Index.

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect).

**Sources available for this assessment:** The published article and its reported tables/figures only. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial-registry record was available in the supplied material.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The report states: “*Individuals were randomized by a computer-generated list*.” This is an explicitly random method of sequence generation.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  The computer-generated list was used for tablet packaging and “*maintained by a third party that was not involved in the conduction of the study*.” The tablets were packaged and labelled identically, and both investigators and participants were blinded. This supports that the allocation code was held independently from those recruiting and managing participants.  
  The report does not explicitly describe the precise operational process by which a marked pack was irreversibly assigned after enrolment, so the answer cannot be unequivocal. Nevertheless, the independent third-party maintenance of the randomization list, identical packaging, and investigator blinding provide sufficient support for **probably adequate allocation concealment**.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  Baseline data were reported for all 60 randomized participants. The article states that there was “*no significant difference in any baseline parameter between the two groups*.” There were numerical differences in some conditions—for example, congestive heart failure (12 versus 8), cerebrovascular disease (1 versus 6), and peptic ulcer disease (7 versus 2)—but the small sample size makes chance imbalance plausible. There is no reported marked imbalance in group sizes, no extreme imbalance in a clearly key prognostic factor, and no pattern of differences that clearly indicates compromised randomization.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The allocation sequence was computer generated, and the code was maintained by an independent third party. Baseline characteristics do not provide evidence that randomization failed. Therefore, the result is judged at **low risk of bias arising from the randomization process**.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  The bicarbonate and placebo tablets had identical appearance, packaging, labelling, and artificial mint flavour. The report explicitly states: “*Both patients and investigators were blinded*.”

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  Investigators were explicitly blinded, and biochemical and nutritional results were completed before the randomization code was broken at the end of the study.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Not applicable.**  
  Because patients and investigators were blinded, there is no reported evidence that awareness of allocation caused differential co-interventions, differential implementation of peritoneal dialysis, or other deviations arising from the trial context.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The article explicitly states: “*Analyses were intention to treat irrespective of adherence to treatment regimen.*”  
  Seven participants stopped study medication early because of adverse symptoms (four in the bicarbonate group and three in the placebo group), but the report states that biochemical tests and nutritional assessment continued to 52 weeks according to protocol. This is consistent with an analysis of participants according to randomized assignment.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The study was placebo controlled with blinded participants and investigators. The report does not identify trial-context-related deviations from the assigned strategies. Importantly, treatment discontinuation did not result in stated discontinuation of nutritional follow-up, and the analysis was explicitly described as intention-to-treat irrespective of adherence. Accordingly, the primary SGA result is at **low risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for overall SGA score available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  The trial followed participants for 52 weeks, during which **two participants in the bicarbonate group and five in the placebo group died**. In addition, one participant in each group was transferred to long-term hemodialysis, and one participant in the bicarbonate group underwent kidney transplantation. Participants who died could not contribute SGA measurements after death.  

  The report does not provide a participant flow diagram, does not state the number of participants with SGA data at each assessment time, and does not report exactly how deaths, transfer to hemodialysis, transplantation, or any other incomplete assessments were handled in the repeated-measures analysis. Thus, it cannot be verified that outcome data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants at the later scheduled assessments.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No sensitivity analysis for missing SGA data is reported. No imputation approach, mixed-model specification, weighting approach, or analysis addressing attrition due to death or other post-randomization events is described.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  It could. Death, transfer to long-term hemodialysis, and transplantation are clinical events plausibly related to participants’ health and nutritional status. Therefore, whether later SGA data were missing could depend on the participants’ underlying or unobserved nutritional outcome.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  Death is directly indicative of severe health deterioration and makes subsequent SGA assessment impossible. In a study whose primary outcome is a clinical nutritional-status score, missingness after death is plausibly related to the participant’s true health and nutritional condition. Mortality was also numerically unequal between the groups (2/30 bicarbonate versus 5/30 placebo). The report supplies no analysis demonstrating that the primary SGA conclusion is robust to these missing data.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Although the investigators state that nutritional assessment continued after discontinuation of study medication, this does not resolve missing outcome data after death or clarify outcome availability after transfer to hemodialysis or transplantation. The extent and handling of missing SGA measurements are insufficiently reported, no sensitivity analyses are presented, and missingness is likely related to the participants’ health status and hence to the SGA outcome. This domain is therefore judged at **high risk of bias**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  The primary outcome was overall SGA score, assessed using a specified four-item, seven-point system. The items assessed were change in body weight, anorexia, subcutaneous tissue, and muscle mass. The study describes training of assessors before the trial and reports a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.84, indicating high agreement between the two observers. There is no indication that the stated SGA measurement method was unsuitable for measuring nutritional status.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  SGA was scheduled at the same time points in both groups: baseline, 12, 24, 36, and 52 weeks. The same measurement approach and trained observers were used for both groups. No group-specific assessment procedures are reported.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — No.**  
  The paper states that SGA was performed “*by two trained observers who were blinded from the treatment group allocation and biochemical results of the patients*.” This directly addresses assessor blinding.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary SGA outcome was measured at the same scheduled times using the same specified procedure in both groups. Outcome assessors were trained and blinded to treatment allocation and biochemical results. Therefore, despite SGA being a subjective assessment, its measurement in this trial is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The article reports the intended primary outcome (SGA score) and describes the planned repeated-measures ANOVA approach. However, the supplied material contains no dated protocol, trial-registry entry, or statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the primary outcome definition, assessment time points, covariate adjustment, and analysis method were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

- **5.2 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — Probably no.**  
  The primary outcome was explicitly identified as SGA score, and the report provides SGA results at each planned assessment time point (0, 12, 24, 36, and 52 weeks), as well as the longitudinal repeated-measures result. The report does not provide evidence that only selected SGA time points or a selected SGA instrument were reported. However, without a protocol or registry, it cannot be independently confirmed that there were no other eligible SGA-related measurements or time points.

- **5.3 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The paper reports repeated-measures ANOVA adjusted for Charlson Comorbidity Index, along with post hoc comparisons. No pre-specified analysis plan is available to establish that this adjustment approach, model, handling of missing data, or choice of post hoc comparisons was determined before unblinded data were available. The article does not itself provide evidence that the reported result was selected because it was favourable, but neither does it allow this possibility to be excluded.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The report identifies the primary outcome and reports results over the stated follow-up time points, which reduces concern that a single favourable SGA time point was selectively reported. However, no prospectively finalized protocol, registry record, or statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied material. Consequently, it cannot be verified that the reported repeated-measures analysis and its adjustment strategy were selected independently of the observed results. This domain is judged as raising **some concerns**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation for overall judgement:**  
The primary SGA result is judged:

- **Low risk** for bias arising from the randomization process;
- **Low risk** for bias due to deviations from intended interventions;
- **High risk** for bias due to missing outcome data;
- **Low risk** for bias in measurement of the outcome; and
- **Some concerns** for bias in selection of the reported result.

Under RoB 2, a result is at **high risk of bias** when it is judged at high risk in at least one domain. The high-risk judgement is driven by missing outcome data: deaths occurred in both groups, more deaths occurred in the placebo group, later SGA measurements after death were necessarily unavailable, and the report does not provide the number of participants contributing SGA data at each time point or analyses demonstrating robustness to plausible missing-data assumptions. The absence of a pre-specified publicly verifiable analysis plan additionally raises some concerns regarding selective reporting of the analysis.

**Final overall RoB 2 judgement for the reported effect on longitudinal overall SGA score: High risk of bias.**
