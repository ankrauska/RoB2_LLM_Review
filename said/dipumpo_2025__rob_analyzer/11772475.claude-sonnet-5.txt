# ROB 2 Assessment Report: Multicenter Randomized Evaluation of Methadone Medical Maintenance

**Preliminary Considerations:**
- Study design: Individually-randomized parallel-group trial (three arms: routine care, clinic-based MMM, office-based MMM)
- Outcome assessed for this evaluation: Rate of drug-positive urine specimens at 6 months (objective outcome); Treatment satisfaction (CSQ) is addressed where relevant
- Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat)
- Numerical result assessed: Urinalysis positive rates (Table 1) and CSQ satisfaction scores (Table 2)

---

## 1. Bias Arising from the Randomization Process

**Judgment: Some Concerns**

**Motivation:**
- The paper states patients were "randomized" and "stratified on the following variables: (1) any self-reported drug or alcohol use in the past 30-days; and (2) having negative urine drug screens for at least 2 years." No description is provided of the actual method used to generate the random allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number tables). This warrants a response of "No information" to the sequence generation signalling question.
- No information is provided regarding allocation concealment mechanisms (e.g., sequentially numbered envelopes, central randomization). It is unclear who performed the randomization and whether those enrolling participants could foresee assignments.
- Baseline characteristics were reported in aggregate (age, sex, education, income) but not by treatment group, precluding assessment of baseline imbalance across the three arms. Table 2 reveals a significant baseline difference in marijuana use between groups (Routine 16%, Clinic 21%, Office 0%), which is notable and unexplained—this raises some concern regarding whether randomization achieved balance, though this could plausibly be due to chance in a small sample (n=73).
- Given no information on sequence generation or allocation concealment, combined with an observed (though plausibly chance) baseline imbalance in a key variable (marijuana use), the domain-level judgment defaults to "Some Concerns" per the RoB 2 decision algorithm (Table 4: NI/NI/Y→Some concerns).

**Limitation/Ambiguity:** The manuscript does not describe the randomization sequence generation method or allocation concealment procedures. This is a specific gap in reporting that could not be resolved from the text provided.

## 2. Bias Due to Deviations from Intended Interventions

**Judgment: Some Concerns**

**Motivation:**
- **Blinding:** This trial was unblinded/open-label. Participants and treatment providers were clearly aware of intervention assignment because the interventions themselves (frequency of clinic visits, take-home medication amounts, location of care) constituted the study conditions and could not feasibly be masked. (Signalling Q2.1/2.2: Yes)
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** The paper explicitly reports a "stepped care" system whereby patients who had drug-positive urines or failed medication recalls were moved to intensified care, and later returned to their original assigned condition. This is a pre-specified protocol-consistent deviation and does NOT constitute an inappropriate deviation from intended intervention, as it was built into the trial design equally across all arms.
- However, because the study was unblinded, there is a possibility that trial context (e.g., participant awareness of being monitored differently, or clinician awareness of assignment) could have influenced behavior differentially between groups—e.g., knowledge that MMM group had reduced monitoring might affect drug-use behavior or reporting. The paper does not explicitly address whether such trial-context-driven deviations occurred or were balanced between groups.
- **Analysis:** The primary analyses use an intention-to-treat sample (n=73) for retention and urine positivity but subsequent self-report/ASI/CSQ analyses were restricted to the n=66 who completed the full 6 months, excluding 7 dropouts. This is not a full ITT analysis for the self-report outcomes; however, dropout numbers were low and balanced across groups (Wilcoxon-Gehan statistic=0.14, ns), so the potential impact appears limited but is not explicitly demonstrated to be negligible via sensitivity analysis.
- Given unblinded design with plausible but unconfirmed trial-context effects, and an incomplete (non-ITT, though justified) analysis for secondary outcomes, the judgment is "Some Concerns" rather than "Low" or "High."

**Limitation/Ambiguity:** The manuscript does not explicitly discuss whether awareness of group assignment led to behavioral changes unrelated to the actual intervention (e.g., differential reporting of drug use due to feeling monitored less), which is a specific ambiguity limiting definitive judgment.

## 3. Bias Due to Missing Outcome Data

**Judgment: Low Risk**

**Motivation:**
- Outcome data (urinalysis results) were available for the full intent-to-treat sample (73 patients) using averaged data across all available specimens for each person via ANOVA; retention data explicitly reported at 90%, 89%, and 92% across the three arms with no significant between-group difference (Wilcoxon-Gehan statistic=0.14, df=2, ns).
- Only 7/73 patients (~9.6%) dropped out over 6 months, and this small number was evenly distributed across groups with clearly documented, non-outcome-related reasons (dislike of recall frequency, transfer to non-participating clinic, self-reported inability to manage supply, unknown departure, decision to taper methadone). These reasons do not suggest that missingness was related to the true value of the outcome (drug use) in a way that would differentially bias the groups.
- The proportion of missing data is small enough, and reasons are sufficiently documented and unrelated to outcome, that missing outcome data are unlikely to bias the result.

## 4. Bias in Measurement of the Outcome

**Judgment: Some Concerns**

**Motivation:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** Urinalysis (EMIT, confirmed by TLC/FPIA) is an objective, validated, and appropriate method for measuring illicit drug use. This portion of the outcome measurement is not inappropriate. (Q4.1: No)
- **Comparability of measurement between groups:** This is where concern arises. The paper explicitly states: "Patients assigned to the routine treatment condition provided a urine specimen on a random monthly schedule... Patients assigned to the MMM conditions left one specimen at the time of the monthly counseling contact and medication pick-up (non-random collection) and another specimen at the time of the random monthly study participant medication recall." Additionally, "Specimens collected in the clinics were observed by program staff and temperature tested... Urine specimens collected in the physician's office were temperature tested only" (not observed). This represents a difference in outcome ascertainment procedure between groups (differential collection method/oversight based on setting), raising concern that detection of drug use could differ systematically between the office-based arm (less rigorous observation) and clinic-based/routine arms. (Q4.2: Yes/Probably yes)
- **Blinding of assessor:** For the objective urinalysis outcome, the laboratory assay itself is not subject to assessor bias (an objective, automated-type test), so once specimens are obtained, assessment is unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of group assignment.
- However, the differential specimen collection/observation procedures across settings constitutes a documented difference in outcome ascertainment methodology between intervention groups, which per RoB2 guidance (Q4.2 = Yes) is sufficient to raise the domain judgment, though this seems more likely to bias towards under-detection in the office-based arm (unobserved collection) rather than reflecting assessor-influenced judgment bias. Given the reduced oversight (non-observed collection) specifically in the office-based intervention arm, this is a specific and identifiable source of concern under this domain.
- For the CSQ satisfaction outcome (participant-reported), participants were clearly aware of their intervention assignment (unblinded), and self-reported satisfaction could plausibly be influenced by knowledge of receiving the preferred/less-intensive MMM condition, raising further concern for that outcome specifically.

**Limitation/Ambiguity:** The differential level of oversight in urine collection (observed in clinics vs. unobserved/only temperature-tested in physician's office) is an identified and reported procedural difference that could differentially affect detection of tampered specimens between arms, but the paper does not quantify or address the impact of this discrepancy.

## 5. Bias in Selection of the Reported Result

**Judgment: Some Concerns**

**Motivation:**
- No trial protocol, trial registry entry, or statistical analysis plan is referenced, cited, or available in the article/reference list to confirm that outcome measures and analyses were pre-specified before unblinded data were available. (Q5.1: No information)
- The paper reports multiple outcome measures (urinalysis, medication recall using two different failure definitions post-hoc ["incorrect amount" vs "no show"], ASI substance-use subscales, CSQ, monthly hours in treatment, new activities) without clear indication that all were pre-specified with defined primary/secondary status a priori. The post-hoc creation of two categories of medication recall failure ("incorrect amount" and "no show") appears to be a data-driven categorization introduced after the fact to explain/contextualize recall failures, raising some concern about outcome/analysis selection, though this seems presented as explanatory rather than as the primary inferential result.
- No evidence of multiple unreported analyses or selective suppression of unfavorable results was found; results are reported comprehensively across all stated measures, including non-significant results (e.g., NS retention, NS urinalysis differences), which somewhat mitigates concern.
- Given the complete absence of a registered protocol/SAP reference and the inability to confirm pre-specification, but no overt evidence of results-based selective suppression, the judgment defaults to "Some Concerns" (per Table 14: No information on pre-specification → Some concerns).

**Limitation/Ambiguity:** No trial registration number, protocol, or statistical analysis plan is cited in the article, making it impossible to verify whether outcome measures/analyses were finalized prior to unblinded data availability.

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall Judgment: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Motivation:**
Applying the RoB 2 algorithm for reaching an overall judgement:
- Domain 1 (Randomization): Some Concerns
- Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions): Some Concerns
- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data): Low Risk
- Domain 4 (Measurement of outcome): Some Concerns
- Domain 5 (Selection of reported result): Some Concerns

Per RoB 2 rules: "Some concerns for multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result" leads to an overall judgment of **High Risk of Bias**. In this case, four of the five domains (1, 2, 4, and 5) are rated "Some Concerns." This concentration of concerns—spanning the integrity of the randomization process, unblinded trial context effects, differential outcome ascertainment procedures between settings (particularly reduced oversight of urine collection in the office-based arm), and unverifiable pre-specification of analyses—cumulatively and substantially lowers confidence in the reported result (i.e., low rates of positive urinalysis and high treatment satisfaction with MMM). No single domain alone reached "High Risk," but the accumulation across four domains meets the explicit RoB 2 criterion for an overall "High Risk of Bias" judgment.

**Key reported limitations/ambiguities requiring transparency:**
1. No description of random sequence generation or allocation concealment method.
2. No group-specific baseline table provided, limiting assessment of randomization success (notable unexplained imbalance in marijuana use at baseline).
3. Differential urine specimen collection oversight between clinic settings (observed) and office setting (unobserved, temperature-tested only).
4. No cited trial registration, protocol, or statistical analysis plan to confirm pre-specification of outcomes/analyses.
5. Unblinded design with potential (unaddressed) trial-context-driven behavioral or reporting differences between study arms.
