# RoB 2 Assessment: FLAME Trial (Chollet et al., 2011)
**Fluoxetine for motor recovery after acute ischaemic stroke**

**Outcome assessed:** Change in Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale (FMMS) score from baseline to day 90 (primary outcome)
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)
**Comparison:** Fluoxetine 20 mg/day vs placebo (both + physiotherapy)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process
**Judgment: SOME CONCERNS**

- **Sequence generation (1.1 – Yes):** "Randomisation was balanced by centre, with an allocation sequence based on a block size of four, generated with a computer random-number generator by the pharmacist at the coordinating centre." This constitutes a genuine random component.
- **Allocation concealment (1.2 – Yes):** Concealment was achieved through "sequentially numbered opaque envelopes," prepared and dispensed by an independent pharmacist and hospital pharmacy department, separate from the enrolling investigators. Patients, carers, and outcome assessors were masked throughout.
- **Baseline imbalance (1.3 – Yes, concern raised):** The paper explicitly states: "mean age was slightly higher and previous history of stroke was more frequent in the fluoxetine group... FMMS score at inclusion was higher in the fluoxetine group than in the placebo group" (17.1 vs 13.4 on total FMMS; previous stroke 17% vs 7%). Because baseline FMMS is a key prognostic factor directly linked to the primary outcome (change in FMMS), and this imbalance favoured the group that ultimately reported the better outcome, it raises a signal of a possible problem with the randomization process (even though small block sizes stratified across nine centres can produce such imbalances by chance). Although the analysis adjusted statistically for these variables, per RoB 2 guidance such adjustment cannot fully rule out residual confounding from unmeasured prognostic imbalance, and the underlying concern is about the process itself, not the analysis.

**Motivation for judgment:** Sequence generation and concealment were adequate (low risk features), but the documented, clinically-relevant, baseline imbalance in the primary outcome-defining measure (FMMS) and in age/previous stroke — which the trialists themselves highlight — is sufficiently important that it cannot be dismissed simply as chance in a study of this size (n=59/59) with block randomization stratified by nine centres. This yields an overall "Some concerns" judgment for this domain, per the RoB 2 decision algorithm (random sequence = Yes, concealment = Yes, imbalance = Yes → Some concerns/possible High depending on magnitude).

**Limitation/ambiguity noted:** No formal statistical test (p-value) for the baseline imbalance in FMMS was reported, limiting definitive judgement of whether this exceeds chance-level imbalance.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)
**Judgment: LOW RISK**

- **2.1/2.2 (Awareness):** Participants, carers, and treating personnel were blinded ("double-blind" design with identical placebo capsules prepared by the pharmacist). Answer: No.
- **2.3–2.5 (Deviations due to trial context):** Because blinding of participants and personnel was maintained, deviations arising from awareness of allocation (differential co-interventions, contamination) are unlikely. Treatment compliance was almost identical between groups (mean cumulative dose 88.3 vs 87.4 tablets, p=0.722; relative dose intensity 94.8% vs 94.0%, p=0.854), indicating no meaningful differential deviation.
- **2.6 (Appropriate analysis):** The "full analysis set" (113/118) followed ITT principles — participants analysed in their randomised groups, only 5 participants excluded for missing outcome data (deaths/withdrawals), not for non-adherence. This is an acceptable modified-ITT approach.
- **2.7:** Not applicable, as the analysis approach was appropriate.

**Motivation:** Successful blinding, balanced and high adherence, and use of an ITT-consistent analysis for the effect of assignment to intervention collectively support a low risk of bias for this domain.

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data
**Judgment: LOW RISK**

- Of 118 randomized patients, 113 (95.8%) had primary outcome data available at day 90 (Figure 1). This is a small proportion of missing data (4.2%), unlikely to have a material impact on this outcome.
- Reasons for missing data were documented and were similar in both frequency and nature across groups: fluoxetine group — 1 death (respiratory distress from food inhalation), 1 withdrawal (severe hypoxia); placebo group — 1 death (septic shock), 2 withdrawals (kidney tumour, pulmonary embolism). These are largely unrelated to the underlying motor-recovery outcome and are balanced numerically between arms (2/59 vs 3/59).
- No differential pattern of dropout related to treatment allocation or to the outcome itself is apparent.

**Motivation:** The small overall proportion of missing data, similar between-group frequency, and non-outcome-related reasons for attrition indicate missingness could not plausibly bias the estimated treatment effect.

---

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome
**Judgment: LOW RISK**

- **4.1 (Appropriateness of measurement method):** FMMS is a validated, widely used scale with established inter-rater/intra-rater reliability. Method not inappropriate.
- **4.2 (Differential measurement/ascertainment):** All patients underwent standardized FMMS assessment by physiotherapists at baseline, day 30, and day 90 using the same procedure in both groups; ascertainment did not differ by group.
- **4.3 (Blinded assessors):** "Physicians assessing the outcome were masked to group assignment," and FMMS was assessed by physiotherapists — the report states outcome assessors and site staff were kept blinded throughout the study.
- **4.4/4.5 (Potential for unmasking to influence assessment):** FMMS is an observer-reported outcome involving some clinical judgement, so unmasking could theoretically influence scoring. However, digestive side effects of fluoxetine (nausea, diarrhoea) were relatively modest (25% vs 11%) and no formal unblinding assessment (e.g., guessing exercise) was reported, but there is no explicit evidence in the report that assessors became unblinded or that assessment was influenced by treatment guesses.

**Motivation:** Blinding of outcome assessors was explicitly stated and maintained through masked packaging/procedures; no evidence of differential ascertainment or unblinding-driven bias is present in the report.

**Limitation/ambiguity noted:** No formal test of the success of blinding (e.g., end-of-study guess of allocation) was reported, which is a minor limitation in verifying that blinding was fully preserved, particularly given the higher rate of GI side effects in the fluoxetine group.

---

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result
**Judgment: LOW RISK**

- The trial was prospectively registered (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00657163) prior to completion, with a clearly pre-specified primary outcome (Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale progression at 3 months) matching the reported primary outcome exactly.
- Pre-specified secondary outcomes (NIHSS, modified Rankin Scale, MADRS) were all reported in the results (Table 4), consistent with the registered outcome list.
- The statistical analysis plan (multiple linear regression adjusting for centre and baseline imbalances) is described transparently in the Methods, and the same pre-specified adjustment set (centre, age, history of stroke, and baseline FMMS score) was applied consistently across outcomes.
- No evidence of selective reporting from multiple candidate outcome measurements/scales or multiple undisclosed analytic approaches; sensitivity/post-hoc analyses (subgroup excluding thrombolysed patients; adjustment for clinical depression) are explicitly labelled "post-hoc" and reported alongside, not in place of, the primary analysis.

**Motivation:** Alignment between the registered protocol outcomes and the outcomes/analyses reported, together with transparent labelling of pre-specified versus post-hoc analyses, supports a low risk of bias in selection of the reported result.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement
**Overall Judgment: SOME CONCERNS**

**Rationale:** Domains 2 (deviations from intended interventions), 3 (missing outcome data), 4 (measurement of outcome), and 5 (selection of the reported result) are all judged at **low risk of bias**. However, Domain 1 (bias arising from the randomization process) is judged as **"Some concerns"** due to a baseline imbalance in a key prognostic factor (baseline FMMS score) and other prognostic variables (age, previous stroke) favouring the fluoxetine group, despite adequate methods of sequence generation and allocation concealment. Per the RoB 2 decision rules, a "Some concerns" rating in any single domain — without a "High risk" rating in any domain — mandates an overall judgement of **"Some concerns"** for this result.

**Key limitation explicitly flagged (as ambiguity, not assumption):** The trial report does not provide a formal statistical test of the observed baseline imbalance in FMMS score and other prognostic variables, nor does it report whether this imbalance arose by chance (plausible given the block randomization design and multiple centres) or reflects a problem with the randomization/allocation process. This ambiguity, combined with the direction of the imbalance favouring the group that showed the superior outcome, is the primary driver of the "Some concerns" rating and could not be resolved from the information provided in the article.
