## RoB 2 assessment

**Target result assessed:** six-month mammography screening measured from **electronic clinical records**, comparing each active intervention strategy with standard care (particularly, low-intensity intervention: 51.8% vs 6.0%; high-intensity intervention: 70.1% vs 6.0%).  
**Effect of interest:** effect of **assignment** to the intervention strategies (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Design:** individually randomized, three-arm parallel-group randomized clinical trial.  
**Sources available for this assessment:** the published article and its reported flow diagram. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan (SAP), or trial-registry record was provided.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article reports that the 500 enrolled women were allocated using “a stratified randomization method blocking by age (50–59; 60–70)” and a “computerized random generator program.” This is a random sequence-generation method.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Yes.**  
  After women had consented and completed the baseline survey, “a complete data base with 500 eligible women interviewed at baseline was sent to the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center in the US (i.e. the coordination center)” for random allocation. Allocation was therefore undertaken remotely by a coordination centre after enrolment and baseline assessment, rather than by the personnel recruiting women at the Chilean clinic. This provides adequate protection against foreknowledge of the allocation at enrolment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  The article states that “no significant differences were observed between groups in the demographic characteristics of the participants.” The report does not describe any substantial imbalance in group sizes: the allocated groups were 167, 166, and 167 women. There is no reported baseline imbalance indicating failure or manipulation of randomization.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
Sequence generation was computerized and stratified by age, allocation occurred through a remote coordination centre after women were enrolled, and no baseline findings suggest a problem in randomization. Therefore, the electronic-record mammography-screening result is at low risk of bias arising from the randomization process.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The article explicitly states: “Blinding participants to their intervention group was not possible in this study.” Participants receiving mailed materials, telephone contact, or a home visit necessarily knew that they had received these contacts.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The lay health educators delivering telephone and home-visit contacts necessarily knew participants’ intervention status. However, clinic staff and radiology-unit staff were reported to be blinded to allocation group.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The reported intervention delivery corresponds to the intended intervention strategy. All women in the low-intensity and high-intensity groups received the mailing. In the high-intensity group, telephone and/or home contact was delivered to women who had not arranged mammography after the mailing, as specified by the intervention protocol. This conditional personal contact was therefore part of the intended high-intensity strategy, not a deviation.  
  Opportunistic advice occurred in approximately 14%–15% of women and was reported to have a very similar distribution across groups. The study does not report differential non-protocol co-interventions arising from knowledge of allocation.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The study explicitly states that an “intention to treat analysis was conducted using electronic clinical records,” that the records “were reviewed for all women,” and that compliance was estimated using “the baseline denominators of each group.” Thus, all 500 randomized women were retained in the denominator and analysed according to assignment.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
Although participants and interventionists were not blinded, the intervention was inherently visible and was delivered as specified. The reported differences in contact intensity were intentional components of the randomized strategies, not deviations. Opportunistic advice was similarly distributed between groups. Most importantly, the primary electronic-record result was analysed according to the randomized groups using baseline denominators for all randomized women. There is no reported evidence of trial-context deviations likely to have biased the intention-to-treat result.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — Not applicable.**

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  Mammography completion was measured using two electronic sources: the database of mammograms conducted at the radiology unit affiliated with the clinic and the electronic clinical chart where test results were registered. These are appropriate sources for determining whether a mammogram was performed within the participating healthcare system.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The same electronic data sources were used to assess mammography completion for women in all three randomized groups. The article reports that clinic staff and radiology-unit staff were blinded to allocation group. No group-specific outcome ascertainment procedure is reported.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — No.**  
  The article states that the analysis was conducted by investigators at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center who “were blinded to the group assignment of participants.” It also states that clinic and radiology staff were blinded to allocation. Furthermore, the outcome was recorded in electronic records rather than being a subjective participant-reported rating.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable / probably no.**  
  Completion of a mammogram documented in electronic records is an objectively ascertainable event. The outcome assessment does not require a subjective judgment about whether screening occurred.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The outcome was measured using electronic clinical records for all groups, with the relevant staff and data analysts reported as blinded to group allocation. Mammography completion is an objective event. There is no evidence that its measurement or ascertainment differed by intervention group or was vulnerable to subjective assessment influenced by knowledge of assignment.
