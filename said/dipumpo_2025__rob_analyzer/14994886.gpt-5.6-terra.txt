## RoB 2 assessment

**Study design:** Individually randomized parallel-group trial.  
**Comparison:** 0.014-inch versus 0.016-inch initial NiTi orthodontic archwire.  
**Result assessed:** Presence of pain **1 day (24 hours)** after wire insertion: 51/56 (91.0%) in the 0.014-inch group versus 48/53 (90.5%) in the 0.016-inch group.  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to the initial archwire size (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Sources used:** The uploaded article and its tables/figures only. No protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial registry entry, or additional trial report was available in the supplied material.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The article states that “the patients were randomly selected for insertion of the 0.014 or 0.016 inch wires.” However, it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated. There is no report of computer-generated random numbers, random-number tables, coin tossing, randomly permuted blocks, or another method containing a documented random component. Under RoB 2, a statement that a study was randomized without the method is insufficient to establish random sequence generation.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  No information is reported on allocation concealment. The article does not describe central randomization, pharmacy-controlled allocation, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another process preventing recruiters or clinicians from foreseeing the next assignment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  Group sizes were similar (56 versus 53 participants). The reported sex distribution was broadly similar (29 boys/27 girls in the 0.014-inch group; 23 boys/30 girls in the 0.016-inch group), as was the distribution of wire placement in both arches versus the maxilla only. These data do not show a clear imbalance incompatible with chance. However, relevant clinical baseline characteristics were incompletely reported: specifically, “dental crowding was not evaluated.” This limitation does not itself demonstrate a failure of randomization.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The report labels the allocation as random but does not provide the sequence-generation method or any allocation-concealment method. Lack of information on concealment leads to **some concerns** under RoB 2 when the available baseline data do not themselves suggest a randomization failure.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — No information.**  
  The report does not state whether participants were blinded to wire size. It also does not report whether participants could distinguish the 0.014-inch from the 0.016-inch wire.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The orthodontic personnel inserting and ligating the archwires would necessarily handle the wire selected for each participant. No blinding of the treating personnel is reported.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The protocol as reported indicates that the assigned wire was inserted and ligated to all teeth. It also reports that no extra-oral appliances, palatal arches, or quad-helix appliances were used during the experimental period. Participants were allowed to use pain relief “when they felt it necessary,” which was an explicitly permitted aspect of the reported study procedures rather than a reported deviation arising from the trial context. No switching between wire groups, failure to insert the allocated wire, protocol-violating co-interventions, or differential implementation of the archwires was reported.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  For the assessed binary outcome at 1 day, the denominators correspond to all randomized participants: 51/56 participants in the 0.014-inch group and 48/53 in the 0.016-inch group reported pain. There is no indication that participants were analysed according to a wire other than that assigned, or that eligible participants with available outcome data were excluded from this binary analysis.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Although treating personnel were aware of the wire inserted, the article does not report deviations caused by the trial context that could have affected the 24-hour pain comparison. The reported 24-hour pain data include all randomized participants in their assigned groups. Therefore, for the effect of assignment to wire size, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Yes.**  
  The article states that questionnaires “were completed by all 109 patients.” For pain at 1 day, the table reports 51 participants with pain in the 0.014-inch group and 48 with pain in the 0.016-inch group; with the randomized group sizes of 56 and 53, respectively, these figures account for all 109 randomized participants.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Outcome data for the assessed binary endpoint—presence of pain 1 day after insertion—were available for all randomized participants. There is no reported loss to follow-up, missing questionnaire, missing 24-hour pain status, or imputation. The domain is therefore at **low risk of bias**.

**Important scope limitation:**  
This judgement applies to the binary 24-hour pain result. The article’s VAS intensity values were obtained only from participants who answered “yes” to having pain. Consequently, the VAS results represent pain intensity among respondents reporting pain, rather than a pain-intensity outcome measured in all randomized participants. That feature does not alter the missing-data judgement for the binary pain outcome assessed here.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  The binary outcome was measured by a patient-completed questionnaire asking whether pain was present. A questionnaire completed at the designated time point is not inherently inappropriate for determining whether the participant reports pain. The article describes the use of a questionnaire and gives the question format.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  Both groups received the same questionnaire after archwire insertion, and pain was assessed at the same stated time points. The article provides no evidence that outcome ascertainment procedures differed by wire group.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — No information.**  
  For a participant-reported pain outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor. The article does not report participant blinding, whether participants were told their allocated wire size, or whether they could identify the size of wire received.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.**  
  Pain is a subjective, participant-reported outcome. If participants knew or inferred their assigned wire size, their expectations could potentially affect whether they reported pain.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  The article contains no evidence that participants knew the wire size, were informed of differential expected pain, or held strong expectations favouring either wire size. It also contains no evidence of differential outcome questioning or encouragement by treating personnel. Nevertheless, the absence of reported participant blinding leaves residual uncertainty.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The pain outcome was subjective and participant-reported. The report does not establish that participants were unaware of their allocated wire size. Since knowledge of the intervention could influence self-reported pain, but there is no direct evidence that it did, this domain raises **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No trial protocol, registration record, statistical analysis plan, or date-stamped pre-specified analysis plan is provided in the article or in the supplied materials.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  Pain was measured at multiple eligible time points: 6 hours and days 1 through 7. The report presents results across these time points, but without a prospectively specified primary time point it cannot be determined whether the 24-hour result was selected from multiple time points on the basis of the findings.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The paper reports several pain-related outcome approaches: time of onset, presence of pain, VAS pain intensity, anterior versus posterior pain, pain-relief use, and impact on daily life. It also uses several statistical methods. No pre-specified analysis plan establishes which outcome definition, time point, analysis population, or analytic approach was intended as primary. There is no direct evidence that the reported 24-hour binary pain result was selected because of its direction, magnitude, or statistical significance; however, selection cannot be excluded.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The report provides no prospective analysis plan and includes repeated pain measurements across eight time points and multiple related outcomes and analyses. In the absence of pre-specified analytical intentions, the risk of selective selection of the reported result cannot be ruled out. There is no direct evidence sufficient for a high-risk judgement. Therefore, the judgement is **some concerns**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**  
The study is at **low risk of bias** for deviations from intended interventions and missing outcome data for the assessed 24-hour binary pain endpoint. However, it has **some concerns** in three domains:

1. **Randomization process:** allocation concealment was not reported, and the method used to generate the allocation sequence was not described.  
2. **Measurement of outcome:** pain was participant-reported and subjective, while participant blinding or lack of knowledge of wire size was not established.  
3. **Selection of the reported result:** no pre-specified protocol or analysis plan was available, despite multiple outcome time points and multiple pain-related analyses.

Under the specified RoB 2 overall rules, **some concerns in multiple domains substantially lower confidence in the result**. The overall judgement for the comparison of 0.014-inch versus 0.016-inch wires on presence of pain at 24 hours is therefore **high risk of bias**.
