## RoB 2 assessment: FLAME trial (Chollet et al., 2011)

**Study and result assessed:** Individually randomized, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial comparing **fluoxetine 20 mg daily plus usual physiotherapy** with **placebo plus usual physiotherapy** for 90 days in patients with acute ischaemic stroke.  

**Outcome/result assessed:** Primary outcome—change in **Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale (FMMS)** score from baseline (day 0) to day 90. The reported adjusted mean improvement was 34.0 points (95% CI 29.7 to 38.4) with fluoxetine and 24.3 points (19.9 to 28.7) with placebo; *p*=0.003.  

**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to fluoxetine versus placebo (intention-to-treat effect).

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The report states that randomisation was “based on a block size of four, generated with a computer random-number generator by the pharmacist at the coordinating centre.” This is an explicitly random sequence-generation method.

- **1.2 Was allocation concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  The article states that allocation “was concealed by use of sequentially numbered opaque envelopes.” The pharmacist at the coordinating centre generated the sequence and assigned participants according to the randomisation list. Although the report does not explicitly state that envelopes were sealed, tamper-proof, or opened only after irreversible enrolment, the described system—centrally managed pharmacist allocation with sequentially numbered opaque envelopes—provides sufficient assurance that allocation was probably concealed.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  The groups had the intended equal allocation (59 participants in each group). The article reports that groups were generally balanced for demographic factors, vascular risk factors, stroke characteristics, stroke severity, thrombolysis, and baseline NIHSS, mRS, and MADRS scores. There were numerical differences in age, previous stroke history, and baseline FMMS score, and these variables were included in adjusted analyses. However, the reported baseline data do not demonstrate an imbalance of sufficient magnitude, pattern, or statistical extremity to indicate failure or manipulation of randomisation. The equal group sizes also do not suggest a problem with blocked allocation.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Random sequence generation was explicitly computer-generated. Allocation concealment was described using sequentially numbered opaque envelopes administered by the coordinating-centre pharmacist. Baseline data do not show evidence that the randomisation process failed. Therefore, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

---

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  Fluoxetine and placebo capsules were described as identical in appearance and packaging. The report states that patients were masked to treatment assignment.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  The article states that “patients, carers, all site study staff, and investigators were masked to treatment assignment.” Thus, personnel involved in delivering care and study procedures were reported to be blinded.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  No evidence is reported that blinding failure, differential co-interventions, differential rehabilitation, or trial-context-related departures from the allocated strategy occurred. All participants received physiotherapy and standard inpatient stroke care. Rehabilitation procedures could differ between centres, but this was the planned pragmatic approach for both treatment groups within centres; it was not reported as a differential post-randomisation deviation resulting from knowledge of allocation. Centre was included in adjusted analyses.

  The protocol allowed open-label fluoxetine when clinical depression occurred, while the blinded study treatment continued. This was explicitly part of the trial treatment strategy rather than an unplanned deviation from the protocol. The article also reports that analyses adjusted for clinical depression before day 90, with the primary conclusion unchanged.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The primary analysis used the “full-analysis set,” including 113 of 118 randomized participants: 57/59 in the fluoxetine group and 56/59 in the placebo group. Participants were analysed according to their randomized group. The five exclusions resulted from two deaths and three withdrawals with unavailable day-90 data, rather than reassignment according to treatment received or exclusion for non-adherence. For this domain, such a modified intention-to-treat approach is appropriate; potential bias caused by unavailable outcomes is assessed separately under Domain 3.

- **Adherence and implementation:**  
  Adherence was similar between groups: mean relative dose intensity was 94.8% in the fluoxetine group and 94.0% in the placebo group. There is no reported evidence of material unequal non-adherence or implementation failure that would compromise the assigned-treatment comparison.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Participants, carers, study staff, and investigators were masked, the placebo was indistinguishable from fluoxetine, adherence was high and comparable, and the analysis retained participants in their randomized groups when outcome data were available. The report provides no evidence of trial-context-related deviations likely to affect the primary FMMS outcome. Therefore, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably yes.**  
  Primary-outcome data at day 90 were available for 113 of 118 randomized participants (95.8%): 57/59 (96.6%) in the fluoxetine group and 56/59 (94.9%) in the placebo group. Thus, only five participants (4.2%) lacked the day-90 primary outcome.

  The missing primary-outcome data were due to:
  - Fluoxetine: one death from respiratory distress after aspiration of food and one withdrawal because of severe hypoxia.
  - Placebo: one death from septic shock and two withdrawals, one due to kidney tumour and one due to pulmonary embolism.

  The amount of missing data is small for a continuous outcome. Although the reasons indicate serious clinical events, the missingness was limited and was similar in magnitude between groups (2/59 versus 3/59). The report does not indicate that the observed treatment effect would plausibly be materially altered by the outcomes of these five participants.

- **3.2–3.4 — Not required for the judgement.**  
  Because outcome data were available for nearly all randomized participants, the RoB 2 algorithm supports a low-risk judgement without requiring further signalling questions.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary FMMS outcome was available for more than 95% of randomized patients, with only five missing observations and broadly similar levels of missingness in the two groups. The small quantity of missing data is unlikely, whatever the unobserved values, to make an important difference to the estimated difference in FMMS improvement. This domain is therefore judged at **low risk of bias**.

---

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  The FMMS is a structured motor assessment scale ranging from 0 to 100 and was the prespecified primary outcome. The investigators state that it is widely used in stroke motor-recovery assessment and has excellent intra-rater and inter-rater reliability and validity. There is no evidence that the instrument was unsuitable for measuring motor impairment in this population.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  The FMMS was assessed at the same scheduled time points—baseline, day 30, and day 90—in both groups. There is no report of differing measurement methods, thresholds, assessors, visit schedules, or ascertainment procedures between intervention groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — No.**  
  The report states that participants, carers, all site study staff, and investigators were masked to treatment assignment. It also states that motor assessments were undertaken by physiotherapists. These assessors were site study staff; the reported masking procedures therefore apply to them. The summary also specifically states that physicians assessing outcomes were masked.

- **4.4–4.5 — Not applicable.**  
  As outcome assessors were masked, concern that knowledge of treatment assignment influenced FMMS assessment does not arise.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary outcome was measured using an established and appropriate motor-function scale at equivalent time points in both randomized groups. Outcome assessment was reported to be blinded. Accordingly, there is no evidence of differential outcome measurement or assessor-influenced measurement bias. This domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The ClinicalTrials.gov record identifies progression in the FMMS at 3 months as the primary outcome, which is consistent with the article’s primary outcome of change in FMMS from baseline to day 90. However, the registry was first posted in April 2008, whereas recruitment began in March 2005. The available registry record does not provide a detailed, date-stamped statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. No separate protocol document or statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied material.

- **5.2 Was the reported result likely selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — Probably no.**  
  The primary outcome in the article was explicitly defined as FMMS change between baseline and day 90. The registry also identifies FMMS progression at 3 months as the primary outcome. Although FMMS was additionally measured at day 30 and the registry mentions an FMMS assessment at 12 months, the day-90 result was the specified primary endpoint in the article and corresponds to the registered 3-month primary outcome. There is no direct evidence that the reported day-90 FMMS result was selected because of its magnitude, direction, or statistical significance.

- **5.3 Was the reported result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The primary result was reported from an adjusted linear regression analysis controlling for centre, age, previous stroke, and baseline FMMS score. The article also reports unadjusted FMMS results and several sensitivity/subgroup analyses. However, without a contemporaneous detailed analysis plan, it cannot be verified whether the covariate-adjusted model, choice of covariates, use of change scores, and reporting of the selected adjusted analysis were all specified before unblinded data were available. There is no direct evidence that the reported analysis was selected on the basis of the observed result, but the absence of a sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan leaves unresolved concern.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary outcome and time point are consistent between the article and registry. However, a detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before access to unblinded outcome data is not available in the supplied sources. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that the particular adjusted analysis reported for the primary FMMS result was selected independently of the observed findings. Under RoB 2, this results in **some concerns**, rather than high risk, because there is no affirmative evidence of selective reporting.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**  
Domains 1 through 4 are judged at **low risk of bias**: the sequence was computer-generated; allocation concealment was probably adequate; placebo masking was implemented for participants, carers, site staff, and investigators; adherence was high and similar; primary-outcome completeness was high; and outcome measurement was standardized and blinded.

Domain 5 raises **some concerns** because the supplied records do not include a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan to verify that the reported adjusted primary-outcome analysis was specified before unblinded outcome data became available. There is no evidence of selective reporting, but the available documentation does not eliminate that possibility.

Following the specified RoB 2 rule, because at least one domain has **some concerns** and no domain is at high risk of bias, the overall risk of bias for the reported primary FMMS change at day 90 is **Some concerns**.
