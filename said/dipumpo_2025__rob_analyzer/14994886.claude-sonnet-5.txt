# ROB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Perception of pain during orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances (comparison of 0.014 inch vs 0.016 inch initial archwires)

**Outcome assessed:** Perception/intensity of pain (VAS scores) and consumption of pain relief following archwire insertion, over 7-day follow-up

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (comparison of two initial wire sizes)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- The article states "Insertion of either a 0.014 or 0.016 inch wire was by random selection" and "The patients were randomly selected for insertion of the 0.014 or 0.016 inch wires." No further detail is provided on the method used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number tables, coin toss, etc.). This is insufficient to confirm a truly random component was used (signalling question 1.1: No information).
- There is no description whatsoever of an allocation concealment mechanism (e.g., sealed envelopes, central randomization). It is not reported who performed the allocation or whether personnel enrolling participants could foresee assignments (signalling question 1.2: No information).
- Regarding baseline imbalance: the two groups appear reasonably similar in size (56 vs 53) and gender distribution (29M/27F vs 23M/30F), and mean ages are not reported separately by wire group (only pooled by gender), so no clear evidence of imbalance is available, but this also limits ability to fully rule out chance imbalance in prognostic factors (e.g., baseline crowding, which was explicitly not evaluated/measured).
- According to the RoB 2 algorithm, when there is no information on sequence randomization AND no information on allocation concealment, but no evidence of baseline imbalance problem, the default judgment is "Some concerns."

**Limitation to report:** The paper does not describe the specific method of random sequence generation nor any allocation concealment procedure, limiting a definitive "Low risk" judgment.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- **Blinding of participants:** Not possible/not addressed — participants were aware of which wire size was placed conceptually, though in practice patients may not distinguish between 0.014 and 0.016 inch NiTi wires visually. However, the paper provides no information on whether participants, clinicians, or those completing questionnaires were blinded to wire assignment (signalling question 2.1/2.2: No information leaning towards "Probably Yes" awareness by clinicians/operators, since blinding of the treating orthodontist inserting the wire is essentially impossible - the operator inserting the wire necessarily knows the wire size).
- Given that care providers (orthodontists inserting the wires and ligating them) were certainly aware of the intervention assigned (deterministically identifiable by wire diameter), and given the subjective, self-reported nature of the outcome (VAS pain scores, self-medication decisions), there was clear potential for deviations from intended intervention arising from the trial context — e.g., differential encouragement, reassurance, or differential threshold for recommending analgesic consumption between groups.
- No information is provided regarding whether any non-protocol interventions (e.g., additional adjustments, orthodontist reassurance, differential analgesic recommendations) were balanced between groups.
- Critically, no explicit statement of an intention-to-treat analysis is provided. All 109 patients who received questionnaires apparently returned them, suggesting full analysis, but the appropriateness of the specific analytic approach for the assignment effect is not explicitly discussed.
- Because the outcome (pain perception, self-medication) is a participant-reported outcome highly susceptible to influence by trial context, and there is no evidence of blinding or control for co-interventions, and no information allowing exclusion of a meaningful influence of deviations on the outcome, this domain is judged as **High risk**.

**Limitation to report:** The article provides no information on blinding of care providers/patients, nor on whether any non-protocol interventions (e.g., reassurance, additional advice on analgesic use) were balanced between the two wire-size groups. This ambiguity, combined with the inherently subjective/self-reported nature of the outcome and lack of blinding safeguards, drives the high-risk judgment.

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- The manuscript states: "The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned at the following appointment." This indicates complete outcome data were available for all randomized participants (109/109) for the primary outcome measures (pain perception, VAS scores, self-medication, effect on daily life) at each time point up to and including day 7.
- No dropouts or losses to follow-up are reported.
- Signalling question 3.1 ("Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?") can be answered "Yes."
- Per the RoB 2 algorithm, when data are available for all or nearly all participants, the default judgment is Low risk regardless of subsequent questions.

**Limitation to report:** None significant; reporting suggests complete follow-up data was obtained.

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- The primary outcome (pain intensity via VAS, and yes/no pain perception) is a **participant-reported outcome**. Per RoB 2 guidance, the outcome assessor for such outcomes is the participant themselves.
- Signalling question 4.3: Were outcome assessors (patients) aware of the intervention received? Yes — patients could reasonably distinguish or at minimum knew which arm/wire combination was being used to some extent, and even if not overtly aware of "0.014 vs 0.016," this is not the primary concern; the key concern is that self-report of pain is inherently susceptible to reporting bias, and there is no blinding of the clinician administering/explaining the questionnaire, nor blinding of the patient to which type of treatment (in the context of study participation) they are undergoing.
- Signalling question 4.4: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention received? Yes — participant-reported pain scores are inherently susceptible to influence by expectation, especially since the study's own hypothesis (larger wire = more pain) could have been communicated or anticipated by patients/clinicians during the consent/enrollment process.
- Signalling question 4.5: Is it likely such influence occurred? There is no information confirming that patients were blinded to wire size (unlikely feasible in a clinical setting where the treating orthodontist and possibly patients could visually distinguish or infer wire gauge), and no description of any efforts to standardize the delivery of instructions or minimize expectation bias between groups.
- Given participant-reported outcomes with no blinding and plausible influence from trial context (patients aware of type of study, and clinicians certainly aware of wire assignment and potentially transmitting cues), this domain is judged **High risk**.

**Limitation to report:** No blinding of outcome assessors (patients themselves, and by extension clinicians administering/collecting the questionnaire) is described or apparently feasible given the nature of the intervention (different diameter wires), raising high risk of differential reporting bias for this inherently subjective, self-reported outcome.

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- There is no trial registration, published protocol, or statistical analysis plan referenced or available for this study. It is therefore not possible to verify whether the reported outcomes/analyses (i.e., specific time points, specific comparisons, specific statistical tests) were pre-specified before unblinded data were available (signalling question 5.1: No information).
- The study reports a very large number of comparisons/time points (initial pain, 6 hours, days 1–7, anterior/posterior separately, daily-life effects, correlation analyses) — a total of 49 questions analyzed across multiple time points and domains. While full results across nearly all time points/subdomains appear to be reported (which reduces the specific concern of selective outcome reporting within the presented data), the absence of a pre-registered protocol means we cannot rule out that additional analyses were conducted, or that particular framings/cutoffs were chosen based on the results (signalling questions 5.2/5.3: No information).
- The reporting appears comprehensive and consistent across all time points and subdomains presented (i.e., not obviously selective, as almost all data across all time points/measures are shown in the tables), which argues against a "High risk" judgment.
- Given the lack of information about pre-specified analysis plans, but no overt evidence of selective reporting from multiple candidate outcomes/analyses within the presented data, the appropriate judgment defaults to **Some concerns**.

**Limitation to report:** No trial registration or protocol was identified/referenced, preventing confirmation of pre-specified analyses. This is a reporting ambiguity rather than definitive evidence of selective reporting.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Rationale (per RoB 2 rules):**
- Domain 1 (Randomization process): Some concerns
- Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions): **High risk**
- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data): Low risk
- Domain 4 (Measurement of the outcome): **High risk**
- Domain 5 (Selection of the reported result): Some concerns

Per the RoB 2 decision rules, a judgment of **High risk of bias** in any single domain is sufficient to classify the overall result as being at **High risk of bias**. In this study, two domains (Deviations from intended interventions, and Measurement of the outcome) were independently judged as High risk, driven principally by:
1. The unblindable nature of the intervention (wire size is identifiable by treating clinicians and potentially patients), combined with a complete absence of reported blinding procedures or safeguards against co-intervention imbalance.
2. The fully participant-reported nature of the primary outcome (pain, VAS scores, self-medication), which is highly susceptible to expectation and reporting bias in the absence of any blinding.

Combined with "Some concerns" in randomization process and selection of the reported result (both driven by absence of methodological detail/protocol rather than confirmed problems), the cumulative effect substantially lowers confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported result. Therefore, the overall risk-of-bias judgment for this outcome is **High risk of bias**.

**Note on transparency:** Several judgments in this assessment (particularly in Domains 1 and 5) rely on the "No information" response category, reflecting genuine reporting gaps in the published article rather than confirmed methodological flaws. These ambiguities are reported here as specific limitations rather than assumptions, in accordance with the requirement to base judgments strictly on what is documented in the article.
