## Scope of the RoB 2 assessment

- **Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial.  
- **Interventions:** Bright light (BRIGHT) and monochromatic blue light (BLUE), each compared with the control light condition (CONTROL).  
- **Outcomes assessed:** Simple acoustic reaction time (milliseconds) and maximum handgrip strength (kg), measured immediately after the 60-minute light exposure.  
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to the randomized light condition (intention-to-treat effect).  
- **Sources reviewed:** The article and the supplied ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT02203539). No full protocol document or dated statistical analysis plan was supplied.

The domain judgements below apply to the reported post-exposure adjusted comparisons for both reaction time and handgrip strength. No outcome-specific difference in the assessment was identified.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states that participants were allocated using an online tool “which applies permuted block randomization with randomly varying block sizes of 3, 6, and 9.” This is a random sequence-generation method.

- **1.2 Was allocation concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  The allocation list was “transmitted by a non-involved person using serially numbered sealed opaque envelopes.” Serial numbering, sealing, opacity, and preparation/transmission by a person not involved in the study support allocation concealment. The report does not explicitly state that envelopes were opened sequentially only after irreversible enrolment, or that they were tamper-proof. This limits certainty, but the reported procedure is sufficiently consistent with concealed allocation to judge it probably adequate.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between groups suggest a problem with randomization? — No information.**  
  The supplied article text refers to baseline characteristics in Table 1 but does not reproduce the full table. Therefore, the available material does not permit a complete assessment of baseline comparability for all relevant prognostic variables. However, the article does not report a substantive baseline imbalance or any other indication that the randomization process was compromised.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Random sequence generation was explicitly random, and allocation was managed by a non-involved person through serially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. No reported evidence indicates manipulation or failure of randomization. The unavailable full baseline table is an assessment limitation, but, under RoB 2, no information about baseline imbalances does not preclude a low-risk judgement when allocation concealment is adequate and the sequence is random.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Participants “only knew the light condition they were exposed to, but not the other two light conditions.” In practice, the conditions were visibly distinguishable: approximately 4400 lx intended bright light, approximately 230 lx monochromatic blue light, and approximately 230 lx control room light. Thus, participants were aware of the condition they received.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  The test supervisor was reported to be blinded to the light condition. The report does not identify another unblinded person who delivered the intervention or performed the outcome testing.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The study documented substantial variation in actual light exposure reaching participants’ eyes and overlap in measured intensities between conditions. The authors attribute this to “different directions of gaze and sitting postures.” This is a limitation in implementation fidelity, but the report does not establish that it arose because of the trial context, recruitment procedures, or unblinded personnel acting differently between randomized groups. There is also no report of differential co-interventions or protocol-inconsistent interventions attributable to knowledge of assigned treatment.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably no.**  
  Seventy-four participants were randomized, but two were excluded retrospectively, one from BRIGHT and one from CONTROL, because of “invalid measurements of peak oxygen uptake” in a pre-randomization fitness test. The reported ANCOVA analyses therefore included 72 rather than all 74 randomized participants. The report does not demonstrate that these exclusions were restricted to participants confirmed to be ineligible before randomization; it instead describes them as retrospective exclusions. Thus, the reported analysis does not fully follow the intention-to-treat principle.

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of failure to analyse participants in their randomized groups? — Probably no.**  
  Only two of 74 randomized participants were excluded (2.7%), with one exclusion in BRIGHT and one in CONTROL. For the continuous outcomes assessed, this small and balanced exclusion is not sufficient, on the information reported, to indicate a likely substantial impact on the estimated group effects.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The intervention effect of interest is the effect of being assigned to a light condition. Although participants were aware of their condition, there is no reported evidence that awareness caused trial-context-related deviations likely to affect reaction time or handgrip strength. However, two randomized participants were retrospectively excluded from the analyses. Since the basis for these exclusions is not clearly established as confirmed post-randomization ineligibility, the analysis was not a full intention-to-treat analysis. The very small, balanced number excluded means this does not reach high risk of bias, but it raises **some concerns**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably yes.**  
  The results report post-exposure reaction time and handgrip-strength analyses for 72 of 74 randomized participants. The two non-analysed participants were excluded because of invalid peak oxygen-uptake measurements from the eligibility assessment, rather than because the reported outcomes were missing. The report does not describe missing reaction-time or handgrip-strength data among the 72 analysed participants.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
For each continuous outcome, data were available for 72/74 randomized participants (97.3%). This exceeds the approximate 95% level that RoB 2 identifies as often sufficient for continuous outcomes, provided the missing observations could not materially alter the effect estimate. The two exclusions are addressed principally under Domain 2 because they concern post-randomization exclusion from analysis rather than documented missing outcome measurement. No differential outcome-data loss, outcome-related dropout, or missing-data mechanism likely related to the true outcome was reported.

**Limitation:**  
The article does not explicitly state whether reaction-time and handgrip data had been collected from the two retrospectively excluded participants. That ambiguity does not change the judgement because the number concerned is very small; however, it prevents confirmation that outcome data were literally available for every randomized participant.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  Reaction time was measured using a computer-based simple acoustic reaction-time task, with the median of valid responses calculated for each participant. Handgrip strength was measured using a hydraulic hand dynamometer, with the mean of three trials calculated. The article provides operational details for both assessments and does not report evidence that either instrument or measurement procedure was invalid for its intended outcome.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  Reaction-time and handgrip tests were performed under the same reported room-light conditions (approximately 230 lx) before and immediately after light exposure. The same stated task procedures, timing, and equipment were used across groups. There is no indication that one randomized group had additional outcome-assessment opportunities or a different measurement threshold.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  The reaction-time outcome was recorded by a computer task, and handgrip strength was recorded by a dynamometer. These measurement devices do not make judgements based on intervention assignment. In addition, the test supervisor was stated to be blinded to the assigned light condition.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — No.**  
  The recorded measures were instrument-derived performance measurements rather than assessor-judged clinical outcomes. The computer records reaction latency and the dynamometer records grip force. No unblinded outcome assessor was reported to classify, interpret, or adjudicate outcomes differently between groups.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The outcomes were assessed with the same objective, instrument-based procedures in all groups, and the supervisor conducting testing was blinded to condition. Participants’ awareness of light exposure does not itself establish bias in *measurement* of an automatically recorded reaction time or dynamometer reading. It could be relevant to behavioural effects of assignment, but no evidence of trial-context-related deviations was reported; that issue was considered in Domain 2. Therefore, the risk of bias in outcome measurement is low.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The article states: “All statistical methods used to compare the groups for reaction time and handgrip strength in the present report were pre-specified and registered.” However, the supplied trial-registration record specifies reaction time and maximum handgrip strength as secondary outcomes only. It does not specify the assessment time point, handling of the two retrospective exclusions, ANCOVA model, covariates, planned pairwise group comparisons, or the additional regression analyses based on actual light intensity. No dated full protocol or statistical analysis plan was supplied. Therefore, the claim of pre-specification cannot be verified in sufficient detail.

- **5.2 Was the reported result likely selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The study assessed reaction time and handgrip strength both before and after exposure. The registry does not specify which time point, summary measure, or outcome metric was intended for the formal between-group comparison. The article reports baseline and post-exposure data and uses baseline-adjusted post-exposure ANCOVA, but the available pre-specified documentation is not sufficiently detailed to exclude selection among potentially eligible outcome measurements or time points.

- **5.3 Was the reported result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  More than one analytic approach was available and reported: the primary group-based ANCOVA comparisons and additional unadjusted and baseline-adjusted linear regressions using actual light intensity. The registry does not set out a sufficiently detailed analysis plan to establish that the reported ANCOVA result, covariate adjustment, exclusion handling, and chosen contrasts were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. There is no direct evidence that results were selected because of their magnitude, direction, or statistical significance.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The study registry confirms that reaction time and maximum handgrip strength were specified as secondary outcomes. However, it does not provide enough detail to verify the pre-specified measurement time point or analysis plan, and no dated statistical analysis plan was available. The article’s statement that the analyses were pre-specified cannot by itself establish that the plan was finalized before access to unblinded data. There is no evidence of selective reporting sufficient for a high-risk judgement, but the absence of verifiable detailed prospective analysis intentions results in **some concerns**.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:**  
- Domain 1 (randomization process): **Low risk of bias**.  
- Domain 2 (deviations from intended interventions): **Some concerns**, because two of 74 randomized participants were retrospectively excluded and the reported analyses were not demonstrably full intention-to-treat analyses.  
- Domain 3 (missing outcome data): **Low risk of bias**.  
- Domain 4 (measurement of outcome): **Low risk of bias**.  
- Domain 5 (selection of the reported result): **Some concerns**, because the supplied registry does not contain a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan to verify the reported time point and analysis choices.

Under RoB 2, a result is judged as having **some concerns** when at least one domain raises some concerns and no domain is at high risk of bias. The concerns in Domains 2 and 5 do not, on the available evidence, substantially lower confidence sufficiently to warrant an overall high-risk judgement.
