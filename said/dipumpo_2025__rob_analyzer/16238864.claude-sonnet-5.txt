# Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) Assessment Report

**Study:** Randomized controlled trial of a brief research-based intervention promoting fruit and vegetable consumption (Kellar & Abraham)
**Design:** Individually-randomized parallel-group trial
**Comparison:** Experimental (persuasive leaflet + implementation intentions) vs. Control (questionnaire-related control task)
**Outcome assessed:** Frequency of RDIFV consumption at 1-week follow-up (self-report)
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), as no per-protocol/adherence adjustments are reported

---

## Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation:** Sequence generation was adequate — the authors explicitly state booklets were ordered using "a sequence of random integers generated by a true random number generator (Haahr, 2004)," satisfying signalling question 1.1 (Yes). However, allocation concealment is not clearly described. Booklets were pre-assembled in a single pile before distribution in lecture theatres/labs; there is no explicit statement that the person distributing the booklets, or participants themselves, could not anticipate or influence which booklet (intervention vs control) a given participant would receive. No description of sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed containers or an independent/central allocation mechanism is provided. This leads to a response of "No information" for signalling question 1.2. Baseline comparisons (MANOVA on pre-intervention behaviour and cognition measures) showed no significant differences between groups, consistent with successful randomization (signalling question 1.3 = No). Per the RoB 2 algorithm, a random sequence with no information on concealment, combined with baseline balance, maps to "Some concerns."

**Limitation noted:** The specific mechanism of allocation concealment (i.e., whether the researcher distributing booklets could distinguish intervention from control booklets, and whether participants' condition assignment could be anticipated before completion) is not reported and could not be verified from the text.

---

## Domain 2: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation:** Blinding of participants was not, and could not be, fully achieved because the intervention itself is the persuasive leaflet content — participants inherently were aware of what they received (signalling question 2.1 = Yes, by necessity of study design). However, participants were explicitly instructed "not to discuss the contents of their booklet with other participants over the following week" to reduce contamination, and completion was under supervised ("examination") conditions, which reduces (but does not eliminate) the risk of contamination-driven deviations arising from "trial context" (signalling questions 2.3/2.4 = probably no evidence of contextual deviations affecting outcome, though this relies on self-report of compliance with the no-discussion instruction and is not verified).
The analysis reported appears to follow intention-to-treat principles for available data (all randomized participants with follow-up data were analysed by original group assignment; no reported switching or crossover), satisfying signalling question 2.6 (Yes) for those with outcome data. There is no evidence of a naïve per-protocol or as-treated analysis being substituted. However, because participants could not be blinded to allocation (given the nature of a persuasive/leaflet intervention), and because full assurance that contamination did not occur is unverifiable (self-report methodology, no objective check), there remains a low-to-moderate risk that deviations from intended intervention (e.g., informal discussion despite instructions) could have influenced the outcome. This is not judged as "likely" but cannot be excluded with confidence.

**Limitation noted:** The paper provides no data or check on whether participants actually complied with the "no discussion" instruction, and no information is given on whether personnel (i.e., person administering booklets) were blinded to group assignment during distribution.

---

## Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:** Attrition at follow-up was substantial: 218 participants completed the baseline booklet, but only 146 (67%) responded at follow-up — an overall attrition rate of 33% (signalling question 3.1 = No, since outcome data were not available for "nearly all" participants). Critically, the authors themselves report a **significant difference between completers and non-completers**: "participants who completed the follow-up questionnaire reported significantly higher pre-intervention intentions, anticipated regret, and fruit and vegetable consumption" (MANOVA F(8,209)=2.05, p<.05). This constitutes direct evidence that missingness in the outcome was related to the true (baseline) value of variables strongly correlated with the outcome, i.e., that missingness plausibly depended on the true value of the outcome (signalling question 3.3 = Yes; signalling question 3.4 = Yes, given this documented empirical relationship).
While drop-out rates did not differ significantly between intervention and control groups (χ²(1)=1.77, ns), this alone is insufficient to rule out bias, since the differential relationship between missingness and prognostic/outcome-related variables was demonstrated regardless of group. No sensitivity analyses, multiple imputation, or other methods to correct for this documented missing-data mechanism were reported. Therefore, the criteria for "High risk of bias" are met: data were not available for nearly all participants, there is no evidence the result was unbiased by missing data, missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome, and there is direct empirical evidence (via the completer/non-completer comparison) that it likely did.

## Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation:** The outcome (self-reported number of days eating fruit/vegetables/RDIFV over the past week) is a participant-reported outcome, meaning the "outcome assessor" is the participant. The method of measurement is not inherently inappropriate (self-report of recalled days is a commonly used and validated proxy, and the authors note correlation with biochemical markers in other studies), so signalling question 4.1 = No. Measurement was identical across groups (same follow-up questionnaire administered to both arms at the same 1-week timepoint), so signalling question 4.2 = No.
However, participants were inherently aware of their intervention assignment (they had received and engaged with either the persuasive/planning leaflet or the neutral control task), so outcome assessors (i.e., participants) were not blinded (signalling question 4.3 = Yes). Because the outcome is self-reported dietary behaviour, assessment could plausibly be influenced by knowledge of intervention receipt — e.g., participants who completed an elaborate planning exercise may be more inclined to over-report subsequent healthy eating due to social desirability, demand characteristics, or cognitive dissonance reduction (signalling question 4.4 = Yes). The authors do not provide strong evidence that this influence definitely occurred (no independent/biochemical validation was performed in this study, this being explicitly listed as a limitation by the authors themselves), so it is not rated as "likely" (signalling question 4.5 = No/Probably no), leading to a "Some concerns" rather than "High risk" judgement.

**Limitation noted:** The authors explicitly acknowledge reliance on self-report without biochemical validation as a limitation, increasing the plausibility of measurement bias linked to awareness of allocation, though they do not report evidence that such bias definitively occurred.

---

## Domain 5: Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation:** No trial registration, pre-specified protocol, or statistical analysis plan is referenced or available in the article, so it cannot be determined whether the analyses reported (e.g., choice of hierarchical regression steps, choice of covariates, choice to report RDIFV/RDIF/RDIV as three separate but overlapping behavioural outcomes, exclusion of PBC from later analyses due to collinearity with self-efficacy) were specified in advance of unblinded outcome data being available (signalling question 5.1 = No information). The exclusion of PBC from subsequent analyses is explained post hoc ("since self-efficacy and PBC were strongly correlated... PBC was excluded from subsequent analyses"), which, while plausible methodologically, indicates an analytic decision made after seeing the data/correlation patterns, raising some concern about outcome/analysis selection (signalling questions 5.2/5.3 = No information, tending toward "Probably yes" for possible selection, since the outcome domain "fruit and vegetable consumption" was operationalised in three overlapping ways (RDIF, RDIV, RDIFV) and all three were reported with significant results, which somewhat mitigates concern for this specific outcome, but does not eliminate the general concern regarding the cognition analyses and choice of covariates).
Given the absence of a registered protocol and the presence of at least one clear post hoc analytic decision, but no clear/strong evidence of results-based selective reporting of the primary behavioural outcome (since all three related behavioural measures were reported, consistently, with similar direction and significance), the judgement is "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

**Limitation noted:** No protocol or trial registration was available to verify whether analyses (particularly cognitive/regression models) were pre-specified; this ambiguity could not be resolved from the published report.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Per the RoB 2 decision rules, an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" is warranted if the study is at high risk of bias in **at least one domain**, or if it has "some concerns" in multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result. In this study:

- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data) was independently judged **High risk of bias**, based on the authors' own reported finding that follow-up completers differed significantly from non-completers on baseline intention, anticipated regret, and fruit/vegetable consumption — directly indicating that missingness was related to variables closely tied to the outcome, with no correction or sensitivity analysis performed, and an attrition rate of 33%.
- Additionally, "Some concerns" were identified in three further domains (randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result), reflecting incomplete reporting of allocation concealment, inevitable lack of blinding for a self-report behavioural outcome following a psychologically engaging intervention, and absence of a pre-registered analysis plan.

The convergence of a "High risk" domain-level judgement (missing data) with multiple "Some concerns" domains substantially lowers confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported intervention effect on fruit and vegetable consumption at follow-up. Therefore, the overall risk of bias for this result is judged as **High**.
