# ROB 2 ASSESSMENT REPORT

**Study:** The effects of two spinal orthoses on balance in elderly people with thoracic kyphosis
**Design:** Individually-randomized parallel-group trial
**Comparison:** Spinomed orthosis vs. Posture-Training Support (PTS)
**Outcomes assessed:** Balance Score, Directional Control, Reaction Time (within-participant with/without orthosis, and between-group comparison)
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- Signalling Q1.1 (Was allocation sequence random?): The authors state that "the randomization procedure was to pick the name of the orthosis from a bag including the names of both orthoses." This is a form of simple random allocation and could be considered "Yes" in principle.
- Signalling Q1.2 (Was allocation sequence concealed?): There is no information whatsoever on allocation concealment. The "pick from a bag" method, unless independently administered and monitored, is highly susceptible to manipulation (predictability, tampering with envelope/name selection) and there is no description of a third party controlling this process, sequential numbering, or any safeguard against foreknowledge. Answer: **No information**, but the described method is inherently high risk given no safeguards were reported.
- Signalling Q1.3 (Did baseline differences suggest a problem?): Critically, the resulting groups were markedly unequal in size (10 vs 8 from only 18 participants), which the authors explicitly attribute to "the group allocation method." An unequal split of this magnitude from a supposedly simple randomization method with an implied 1:1 ratio is a strong indication of failure in the randomization process (analogous to the classic example in the RoB2 guidance of disproportionate allocation suggesting manipulation). No formal randomization software, blocking, or stratification is mentioned, and the "picking from a bag" method offers no protection against selection bias by whoever performed the draw.
- Baseline comparisons (age, weight, height, kyphosis angle, baseline balance parameters) showed no statistically significant differences, but the very small sample size (n=18) limits the power of these comparisons to detect true imbalance.

Given: (i) no described allocation concealment, (ii) an ad hoc, non-standardized "picking from a bag" method with no safeguards, and (iii) unexplained/unexpected imbalance in group sizes explicitly attributed by the authors to the allocation method itself — this constitutes clear evidence of a problem with the randomization process.

**Limitation noted:** The report provides no detail on who performed the allocation, whether the process was concealed from the assessors/participants, or why the group sizes differ so markedly from an intended equal split.

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Signalling Q2.1/2.2 (Were participants/personnel aware of assigned intervention?): This is an inherent feature of the intervention (physical orthosis) — blinding of participants and treating personnel to intervention group is impossible. Participants and orthotists necessarily knew which orthosis was applied. Thus, answer: **Yes**.
- Signalling Q2.3 (Deviations due to trial context?): There is no evidence of non-protocol interventions or deviations arising from awareness of allocation; the balance assessments (SOT, LOS) were performed under standardized, structured lab conditions (EquiTest/Balance Master protocols), each participant tested with and without their own orthosis, minimizing opportunity for differential trial-context-driven deviation. Answer: **No/Probably no**.
- Signalling Q2.6 (Appropriate analysis?): All 18 randomized participants who completed the protocol were included in within-group and between-group analyses; there is no indication of exclusion of eligible randomized participants from the outcome analysis (though this is also linked to the missing-data domain below). Analysis approach (paired t-test with/without orthosis; independent t-test between groups) is appropriate to the assignment being evaluated, given that this is essentially a within-subject immediate-effect comparison rather than a sustained-intervention trial.
- However, lack of blinding of participants/personnel is unavoidable for this intervention type but does introduce a risk that participants' voluntary effort, motivation, or performance during balance testing (a partially volitional/behavioral outcome, especially reaction time and directional control) could be consciously or unconsciously influenced by awareness of which orthosis (or absence of one) they were wearing at the time of testing. This raises "some concern" for the subjective/performance-based components of the outcome (directional control, reaction time), even though the objective platform-based measurements (force plate data) reduce this risk somewhat.

Because blinding was infeasible but no clear evidence of trial-context-driven deviations affecting the outcome was found, and appropriate analysis methods were used, the domain is judged as **Some concerns** rather than high risk.

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- Signalling Q3.1: The article states "Eighteen participants... completed the study procedure," and results tables report full data for all 10 (Spinomed) and 8 (PTS) participants for every outcome (balance score, directional control, reaction time), both with and without orthosis.
- There is no report of any dropout, withdrawal, or missing data points among the randomized/enrolled sample once allocation occurred. All participants who were randomized appear to have completed all test conditions.
- No evidence of exclusions or incomplete outcome data was found for any of the three outcome variables analyzed.

Given data completeness for all participants across all outcomes, this domain is judged **low risk of bias**.

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Signalling Q4.1 (Was measurement method inappropriate?): No — the SOT (EquiTest) and LOS (Balance Master) systems are validated, standardized, objective instrumented systems with established reliability and validity (as cited by the authors, refs 24, 32). Answer: **No**.
- Signalling Q4.2 (Could measurement differ between groups?): The same testing protocol, equipment, and procedure were applied to both groups identically (SOT and LOS tests, random order, same rest intervals). Answer: **No**.
- Signalling Q4.3 (Were outcome assessors aware of intervention?): The study is described as "single blinded," but it is not explicitly stated who was blinded — the participants themselves cannot be blinded to which orthosis (or lack of one) they are wearing during testing, since the test explicitly measures "with and without orthosis" conditions. It is unclear from the article whether the personnel operating/interpreting the EquiTest/Balance Master output (or the individual guiding the test) were blinded to which device was being tested. This is not explicitly reported. Answer: **No information/Probably yes (aware)**, since the device is a visible, physical apparatus and testers would almost certainly see which orthosis (if any) the subject is wearing.
- Signalling Q4.4/4.5: The outcome (balance score, directional control, reaction time) is largely machine-recorded via force-plate transducers, which is an observer-reported outcome not involving subjective judgment for the core recording of the numerical value — this substantially reduces the likelihood that knowledge of the group/condition influenced the recorded measurement itself. However, participant effort/motivation (a factor in volitional tests like LOS) could plausibly be influenced by the participant's own awareness of wearing (or not wearing) the orthosis, introducing a low-to-moderate risk of performance-related bias that is not fully attributable to assessor bias.

Because the phrase "single-blinded" is used without specifying who was blinded, and the outcome (especially LOS, which requires active/volitional patient effort) could plausibly be influenced by participant awareness of the tested condition, but the objective machine-based recording device limits this risk, the domain is judged **Some concerns**.

**Limitation noted:** The article does not clarify who constituted the "single blind" (i.e., assessor, participant, or data analyst), constituting an important reporting ambiguity that limits a more precise judgment.

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Signalling Q5.1 (Was there a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available?): No trial registration, published protocol, or statistical analysis plan is referenced or provided anywhere in the article. There is no way to verify whether the three reported outcomes (balance score, directional control, reaction time) and the specific statistical comparisons (paired t-test within group; independent t-test between groups) were pre-specified in this form before outcome data were examined. Answer: **No information**.
- Signalling Q5.2/5.3 (Selective reporting from multiple measurements/analyses?): The three balance parameters reported (balance score, directional control, reaction time) appear consistently reported across both groups and both within- and between-group comparisons, with no evidence of selective omission of a measured outcome or analysis. However, absent a protocol or trial registry entry, it is not possible to rule out the existence of additional unreported outcomes, time points, or alternative analytic strategies.

Given the complete absence of a registered protocol/SAP, but with an apparently full and consistent reporting of the outcomes that were described in the methods, the domain is judged as **Some concerns** rather than high risk.

**Limitation noted:** No trial registration number or accessible protocol/SAP was found in the article, precluding a definitive determination of whether outcome/analysis selection occurred.

## 6. OVERALL RISK-OF-BIAS JUDGEMENT

**Overall judgment: High risk of bias**

**Justification:**
Per the RoB 2 decision rules, an overall judgment of "High risk of bias" is warranted if the study is at high risk of bias in at least one domain, OR if there are "some concerns" in multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result.

In this study:
- **Domain 1 (Randomization process) = High risk of bias**, driven by an unconcealed, non-standardized allocation method ("picking from a bag") with no safeguards, and an unexplained, substantial imbalance in resulting group sizes (10 vs. 8) that the authors themselves attribute to the allocation method — this is direct evidence of a problem with the randomization process as defined by the RoB 2 tool.
- Additionally, "Some concerns" were identified in three further domains (deviations from intended interventions, measurement of outcome, and selection of the reported result), compounding the uncertainty around the trustworthiness of this result.

Because Domain 1 alone meets the criterion for "High risk of bias," and this is reinforced by multiple "Some concerns" ratings across the remaining domains, the overall risk-of-bias judgment for this result (comparative and within-group effects of Spinomed vs. PTS orthoses on balance score, directional control, and reaction time) is **High risk of bias**.

**Key limitations affecting this assessment (as reported, not assumed):**
1. No description of allocation concealment procedures.
2. Unexplained group-size imbalance (10 vs. 8) directly attributed by authors to the allocation method.
3. Ambiguity regarding who was blinded in this "single-blinded" design.
4. Absence of a publicly available/referenced trial protocol, registration, or statistical analysis plan.
5. Very small overall sample size (n=18), limiting the ability to detect true baseline imbalances and reducing precision of all effect estimates (noted as context, not used to alter domain-specific judgments beyond RoB 2 criteria).
