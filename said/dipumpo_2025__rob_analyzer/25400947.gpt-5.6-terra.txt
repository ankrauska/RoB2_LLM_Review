## RoB 2 assessment

**Study:** *Using Celecoxib for the Suppression of Preterm Labor Instead of Magnesium Sulfate*  
**Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial; 600 pregnant women reportedly randomized 1:1 to intravenous magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄) or oral celecoxib.  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to celecoxib versus MgSO₄ (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Result assessed:** The reported result for delivery/contraction suppression within 48 hours. The report is internally inconsistent: Table 1 labels the outcome as “delivery before 48 hours” but the narrative interprets 75.7% in the celecoxib group as successful prevention/suppression of preterm labour for 48 hours. This ambiguity prevents unambiguous identification of the endpoint direction, but does not remove the methodological concerns described below.  
**Sources available:** Article only. No protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial-registry record was supplied or identified in the article.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The article states that participants “were divided in two groups by simple random sampling” and later that they were “randomly divided in two equal groups.” It does not describe how an allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, random permuted blocks, or another method containing a random component). Under RoB 2, a statement that a study was randomized or used “simple random sampling,” without a described random sequence-generation method, is insufficient to establish that the allocation sequence was random.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  There is no description of allocation concealment. The report does not mention central randomization, a telephone/web allocation system, pharmacy-controlled allocation, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another mechanism that would prevent recruiters from foreseeing allocations.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Yes.**  
  The report identifies a statistically significant difference in cervical effacement between groups at baseline: mean effacement was **35.93% (SD 18.07)** in the MgSO₄ group and **42.0% (SD 15.64)** in the celecoxib group (**P ≤ 0.0001**). Cervical effacement is a clinically important prognostic characteristic in women presenting with preterm labour, because it reflects progression of labour and may be associated with failure of tocolysis and earlier delivery. The report itself also states that gestational age differed significantly between groups (**P < 0.0001**), although group-specific baseline gestational-age data are not presented in Table 3. The marked reported imbalance in effacement, combined with the absence of information on allocation concealment, suggests a problem with the randomization process.

**Motivation for judgement**  
RoB 2 classifies this domain as **high risk of bias** when allocation concealment is not reported and baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization. This trial has both features. The clinically important baseline imbalance in cervical effacement is unlikely to be adequately explained or dismissed using the information reported, and no allocation-concealment method is documented.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably yes.**  
  The treatments were materially different: one group received intravenous MgSO₄, including a loading dose and continuous maintenance infusion with toxicity monitoring, while the other received an oral celecoxib capsule every 12 hours. The study is described only as “single blinded,” without identifying who was blinded or describing any placebo, sham infusion, double-dummy procedure, or other means to maintain participant blinding. The routes, treatment procedures, and monitoring requirements make participant awareness probable.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Personnel administered either intravenous MgSO₄ with urine-output, deep-tendon-reflex, and respiratory-rate monitoring, or oral celecoxib with monitoring for different adverse effects. The personnel delivering and monitoring these treatments necessarily knew which intervention was administered.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  The article does not report treatment crossover, receipt of alternative tocolytics, unequal co-interventions, non-protocol obstetric interventions, or implementation failures. It also does not state whether such deviations were assessed.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — No information.**  
  The report does not state that an intention-to-treat analysis was performed. It merely states that *t*-tests and chi-square tests were used. The trial states that 300 women were allocated to each group, but Table 1 reports outcome data for 300 women in the MgSO₄ group and only **257 women** in the celecoxib group (227 + 30). Thus, 43 randomized women in the celecoxib group are not accounted for in this outcome table. The report does not explain whether these women had missing outcome data, were excluded after randomization, withdrew, crossed over, or were omitted due to an error in the table.

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of failure to analyse participants in the groups to which they were randomized? — Yes.**  
  The unexplained discrepancy concerns 43/300 participants (14.3%) in the celecoxib group. Given the intended outcome concerns treatment response and delivery within 48 hours, exclusion or reassignment of this number of participants could substantially affect the comparative result.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The study was effectively open to carers and treatment providers, while the effect of awareness-related deviations cannot be evaluated because no relevant data are provided. More importantly, analysis of all randomized participants in their assigned groups cannot be established. The discrepancy between the reported randomized sample (300 per group) and the reported celecoxib outcome total (257) is substantial and unexplained. Under RoB 2, where an appropriate assignment-effect analysis is not established and there is potential for a substantial impact from exclusions or analysis outside randomized groups, the appropriate judgement is **high risk of bias**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  The paper reports 600 participants, with 300 assigned to each group. For the 48-hour outcome, Table 1 includes 300 MgSO₄ participants (240 + 60) but only 257 celecoxib participants (227 + 30). Therefore, outcome data are not reported for 43/300 randomized celecoxib participants. This proportion is not sufficiently small to conclude that the missing outcomes could not materially affect the result.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No sensitivity analysis, missing-data analysis, imputation procedure, weighting procedure, or other analysis addressing the possible impact of missing outcome data is reported.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  The outcome is related to persistence or suppression of preterm labour and delivery timing. In this clinical context, missing outcome data could be related to treatment failure, rapid delivery, transfer, withdrawal, adverse events, or other clinical circumstances related to the outcome. The article gives no reasons for the 43 unaccounted-for celecoxib participants.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  The missing or unaccounted-for outcome data are highly unbalanced: all 43 unaccounted-for participants are in the celecoxib arm, whereas the MgSO₄ outcome total equals the reported randomized total. This marked between-group discrepancy, combined with no explanation of the missingness and no analysis demonstrating robustness, makes outcome-related missingness likely under the RoB 2 framework.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The article’s numerical reporting does not support complete outcome ascertainment. The unexplained absence of outcome data for 14.3% of the celecoxib group, with no corresponding discrepancy in the MgSO₄ group, creates a serious risk that the reported comparative estimate is biased. Because missingness is plausibly—and likely—related to the true clinical outcome, this domain is at **high risk of bias**.

**Reporting limitation relevant to this judgement:** It is possible that Table 1 contains a typographical error, because the narrative repeatedly refers to a 75.7% celecoxib response rate, which corresponds to 227/300 rather than 227/257. However, the article does not identify Table 1 as erroneous, does not provide corrected counts, and does not account for the remaining 73 women that would be required for the percentages to total 100% among 300 participants. RoB 2 assessment must therefore use the internally inconsistent information as reported rather than assume a correction.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  The study reports that uterine contractions were checked every two hours over 20-minute periods and that cervical dilatation and effacement were evaluated. These are clinically relevant assessments for determining whether preterm labour had been suppressed. The article does not provide enough detail to establish standardized measurement procedures, but it does not demonstrate that the stated methods were inherently inappropriate.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The report describes the same stated schedule for checking uterine contractions, and it does not explicitly report different outcome-assessment time points or thresholds between groups. However, the lack of a detailed measurement protocol remains a limitation.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Probably yes.**  
  The study is described as single blinded without clarification of who was blinded. The two interventions had visibly and procedurally different modes of administration—intravenous MgSO₄ infusion versus oral celecoxib—and required different safety-monitoring procedures. The clinicians assessing contractions and cervical findings were likely aware of treatment allocation.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.**  
  Assessment of uterine contractions and, particularly, cervical dilatation and effacement involves clinical observation and judgement. The reported outcome also includes a determination that contractions were “arrested” and that cervical changes did not progress. Such assessments can potentially be influenced by knowledge of assigned treatment when assessors are unblinded.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  The report does not provide direct evidence that outcome assessment was influenced by treatment knowledge, such as different assessment criteria, different visit intensity, or differential clinical decision-making. Nonetheless, it does not describe independent blinded outcome assessment or standardized safeguards against observer influence.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The outcomes were assessed by personnel likely aware of the assigned intervention, and relevant components of the outcome require clinical judgement. This creates the potential for differential measurement. However, the article provides no direct evidence that outcome assessment was actually influenced by knowledge of treatment assignment. In accordance with RoB 2, this supports a judgement of **some concerns**, rather than high risk, for this domain.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions and assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial registration number, or pre-specified primary-outcome definition is reported. The article only describes use of chi-square and independent *t*-tests. Therefore, it cannot be established that the reported analysis was pre-specified before outcome data were available.

- **5.2 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The trial reports several relevant measures and time-related outcomes, including contraction arrest, delivery before/after 48 hours, duration of drug use, cervical effacement, cervical dilatation, and gestational age. In the absence of a protocol or detailed outcome-analysis plan, it cannot be determined whether the reported 48-hour result was selected from among multiple eligible measurements or time points based on its findings.

- **5.3 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  No prespecified analysis approach is available. The report does not explain whether analyses were adjusted or unadjusted, how baseline imbalances were handled, how missing data were handled, whether alternative definitions of treatment response were considered, or whether analyses were conducted in the full randomized population.

**Motivation for judgement**  
There is no publicly reported pre-specified protocol or analysis plan against which the reported result can be checked. The article reports multiple outcome measures and analyses but provides insufficient detail to establish that the 48-hour result and its analysis were selected without reference to the observed findings. There is no affirmative evidence of selective reporting sufficient for a high-risk judgement, but the missing pre-specification information leads to **some concerns**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation for overall judgement**  
The overall judgement is **high risk of bias** because at least one domain—and in this assessment, three domains—is judged at high risk of bias:

1. **Bias arising from the randomization process:** allocation concealment is not reported and clinically important baseline imbalance in cervical effacement strongly suggests a possible problem with randomization.  
2. **Bias due to deviations from intended interventions:** the analysis population is not adequately accounted for; the 48-hour outcome table omits 43 participants from the celecoxib group, and an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis cannot be confirmed.  
3. **Bias due to missing outcome data:** outcome data are not reported for 43/300 celecoxib participants, missingness is markedly unbalanced between groups, and no explanation or robustness analysis is provided.  

In addition, there are **some concerns** regarding outcome measurement because likely unblinded clinical assessors evaluated judgement-dependent outcomes, and regarding selective reporting because no pre-specified protocol or analysis plan is available. These limitations substantially reduce confidence in the reported conclusion that celecoxib and MgSO₄ had similar effectiveness for suppression of preterm labour.
