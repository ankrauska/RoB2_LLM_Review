## RoB 2 assessment

**Assessment target:** the reported effect of assignment to educational format—community video, knowledge video, both videos, or pictorial pamphlet—on **proper inhaler-use technique at the 3-month post-intervention follow-up**. This is an individually randomized, four-arm parallel-group trial (92 randomized participants). The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to intervention** (intention-to-treat effect).

A limitation intrinsic to this assessment is that the article does not present a clearly specified single between-group effect estimate for each randomized comparison. It predominantly reports within-group pretest-to-follow-up changes and adjusted analyses with incompletely reported model details. The assessment therefore concerns the reported 3-month inhaler-technique result as the most clearly tabulated objectively observed outcome.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The article states that eligible participants were “randomly assigned” to the four groups, and the registry records the allocation as randomized. However, neither source describes how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, random blocks, or minimization). Under RoB 2, a statement that a study was randomized, without a description of a random component in sequence generation, is insufficient to establish that the sequence was random.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The report does not describe allocation concealment. There is no statement of centralized allocation, independent randomization, pharmacy-controlled allocation, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another mechanism that would prevent recruiters from knowing upcoming assignments before enrolment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No information.**  
  The four groups were similar in size (20–22 participants), which does not itself suggest an obvious departure from the intended allocation ratio. However, the article does not provide a full baseline table by randomized group for key prognostic variables such as baseline inhaler technique, asthma severity, medication regimen, age, education, or other factors relevant to the outcome. The available table provides overall characteristics and ethnic composition by group but does not permit a meaningful assessment of baseline balance for the inhaler-use outcome.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The absence of information on both sequence generation and allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement. There is no reported evidence of a clearly problematic allocation method or a major baseline imbalance; therefore, this domain is judged **some concerns**, rather than high risk.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Participants necessarily knew whether they watched a community video, watched a knowledge video, watched both videos, or read a pamphlet. These are visibly distinct educational formats and no participant blinding was feasible or reported.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The facilitators delivered or supervised the assigned educational materials and conducted interviews. They would necessarily have been aware of whether the participant received videos or a pamphlet. The article states that facilitators were not aware of the study hypothesis, but this does not establish that they were unaware of intervention assignment.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  The report does not describe contamination between groups, use of non-protocol asthma education, differential contact time, co-interventions, or whether participants accessed materials allocated to other groups. It also does not report whether all allocated participants viewed/read the complete assigned material. The article reports that four participants “discontinued intervention,” but does not describe their allocated groups, reasons, or the nature of discontinuation.

- **2.4–2.5 Were such deviations likely to affect the outcome and balanced between groups? — Not applicable / no information.**  
  Since the existence and nature of trial-context deviations are not reported, their effect on inhaler technique and balance across groups cannot be assessed.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes, with limitations.**  
  The investigators report ANOVA and ANCOVA analyses using intervention type and covariates, and participants appear to have been analysed according to their assigned educational group or educational format. Participants with missing follow-up data were excluded from the reported analyses; for this RoB 2 domain, exclusion due to missing outcome data is addressed in Domain 3 rather than Domain 2.  
  However, the reporting is insufficiently clear: the paper focuses substantially on within-group change over time, combines groups for some analyses, and does not clearly provide the numerical adjusted between-group estimates for each prespecified randomized comparison.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Participants and intervention personnel were aware of allocation. The article does not provide enough information to determine whether knowledge of intervention assignment resulted in differential trial-context deviations, such as unequal additional asthma education, differential support, or contamination. Although the analysis appears broadly compatible with analysis by randomized assignment, its reporting is incomplete and the reported results do not clearly correspond to a single prespecified contrast. The domain is therefore judged **some concerns**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably no.**  
  Ninety-two participants were enrolled and randomized, but 85 completed the study. Thus, outcome data were unavailable for seven randomized participants (7.6%). For a continuous outcome such as inhaler-technique score, this is below the usual approximate 95% completeness level cited in RoB 2 guidance. In addition, the degrees of freedom reported for some analyses suggest that the number included in particular models may have been lower than 85, although the exact analysis population is not clearly reported.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The article does not report an analysis addressing missing data, such as an appropriate multiple-imputation approach, inverse-probability weighting, or sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-data assumptions. No analysis demonstrates that the result would be robust to the missing outcomes.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  Participants discontinued the intervention or did not complete the study, and the reasons for missing outcome data are not reported. For an educational intervention assessing inhaler technique, withdrawal or non-completion could plausibly be associated with health literacy, engagement, baseline ability, perceived benefit, asthma status, or difficulty using inhalers—all of which could be associated with the unobserved inhaler-technique outcome.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — No information.**  
  The report does not provide reasons for the seven losses, their group allocation, their baseline inhaler technique, or the distribution of missing data by intervention group. Consequently, it is impossible to determine whether missingness was related to the true follow-up outcome or differed by intervention group.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Outcome data were not available for all or nearly all randomized participants; no analysis or sensitivity analysis establishes that missing data did not bias the result; and the reasons and group distribution of missingness are unreported. Because missingness could depend on the unobserved outcome and the information needed to rule out likely outcome-dependent missingness is absent, this domain is judged **high risk of bias** under the RoB 2 algorithm.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  Inhaler technique was assessed using a standardized nine-step observational checklist for different inhaler devices. The reported scoring system ranged from 0 to 9, with higher scores reflecting completion of the required inhaler-use steps. This is an appropriate method for evaluating observed inhaler-use technique.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The same checklist and interview process were reportedly used for all participants. The study does not report use of a different measurement tool, threshold, or follow-up procedure in one intervention group compared with another.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — No information.**  
  The inhaler assessment was performed by the facilitator/interviewer and research coordinator. The article states that facilitators were unaware of the study hypothesis, but it does not state that assessors were blinded to the participant’s educational allocation. Given that the facilitators were involved in intervention delivery or follow-up procedures, blinding cannot be inferred.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably yes.**  
  Although inhaler technique was assessed using a standardized checklist, some assessment and scoring judgement is involved in observing whether each inhaler-use step was performed correctly. Knowledge that a participant had received one or two educational videos rather than a pamphlet could potentially influence assessment.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  The use of a defined nine-step checklist and two observers provides some protection against strongly subjective assessment. The report does not provide evidence that assessors consciously or systematically rated one intervention group more favourably. Nevertheless, assessor blinding was not reported.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The outcome measurement method itself was appropriate and apparently applied similarly across groups. However, it is not reported whether outcome assessors were blinded to intervention allocation. Because inhaler technique is an observer-assessed outcome with at least some scope for judgement, assessor awareness could have influenced scoring. There is no direct evidence that this occurred, so the domain is judged **some concerns**, not high risk.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — Probably no.**  
  The ClinicalTrials.gov record confirms the trial was registered, but it does not provide a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan for the reported inhaler-technique outcome. The registry’s stated primary outcome is medication adherence with a “one year” time frame, whereas the article emphasizes pretest to 3-month follow-up results and includes multiple outcomes and subgroup analyses. The article does not cite or provide a dated protocol or statistical analysis plan specifying the relevant outcome measurement, time point, analysis model, covariates, contrast between groups, or approach to missing data.

- **5.2 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  Inhaler technique was assessed at baseline, immediate post-test, and 3-month follow-up; a later telephone assessment was also conducted but used a shorter questionnaire and did not provide the same observed inhaler-technique measurement. The paper summarizes selected pretest and 3-month data, but no pre-specified hierarchy of time points is available. The available sources do not establish whether the 3-month result was selected because of its magnitude, direction, or statistical significance.

- **5.3 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The investigators report use of ANOVA, ANCOVA, adjusted analyses using age, sex, ethnicity, and education, and interaction terms. They also report analyses by individual study group, ethnicity, sex, age, and education. Without a sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan, it cannot be determined whether the reported analysis was selected from multiple analyses on the basis of its result.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
No detailed pre-specified analysis plan finalized before outcome-data analysis was available. Multiple outcome time points, outcome constructs, adjusted and subgroup analyses were possible, and the reporting does not make clear which analysis was prospectively primary. However, the article and registry do not provide direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable. Therefore, the appropriate judgement is **some concerns**, rather than high risk.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**  
The study is judged at **high risk of bias** because it is at high risk of bias in **Domain 3 (missing outcome data)**. Seven of 92 randomized participants did not complete the study, the report does not provide their reasons for missing outcome data or their allocation by intervention group, and no analysis or sensitivity analysis demonstrates that the reported inhaler-technique effect is robust to plausible missing-data assumptions.

In addition, there are **some concerns** in the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. These concerns arise from incomplete reporting of sequence generation and allocation concealment, absence of information on intervention contamination or differential co-interventions, unreported assessor blinding, and the lack of a detailed prospective analysis plan.
