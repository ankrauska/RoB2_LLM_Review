# RoB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Prospective randomized comparison of LAVH vs AH for uteri >200 g
**Design:** Individually-randomized parallel-group trial
**Outcome assessed for this report:** Length of hospital stay (primary outcome); comments also apply to related outcomes (blood loss, pain index, complications) where relevant
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- 1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? → **Yes.** The text states: "Procedure type was assigned by a computer-generated randomization list."
- 1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until enrolment? → **Probably yes.** "Concealment of randomization was done by telephone inquiry," which is a centralized method generally considered adequate. However, no further detail is given on who administered this service, whether it was truly independent of enrolling clinicians, or how irrevocably assignments were confirmed.
- 1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization? → **No/Probably no**, with an important caveat. Age, gravidity, parity, and preoperatively estimated uterine weight were similar (Table 1; explicitly stated "not significant"). However, a statistically significant difference was noted in the level of surgeon experience: patients undergoing AH were more often operated on by a resident (60% vs 28.6% in LAVH group), a difference reported as significant (p < 0.05). While this is not a "baseline" clinical characteristic of the participant in the traditional sense, it represents an unequal distribution of an important prognostic/methodological co-variate between arms that emerged after randomization, raising some concern about whether allocation truly balanced factors relevant to outcome (surgical skill/experience), or whether this reflects a downstream, non-random process (e.g., systematic assignment of more complex/large cases to attendings).

**Motivation for judgement:** Random sequence generation is confirmed and allocation concealment method (telephone) is plausible and generally adequate, satisfying criteria for "Low" on domain-specific technical grounds. However, the significant imbalance in surgeon seniority between groups is a methodological baseline factor that could independently influence operative outcomes (blood loss, complications, hospital stay), and its origin is not explained by the authors. Per RoB 2 guidance, meaningful imbalances that are unlikely to be due to chance and could bias the outcome estimate warrant "Some concerns," even when sequence generation and concealment are adequately reported. **Limitation/ambiguity to report:** The article does not explain why surgeon seniority differed significantly between groups; this ambiguity cannot be resolved from the text and is flagged as a limitation of the assessment rather than assumed to reflect subversion of randomization.

**Predicted direction of bias:** Unpredictable (could favor either group depending on whether resident-performed cases had systematically different outcomes).

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions:**
- 2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention? → **Yes.** The authors explicitly acknowledge: "the patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment; thus, their expectations may have influenced the duration of their recuperation or their satisfaction with the procedure."
- 2.2 Were carers/people delivering the intervention aware of assignment? → **Yes.** By nature of the surgical procedures (laparoscopic vs open abdominal), surgeons and care teams could not be blinded.
- 2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? → **Probably yes.** The lack of blinding plausibly influenced subjectively assessed outcomes, particularly the pain index and satisfaction/questionnaire responses (patient expectations bias, as the authors themselves note), and potentially the self-determined discharge decision, since "each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital" — a subjective, unblinded, patient-driven endpoint directly determining the primary outcome (length of hospital stay).
- 2.4 Were these deviations likely to have affected the outcome? → **Yes.** The primary outcome (hospital stay) was operationally determined by an unblinded patient's own subjective decision, strongly susceptible to influence by knowledge of which procedure was received and expectation of faster recovery from the "less invasive" laparoscopic approach.
- 2.5 Were these deviations balanced between groups? → **No/Probably no.** Given the directional hypothesis of the study (that LAVH is superior) and unblinded expectations, deviations are unlikely to be balanced; the effect would plausibly be systematically in favor of LAVH.
- 2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? → **No information/Unclear.** No explicit statement of intention-to-treat analysis; however, no participants appear to have been excluded post-randomization, and all 48 randomized patients appear to be analyzed for surgical/objective outcomes. For the questionnaire-based outcomes (satisfaction, recuperation), only 35/48 (72.9%) answered, introducing missing data handled elsewhere (Domain 3), but no adjustment or sensitivity analysis was reported.

**Motivation for judgement:** The core issue is that the primary outcome (length of hospital stay) was not an objective, protocol-driven measurement but was self-determined by an unblinded patient ("each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged"). This is a major, unaddressed source of bias due to deviation from intended intervention/assessment context, compounded by the authors' own acknowledgment that "expectations may have influenced the duration of their recuperation or their satisfaction with the procedure." Because blinding was impossible (inherent to comparing surgical approaches) and no measures were taken to standardize discharge criteria or otherwise mitigate the effect of patient expectation on this key outcome, the domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

**Predicted direction of bias:** Favors experimental intervention (LAVH), as patient/surgeon awareness and expectation of a "less invasive" procedure would plausibly shorten reported hospital stay and improve reported satisfaction independent of a true causal effect.

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- 3.1 Were outcome data available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? → For the primary outcome of hospital stay and intraoperative/objective outcomes: **Yes**, data appear complete for all 48 randomized women (28 LAVH, 20 AH), as reflected in Tables 1 and 2. However, for the longer-term questionnaire-based outcomes (satisfaction, recuperation details), only 35/48 (72.9%) responded (20/28 LAVH = 71.4%; 15/20 AH = 75%).
- 3.2 Is there evidence the result was not biased by missing data? → **No** for the questionnaire-derived outcomes; no sensitivity analysis or imputation was reported.
- 3.3 Could missingness depend on the true outcome value? → **Probably yes.** It is plausible that patients less satisfied or with worse outcomes (in either group) were less likely to return/complete a mailed self-administered questionnaire, or conversely, that satisfied patients were more motivated to respond.
- 3.4 Is it likely that missingness depended on the true value? → **No/Probably no** at the judgement level for reporting purposes, since the proportion of missing questionnaires was similar and non-significantly different between groups (71.4% vs 75%), suggesting the missingness was not clearly differential.

**Motivation for judgement:** For objective/surgical outcomes and the primary outcome of hospital stay, data appear complete, supporting low risk of bias for that specific outcome. However, for patient-reported/questionnaire-based secondary outcomes (satisfaction, long-term recuperation), nearly 27% of participants did not respond, and while the proportion missing was balanced between groups, no formal analysis was undertaken to demonstrate the robustness of the results to this missingness, nor were reasons for non-response reported. This creates "Some concerns" specifically affecting interpretation of satisfaction/recuperation data, which are important secondary findings emphasized in the abstract and conclusions.

**Predicted direction of bias:** Unpredictable.

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions:**
- 4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? → **No/Probably no** in principle (hospital stay in days, blood counts, and pain scores are standard measures); however, for pain, an unvalidated 0–10 scale was used and for recuperation, an unvalidated, non-standardized questionnaire was administered ("self-administered nonvalidated questionnaire").
- 4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? → **Yes.** Critically, "each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital" — this means the primary outcome itself (length of hospital stay) was operationalized through a subjective, patient-driven decision rather than objective clinical discharge criteria applied identically to both groups.
- 4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? → **Yes.** Patients (who are the "assessors" for pain, satisfaction, discharge-readiness, and recuperation) were, by definition, aware of which procedure they underwent, since blinding was impossible.
- 4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention received? → **Yes.** This applies directly to the pain index, discharge decision, recuperation duration, and satisfaction/questionnaire responses.
- 4.5 Is it likely that assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? → **Yes.** The authors themselves state directly: "patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment; thus, their expectations may have influenced the duration of their recuperation or their satisfaction with the procedure." Given the strong prior expectation (reinforced by pre-study literature and clinical culture) that minimally invasive surgery leads to faster recovery and less pain, and that this outcome directly determines a key study endpoint, the risk that the self-determined discharge outcome and patient-reported pain/satisfaction were influenced by intervention awareness is high.

**Motivation for judgement:** The primary outcome (hospital stay) and several key secondary outcomes (pain index, satisfaction, recuperation) are all either directly patient-reported or patient-driven decisions made without blinding, in a context where the direction of expected benefit (LAVH superior) was well known/anticipated by both patients and clinicians. The authors' own explicit acknowledgment of this issue substantiates a judgement of **High risk of bias** for this domain, as it is likely (not merely possible) that assessment of these outcomes was influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

**Predicted direction of bias:** Favors experimental intervention (LAVH).

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions:**
- 5.1 Were the data analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? → **No information.** No trial registry entry, protocol, or statistical analysis plan is referenced or available in the article. The article does specify a priori that the primary outcome was length of hospital stay and provides an a priori sample-size calculation based on this outcome ("we hoped to reduce the median hospital stay… we calculated that we would need a sample size of 20 cases per group"), which is reassuring evidence of pre-specification for this single outcome. However, no such information is available regarding the many secondary outcomes reported (blood loss, transfusion, pain index, hemoglobin/hematocrit change, complications, satisfaction).
- 5.2 Was the result likely selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements? → **No information.** Pain was assessed on days 1, 3, and 5, but only day 4 is reported in the results/tables narrative (Table 2 shows "day 4," while the Methods state assessments were on days "1, 3, and 5" — a discrepancy that raises the possibility that the most favorable timepoint/data point was selected, though this could also be a reporting/transcription inconsistency).
- 5.3 Was the result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses? → **No information.** No evidence of multiple alternative analyses (e.g., adjusted vs unadjusted) being conducted; the statistical methods described (chi-square, Fisher's exact) appear applied consistently.

**Motivation for judgement:** The primary outcome was clearly pre-specified with an a priori sample size justification, supporting low risk of bias for that specific result. However, the absence of any registered protocol or statistical analysis plan, combined with an unexplained discrepancy between the pain-assessment timepoints described in the Methods (days 1, 3, 5) and the timepoint actually reported in Table 2 (day 4), introduces uncertainty as to whether outcome measurements/timepoints for secondary outcomes were selected on the basis of the results. This ambiguity cannot be resolved from the information provided in the article and is reported as a limitation of this assessment rather than assumed to reflect deliberate selective reporting.

**Predicted direction of bias:** Unpredictable (for secondary outcomes); Not applicable/Low concern for the primary outcome (hospital stay).

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement: **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Justification:** Per RoB 2 rules, an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" is warranted if the study is at high risk of bias in at least one domain, or has "some concerns" in multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result. In this study:

- Domain 2 (deviations from intended interventions) is judged **High risk of bias**, because the primary outcome (hospital stay) was operationalized via an unblinded patient's own subjective decision, explicitly acknowledged by the authors as susceptible to expectation bias.
- Domain 4 (measurement of the outcome) is judged **High risk of bias**, for the same fundamental reason — lack of blinding directly and demonstrably (by the authors' own admission) could have influenced the primary and several secondary outcomes.
- In addition, Domains 1, 3, and 5 all carry "Some concerns," driven respectively by an unexplained surgeon-experience imbalance between arms, incomplete/unanalyzed questionnaire response data, and absence of a registered protocol/SAP alongside a timepoint discrepancy in pain reporting.

The convergence of two independent "High risk" domains — both stemming from the same underlying and unresolvable methodological weakness (impossibility of blinding combined with patient-driven, subjective determination of the primary outcome) — together with additional "Some concerns" in three further domains, substantially lowers confidence in the reported treatment effect. Therefore, the overall risk-of-bias judgement for this trial's primary result (length of hospital stay), and by extension for the closely related subjectively-assessed secondary outcomes (pain, satisfaction, recuperation), is **High risk of bias**.

**Note on limitations of this assessment:** This judgement is based solely on the published article; no trial protocol, registration record, or statistical analysis plan was available for review. Several judgements (particularly regarding pre-specification of secondary outcome analyses and the reasons underlying the surgeon-experience imbalance between groups) are limited by this lack of supplementary documentation, and are explicitly flagged above as ambiguities rather than assumptions.
