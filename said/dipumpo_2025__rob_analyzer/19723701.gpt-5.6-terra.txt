## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

- **Study:** MADIT-CRT (Moss et al., *N Engl J Med* 2009), individually randomized, parallel-group trial.
- **Comparison:** CRT–ICD (experimental intervention) versus ICD alone (comparator).
- **Outcome/result assessed:** Primary composite outcome of **time to death from any cause or first nonfatal heart-failure event**, whichever occurred first; reported hazard ratio **0.66 (95% CI 0.52–0.84)**, based on all 1820 randomized participants and an average follow-up of 2.4 years.
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment to intervention** (intention-to-treat effect).
- **Sources assessed:** The primary article and the supplied ClinicalTrials.gov record/protocol information (NCT00180271).

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Probably yes.**  
  The article states that patients “were randomly assigned in a 3:2 ratio” and that randomization was stratified by clinical centre and ischemic status, using “an algorithm that ensured near balance in each stratum.” The trial registry also identifies allocation as randomized. Although the report does not explicitly state the random element within the balancing algorithm, the description of this large multicentre trial supports a judgement of *probably yes* rather than no information.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  Neither the article nor the supplied registry/protocol information describes the mechanism of allocation concealment. There is no statement of central randomization, an independent randomization service, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another method that would establish concealment from personnel enrolling participants. Randomization and post-randomization blinding are not substitutes for allocation concealment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  Baseline characteristics were reported for all randomized participants and were closely similar between groups. This includes age, sex, ischemic versus nonischemic cardiomyopathy, NYHA class, diabetes, hypertension treatment, left bundle-branch block, QRS duration, ejection fraction, ventricular volumes, and heart-failure medications. The group sizes, 1089 versus 731, are compatible with the planned 3:2 allocation. No imbalance is reported that indicates a problem with randomization.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The reported randomization and the balanced baseline data provide reassurance that the allocation process probably used a random sequence. However, allocation concealment is not described. Under RoB 2, absence of information on concealment, despite baseline balance compatible with chance, leads to **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The trial registry specifies no masking. Moreover, the interventions were visibly and clinically different: CRT–ICD included biventricular pacing, whereas the comparator was ICD alone.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The article explicitly states that treating physicians were aware of study-group assignments.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  Deviations occurred: 82 participants assigned to CRT–ICD received an ICD-only device because of technical difficulty positioning the CRT lead, and 91 assigned to ICD-only received CRT–ICD during follow-up. Of the latter, 61 crossed over after a heart-failure event and therefore after occurrence of the primary endpoint; 30 crossed over at physician discretion before an endpoint. The reported technical implantation failures and subsequent clinical treatment decisions do not establish that deviations occurred because of trial recruitment, engagement, lack of equipoise, or another trial-context mechanism. The article provides no evidence that non-protocol co-interventions or deviations arising from knowledge of allocation were systematically introduced in one group because of the trial context.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The article states that “data analysis was performed according to the intention-to-treat principle.” The primary analysis included all 1089 participants assigned to CRT–ICD and all 731 assigned to ICD-only treatment, analyzed according to randomized assignment. The registry likewise states that “data from all patients enrolled were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis.”

**Motivation for judgement**  
Participants and treating personnel were unblinded, and treatment crossovers occurred. However, for the effect of assignment to intervention, RoB 2 considers deviations problematic only when they arose because of the trial context and were likely to affect the outcome. The report does not show this. The primary analysis appropriately retained randomized group assignment despite crossovers and implantation failures. Therefore, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably no.**  
  The article reports that 44 of 1089 participants in the CRT–ICD group (4.0%) and 55 of 731 participants in the ICD-only group (7.5%) “declined to continue participating in the study, were withdrawn by a physician, or were lost to follow-up.” Thus, 99 of 1820 randomized participants (5.4%) had follow-up discontinued, with a notably higher proportion in the ICD-only group.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The article does not report an analysis demonstrating robustness of the primary result to missing data, such as a sensitivity analysis using plausible assumptions about outcomes among participants with discontinued follow-up. No method that demonstrably corrects potential bias from informative censoring is described.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  The primary outcome includes heart-failure events and death. Withdrawal, physician withdrawal, or loss to follow-up could plausibly be related to participants’ health status, subsequent heart-failure events, or risk of death. The report combines these distinct reasons and does not provide outcome-specific information enabling exclusion of such a relationship.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  The proportion with discontinued participation/loss to follow-up differed between groups: 7.5% in the ICD-only group versus 4.0% in the CRT–ICD group. For a time-to-event endpoint driven by heart-failure events, this differential censoring, together with reasons that could be health-related and are not reported separately by treatment group, provides evidence that missingness may have been related to prognosis and outcome occurrence.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The primary result is based on time-to-event analysis. Follow-up was discontinued or participants were lost for 99 randomized participants, and this was substantially more common in the ICD-only group. The article does not provide the specific reasons for missing outcome data by randomized group, does not establish that censoring was non-informative, and does not report sensitivity analyses demonstrating that the hazard ratio was robust to plausible outcomes among participants with missing follow-up. Since the outcome is heart failure or death and differential missingness occurred, it is likely that missingness could depend on the true outcome value. This leads to a **high risk of bias** judgement.

**Assessment limitation:** The supplied report does not separate the 99 participants into those who declined participation, were withdrawn by physicians, or were lost to follow-up, nor does it report their timing or outcome status. This prevents a more precise characterization or direction of the resulting bias.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  The primary endpoint was prospectively defined as death from any cause or a nonfatal heart-failure event. Death is an objective endpoint. Heart-failure events were defined using signs and symptoms consistent with congestive heart failure and a requirement for intravenous decongestive therapy in an outpatient setting or an augmented decongestive regimen during hospitalization. The endpoint was adjudicated according to prespecified criteria.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Yes.**  
  Treating physicians knew treatment assignment and made the clinical decisions central to identifying a heart-failure event: diagnosis of heart failure, use of augmented decongestive therapy, and hospitalization or outpatient intravenous therapy. The article itself states: “the investigators who decided on therapy or hospital admission for patients with heart failure were aware of such assignments” and acknowledges that this knowledge “contributed in some way to the lower frequency of heart failure in the CRT–ICD group.” Thus, ascertainment of the heart-failure component could have differed between groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of intervention received? — Yes, for the clinically decisive assessment leading to heart-failure-event identification.**  
  Although the independent heart-failure adjudication committee was unaware of treatment assignment, the treating physicians who diagnosed heart failure and decided whether to provide the treatment required by the endpoint definition were aware of assignment. For this component, the treating physicians were outcome assessors and decision-makers.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.**  
  The heart-failure endpoint partly reflects treating-physician decisions regarding treatment escalation and hospital admission. Such outcomes can be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received. This concern is especially material because the intervention could not be blinded and because the primary composite effect was driven by heart-failure events rather than death.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably yes.**  
  The trial was unblinded; the heart-failure component involved clinical judgement and treatment/admission decisions; and the authors explicitly acknowledge the possibility that clinicians’ knowledge of treatment assignment contributed to the lower observed heart-failure frequency in the CRT–ICD group. The observed primary effect was driven by reduced heart-failure events (hazard ratio for heart failure alone 0.59), while mortality was identical between groups (hazard ratio 1.00). Thus, the potentially susceptible component was the component responsible for the reported benefit.

**Motivation for judgement**  
Blinded endpoint adjudication is an important safeguard, but it does not eliminate the risk created when unblinded treating physicians determine whether the clinical circumstances and treatment decisions required to trigger a heart-failure event have occurred. Because the primary endpoint was driven by the heart-failure component, and because the report explicitly recognizes the possibility of bias from unblinded clinical decisions, this domain is judged at **high risk of bias**.

**Assessment limitation:** The report does not state whether the blinded adjudication committee systematically reviewed information for all participants or only events identified and submitted by treating investigators. This ambiguity precludes determining the extent to which blinded adjudication could mitigate differential event identification.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analyzed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — Probably yes.**  
  The trial was registered in 2005, before completion and before the primary-completion date in June 2009. The registry specifies the primary endpoint as all-cause mortality or first heart-failure event, with analysis at an average 2.4 years of follow-up. The article reports that the study design and protocol had been published previously, and specifies a prespecified group-sequential design, interim monitoring boundaries, a stratified log-rank test for the primary analysis, and a stratified Cox model for hazard-ratio estimation. The trial stopped after crossing the prespecified efficacy boundary at the ninth planned interim analysis.

- **5.2 Was the reported result likely selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain on the basis of the results? — No.**  
  The primary outcome, its components, and the time-to-first-event framework were prospectively specified. The article reports the composite endpoint, heart failure alone, and death at any time. Reporting the components does not show that the primary composite result was selected from multiple measurements based on statistical significance.

- **5.3 Was the reported result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data on the basis of the results? — No.**  
  The article reports the prespecified intention-to-treat, stratified log-rank and Cox proportional-hazards analyses. It describes adjustment for the group-sequential monitoring rule and inclusion of late-reported events occurring before trial termination. There is no reported discrepancy between the registered primary outcome and the published primary result, and no evidence that the reported hazard ratio was selected from multiple eligible analyses because it was favorable.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The registered outcome and the published primary result are consistent, and the article describes a prospectively specified group-sequential design and primary analytical approach. No evidence in the provided sources indicates selective choice of the reported endpoint, time point, or analysis based on results. This domain is therefore judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**

The primary result for death from any cause or first nonfatal heart-failure event is judged at **high risk of bias** because:

1. **Bias due to missing outcome data is high risk:** follow-up discontinuation/loss occurred in 5.4% overall and was differential between groups (7.5% ICD-only vs 4.0% CRT–ICD), with no reported outcome-specific reasons, informative-censoring assessment, or sensitivity analysis; and  
2. **Bias in measurement of the outcome is high risk:** the effect was driven by heart-failure events, whose triggering clinical diagnosis, treatment escalation, and hospitalization decisions were made by physicians aware of treatment assignment. The authors themselves acknowledge that this knowledge may have contributed to the lower heart-failure-event frequency in the CRT–ICD group.

The study also raises **some concerns** regarding allocation concealment because the concealment method was not reported. The deviation-from-intended-intervention and selective-reporting domains are judged at low risk of bias for the specified intention-to-treat primary result.
