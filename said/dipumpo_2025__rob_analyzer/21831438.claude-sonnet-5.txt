# ROB 2 Assessment Report: CAESAR Trial (Lee et al., 2011)

**Preliminary Considerations:**
- **Study design:** Individually-randomized parallel-group trial
- **Comparison:** Candesartan 16mg/HCT 12.5mg → 32mg/HCT 12.5mg (experimental) vs. Candesartan 16mg → 32mg monotherapy (comparator)
- **Outcome assessed:** Change in mean sitting DBP from baseline after 4 weeks (primary outcome); result: −17.8 (10.2) mm Hg vs. −14.1 (10.1) mm Hg, P=0.0028 (ITT population)
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)
- **Sources used:** Journal article, ClinicalTrials.gov registry record (NCT00621153) with posted results

---

## 1. Bias Arising from the Randomization Process

**Judgment: SOME CONCERNS**

**Motivation:**
- Sequence generation: The article states "mixed block randomization (4 and 6 blocks) method" was used — this confirms a random component (Signalling Q1.1 = Yes).
- Allocation concealment: No information is provided on how the allocation sequence was concealed from investigators/enrollers. There is no mention of central randomization, sealed envelopes, or any other concealment mechanism (Signalling Q1.2 = No information).
- Baseline imbalance: A statistically significant difference in age was found between groups (51.0 vs 47.8 years, P=0.02), explicitly noted by the authors as occurring "despite random allocation." While a single significant baseline difference could be compatible with chance, the authors' own emphasis on this discrepancy, combined with the use of blocked randomization (which can allow prediction of allocation, especially with small/fixed block sizes as used here — 4 and 6 blocks), raises some concern about whether concealment was adequately implemented.
- Per the RoB2 algorithm: absence of information on allocation concealment (NI) combined with an observed baseline imbalance in a variable (age) — since no other prognostic baseline variables showed significant imbalance and no other evidence for manipulation is present, this is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

**Limitation noted:** The trial report and protocol do not describe the method of allocation concealment; this ambiguity was resolved via the "no information" pathway, per RoB2 guidance.

---

## 2. Bias Due to Deviations from Intended Interventions (Effect of Assignment to Intervention)

**Judgment: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Motivation:**
- The study was explicitly an **open-label** design (confirmed by both the article and the registered protocol: "maskingInfo": "NONE"). Participants, carers, and outcome assessors (investigators measuring BP) were all aware of intervention assignment (Q2.1 = Yes; Q2.2 = Yes).
- Because this was an unblinded trial with a subjectively-adjudicated, operator-dependent outcome (blood pressure measurement, which can be influenced by expectation bias, digit preference, and other observer effects), there is a real risk that trial context (e.g., patient/physician expectation that combination therapy would work better) led to deviations from intended intervention — such as differential co-interventions, differential effort in BP measurement technique, or differential encouragement of compliance — that could affect the outcome (Q2.3 = probably yes, though not explicitly reported; Q2.4 = plausible, given known effects of unblinding on BP measurement in the empirical literature).
- No mention is made of any effort to standardize or blind BP assessment despite unblinding of treatment allocation, and no discussion of balance of these deviations between groups is provided.
- Analysis: An ITT analysis was reported as the primary result (Q2.6 = Yes), and this favors a lower risk classification for the statistical analysis portion, but the unblinded nature of a subjective, operator-measured continuous outcome without a placebo control is a well-recognized major source of bias for this domain, particularly amplified for BP trials.
- Per the RoB2 algorithm, when participants/personnel are aware of allocation and deviations related to trial context likely affected the outcome without evidence of balance, the default judgment moves toward "High" risk, especially since no mitigating blinding of outcome assessment or standardized BP protocol beyond routine care is reported.

**Limitation noted:** The article does not explicitly discuss whether deviations from protocol occurred because of the unblinded trial context, nor whether such deviations were balanced across groups. This is inferred based on the open-label design and the nature of the outcome.

## 3. Bias Due to Missing Outcome Data

**Judgment: SOME CONCERNS**

**Motivation:**
- Of 233 randomized patients, 227 took ≥1 dose; the ITT set for the primary outcome comprised 224/233 (96.1%), and specifically 111/117 (94.9%) in the combination group and 113/116 (97.4%) in the monotherapy group had primary outcome data at 4 weeks (Q3.1 = probably yes, borderline "nearly all").
- However, according to the ClinicalTrials.gov flow module, there were more discontinuations in the combination group (20/117, 17.1%) compared to the monotherapy group (15/116, 12.9%), with differing reasons: "Lost to Follow-up" was notably higher in the combination arm (5 vs. 1), while "Withdrawal by Subject" was similar (4 vs. 5) and "Adverse Event" discontinuations were comparable (3 vs. 2).
- This differential dropout pattern (particularly the imbalance in "lost to follow-up") raises the possibility that missingness could be related to the true value of the outcome (e.g., patients doing poorly might be more likely to be lost), though the difference is modest and reasons appear predominantly administrative/voluntary rather than clearly outcome-related (Q3.3 = probably no to unclear; Q3.4 = unlikely, no explicit evidence that missingness depended on true BP values).
- No sensitivity analyses were reported to test robustness to missing data assumptions.
- Given the reasonably high overall data completeness but the unexplained asymmetry in loss-to-follow-up between arms, this is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

**Limitation noted:** Specific reasons for "lost to follow-up" are not detailed in the article, making it impossible to definitively rule out a relationship with the outcome.

## 4. Bias in Measurement of the Outcome

**Judgment: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Motivation:**
- The outcome (sitting DBP/SBP) is an observer-reported outcome involving a moderate degree of judgement/technique (manual positioning, timing, cuff placement, and potential rounding/digit preference), measured using a semi-automated device but administered by an unblinded investigator (Q4.1 = No, the device itself is not inappropriate).
- Measurement methodology appears consistent across groups (repeated measures protocol, same investigator per patient) (Q4.2 = No, methods were not different between groups).
- Critically, outcome assessors (investigators taking BP measurements) were **not blinded** to intervention assignment, as the study was explicitly open-label (Q4.3 = Yes, aware).
- Because BP measurement — even automated — retains subjective components (e.g., patient positioning, timing relative to activity, encouragement/reassurance affecting patient anxiety/white-coat effect) and because knowledge of assignment to a "more intensive" combination therapy could plausibly influence how carefully or optimistically an unblinded assessor performs or records the measurement (Q4.4 = Yes, could be influenced).
- Given the strong a priori hypothesis of the sponsor/investigators that combination therapy would be superior, and the absence of any blinding of outcome assessors, it is likely that assessment of this outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received (Q4.5 = Yes, likely), particularly since this is an industry-sponsored trial with a directional hypothesis, and BP is known from empirical meta-epidemiological research to be susceptible to assessor expectation bias when unblinded.
- The authors themselves acknowledge this limitation directly: *"the open study design could be the potential source of bias regarding the safety profile"* — while stated regarding safety, this concern equally, if not more, applies to the primary efficacy (BP) outcome.

**Limitation noted:** The authors' own acknowledgment of open-label bias supports a "High risk" judgment for this domain, though they only explicitly linked it to safety, not efficacy, results.

## 5. Bias in Selection of the Reported Result

**Judgment: SOME CONCERNS**

**Motivation:**
- A registered trial protocol is available (NCT00621153) with pre-specified primary and secondary outcomes prior to unblinding of outcome data, and the primary outcome reported in the article (change in mean sitting DBP at 4 weeks) matches the pre-registered primary outcome (Q5.1 = Yes for this specific outcome, "probably yes" given consistency between registry and manuscript).
- The primary outcome measurement matches what was pre-specified (single DBP change measure, no alternative scales/definitions apparent), reducing risk of selection from multiple eligible outcome measurements (Q5.2 = No/unlikely for this primary result).
- However, several secondary outcomes registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (e.g., "Changes in Hs-CRP Level," "Compliance Levels") were listed as "NOT_POSTED" in the results database and are not reported at all in the published article, raising concern about selective outcome reporting at the study level (though this is technically an "outcome reporting bias" issue rather than strictly "selection of the reported result" for the specific DBP outcome being assessed here).
- For the specific numerical result being assessed (4-week DBP change), there is no clear evidence of selection among multiple analytic approaches (e.g., both ITT and PP analyses are reported concordantly, Table II), and results are consistent between the two (Q5.3 = No/unlikely).
- Given that: (a) the specific primary result assessed appears to align with pre-specified plans, but (b) there is incomplete transparency regarding whether the full pre-specified statistical analysis plan (SAP) was available/finalized before unblinding, and additional pre-specified secondary outcomes (Hs-CRP, compliance) were seemingly measured but not published, the overall judgment is "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

**Limitation noted:** Full statistical analysis plan (SAP) was not accessible; only the registry entry and journal article were available. The date-stamping confirming finalization of analysis plans prior to unblinding could not be verified.

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

# **Overall Judgment: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Rationale:**
Per the RoB 2 framework, an overall judgment of "High risk of bias" is warranted because:

1. **Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions)** was judged as **High risk**, driven by the open-label design combined with a continuous, operator/patient-influenced outcome (blood pressure) with no blinding and no reported balancing measures for potential trial-context deviations.

2. **Domain 4 (Measurement of the outcome)** was also judged as **High risk**, because the same lack of blinding directly extends to outcome assessment, and the authors themselves acknowledge the open-label design as a source of potential bias.

3. Additionally, **Domains 1, 3, and 5** were each judged as "**Some concerns**," further compounding the overall uncertainty regarding the trustworthiness of the effect estimate.

According to RoB 2 rules, a "High" risk-of-bias judgment in even one domain is sufficient to classify the overall result as "High risk of bias"; here, this criterion is met in **two independent domains** (2 and 4), both stemming from the fundamental lack of blinding in this open-label trial design combined with a moderately subjective, technique-dependent primary outcome (blood pressure). This substantially lowers confidence that the observed treatment effect (greater BP reduction with candesartan-HCT combination vs. monotherapy) is free from bias due to the unblinded trial context and outcome assessment.

**Key overall limitation:** The single most important source of bias in this study is its open-label design applied to a subjectively-influenced, continuous physiological outcome (BP), compounded by incomplete reporting on allocation concealment methods and a modest, unexplained age imbalance at baseline despite randomization.
