## RoB 2 assessment

**Study:** Huang et al., *Embolization combined with endoscopic variceal ligation for the treatment of esophagogastric variceal bleeding in patients with cirrhosis* (Chin Med J 2007;120:36–40).

**Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial.  
**Experimental intervention:** Histoacryl embolization of gastric fundus varices plus endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL) of esophageal varices performed during the same procedure.  
**Comparator:** Histoacryl embolization initially, with planned EVL two months later.  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Result assessed:** Emergency hemostasis: 90/90 (100.0%) in the combined immediate embolization-plus-EVL group versus 72/82 (87.8%) in the delayed-EVL control group.

The article itself was the available source. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospective trial registration is reported or available in the supplied material.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The authors state that “a random number table was produced through Microsoft Excel” and that patients “were divided into a control group and a therapy group following the random number table.” This describes a sequence generation process with a random component.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The report does not describe who generated the random-number table, who enrolled participants, who assigned treatment, or any mechanism to conceal upcoming assignments. There is no description of central randomization, independent allocation, opaque sequential envelopes, or another allocation-concealment procedure.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  Baseline data are presented for all 172 randomized patients. The groups were broadly similar in sex, age, active esophageal bleeding, red signs, Child–Pugh score, portal-vein width, number of esophageal varices, and gastroesophageal-varix classification. Although the distribution of esophageal-varix grades differs numerically between groups, the reported comparison is not statistically significant (P=0.779), and the article provides no evidence that the observed differences were incompatible with chance or indicated manipulation of randomization.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The random sequence was reported as being generated using a random-number table. However, allocation concealment is entirely unreported. Under RoB 2, absence of information on concealment prevents a low-risk judgment unless adequate concealment can reasonably be inferred, which cannot be done from this report. There is no convincing baseline evidence of a failed randomization process. Therefore, this domain raises **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The interventions were visibly and procedurally different: one group received immediate EVL during the embolization procedure, whereas the comparator group was scheduled to receive EVL two months later. No participant blinding is reported, and blinding would not be credible for this timing-of-procedure comparison.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The treating endoscopists necessarily knew whether EVL was performed immediately or deferred. No blinding of treatment personnel is reported.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  The report states that, in the control group, 10 patients with active esophageal variceal bleeding whose hemorrhage was “not controlled” received EVL before the planned two-month interval. This differs from the initially described comparator strategy. However, the report does not state whether these rescue procedures were prespecified management for failed embolization or deviations caused by the trial context. It also does not provide sufficient information about other concomitant treatments or protocol deviations.

- **2.4–2.5 Were such deviations likely to affect the result and balanced between groups? — Not applicable / no information.**  
  The relevant information required to establish trial-context deviations and their balance is not reported.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  The emergency-hemostasis outcome is reported using the full randomized group denominators: 90 patients in the therapy group and 82 in the control group. The article does not report that participants were excluded from this analysis or analyzed according to treatment received rather than randomized assignment.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
Participants and treating personnel were aware of treatment allocation. The article does not describe procedures to minimize differential co-interventions, additional endoscopic treatment, or other departures from the intended strategies. The report documents early EVL rescue in 10 control-group patients, but does not establish whether this was a prespecified failure-management procedure or a trial-context deviation. The numerical emergency-hemostasis analysis appears to retain all randomized participants in their assigned groups; nevertheless, insufficient reporting on deviations in this unblinded trial raises **some concerns**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for emergency hemostasis available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants? — Yes.**  
  Emergency-hemostasis data are reported for all randomized patients: 90/90 patients in the therapy group and 72/82 patients in the control group. No patients are described as missing from this outcome analysis.

- **3.2–3.4 — Not applicable.**  
  Because emergency-hemostasis data were reported for all randomized patients, further assessment of possible bias from missing outcome data is not required for this result.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The reported emergency-hemostasis result uses all 172 randomized patients, with no missing outcome data indicated. Therefore, missing data could not have materially biased this specific result. This domain is at **low risk of bias**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring emergency hemostasis inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  Emergency control of active variceal bleeding is clinically relevant and can be assessed during clinical and endoscopic management. The article provides no evidence that an unsuitable instrument or technically invalid measurement method was used.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of emergency hemostasis have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  Both groups underwent endoscopic assessment and treatment for active gastric fundus variceal bleeding. The report does not explicitly state that outcome-assessment procedures, timing, or criteria were standardized between groups; however, it does not report different formal ascertainment methods between groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Yes.**  
  The intervention was administered by endoscopists who necessarily knew whether immediate EVL had been performed. The report does not identify independent or blinded outcome assessors.

- **4.4 Could assessment of emergency hemostasis have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.**  
  The outcome was expressed as whether “hemorrhaging was controlled,” but the article gives no objective operational definition of emergency hemostasis, no prespecified assessment time point, no standardized criteria for determining control of bleeding, and no blinded adjudication. Determining whether bleeding was controlled may involve clinical and endoscopic judgement.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — No information.**  
  The report gives no evidence that outcome assessment was independent, blinded, standardized, or adjudicated. It also provides no evidence capable of excluding influence of treatment knowledge on the judgment that bleeding had been controlled.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
The trial was necessarily open-label, and the clinicians assessing and delivering care knew allocation. The principal result is based on a clinical judgment—“hemorrhaging was controlled”—without a reported objective definition, specified assessment window, blinded assessment, or independent adjudication. In this setting, knowledge of immediate versus delayed EVL could influence assessment of whether hemostasis had been achieved. Under the RoB 2 algorithm, when assessors are aware, the outcome could be influenced by that knowledge, and the report provides no information to establish that such influence was unlikely, the judgment is **high risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data producing this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial-registration record, or dated prespecified analysis plan is reported in the article.

- **5.2 Is the emergency-hemostasis result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The report does not define the assessment timing or measurement criteria for emergency hemostasis, and no prespecified source is available to determine whether other eligible time points or definitions were measured and analyzed.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses? — No information.**  
  The article does not report a prespecified statistical analysis plan. It provides simple group proportions and P values but does not establish whether alternative analysis approaches, definitions, or adjustments were conducted and selectively unreported.

**Motivation for judgment:**  
There is no evidence of a prospectively finalized analysis plan, and the available article does not allow verification that emergency-hemostasis definitions, time points, and analyses were selected independently of the data. The report does not provide affirmative evidence that the result was selectively chosen; therefore, high risk is not established. Nevertheless, the absence of prespecified analysis information creates **some concerns**.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgment — **High risk of bias**

The overall judgment is **high risk of bias** for the reported emergency-hemostasis result.

This follows directly from the **high-risk judgment in Domain 4 (bias in measurement of the outcome)**. The outcome assessors were aware of intervention assignment, and emergency hemostasis was not operationally defined or assessed using reported blinded, independent, or standardized methods. The result also has **some concerns** in the randomization, deviations-from-intended-interventions, and selection-of-reported-result domains.

According to the specified RoB 2 rule, a study is judged at **high risk of bias overall** when it is at high risk of bias in at least one domain.
