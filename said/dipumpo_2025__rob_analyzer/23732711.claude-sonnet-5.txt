# RoB 2 Assessment: Nambour Skin Cancer Prevention Trial (Sunscreen and Prevention of Skin Aging)

**Outcome assessed:** Change in photoaging (microtopography grade) from 1992 to 1996, comparing daily vs. discretionary sunscreen use (effect of assignment to intervention/ITT-type effect)

---

## 1. Bias Arising from the Randomization Process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Sequence generation: The article states participants were "randomly assigned...by using a computer-generated randomized list" by an investigator "who had no knowledge of the participants." This supports a judgment of **Yes** for random sequence generation.
- Allocation concealment: There is **no explicit description** of the mechanism used to conceal the allocation sequence from those enrolling participants (e.g., no mention of sealed envelopes, central randomization telephone system, or independent pharmacy dispensing). The paper only states that the randomization was performed by an investigator "who had no knowledge of the participants," which addresses lack of selection bias in sequence generation but does not clarify whether concealment from recruiters/participants at the point of enrollment was safeguarded. This is judged as **No information**.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows generally similar baseline characteristics between daily and discretionary sunscreen groups. However, the paper explicitly notes "slightly more people were randomly assigned to β-carotene than to placebo (P = 0.06)" — this pertains to the β-carotene factor, not the sunscreen factor, and is border-line non-significant, compatible with chance. For the sunscreen comparison specifically, no notable imbalances are highlighted. This supports **No** concern for imbalance for the sunscreen comparison.
- Because allocation concealment is not reported (No information) and there are no red-flag baseline imbalances for the sunscreen intervention, per the RoB2 algorithm, this combination (sequence random = Yes; concealment = No information; imbalance = No) maps to **Some concerns**.

---

## 2. Bias Due to Deviations from Intended Interventions (Effect of Assignment to Intervention)

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- **Blinding of participants/personnel:** This trial used open-label sunscreen allocation — the authors explicitly state "placebo sunscreen was considered unethical," so participants and those delivering the intervention (i.e., participants applying sunscreen themselves) were aware of allocation (daily vs. discretionary sunscreen). Thus, answer to signalling question 2.1/2.2 is **Yes** (aware).
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** Because participants knew their allocation, unblinded participants might alter behavior. However, the authors present objective evidence that reported sun exposure and other sun-protective behaviors (shade-seeking, hat use) were similar between groups ("Reported sun exposure was similar between the daily and discretionary sunscreen groups during the trial... P = 0.25"), suggesting deviations attributable to trial context (e.g., compensatory behavior) were unlikely to have differentially impacted the two groups. This supports a judgment of **No/Probably no** for consequential deviations affecting the outcome differentially.
- **Adherence:** Long-term adherence differed as expected between groups by design (77% vs. 33% applying sunscreen ≥3-4 days/week) — this reflects the intervention itself (regular vs. discretionary use) rather than a deviation from assigned intervention/protocol violation, and is appropriately captured within the ITT-type "assignment to intervention" effect being assessed.
- **Analysis approach:** The paper states, "Data were analyzed according to treatment as randomly allocated," consistent with an appropriate ITT approach for the effect of assignment to intervention.
- Overall, while participants were not blinded (a source of potential bias, especially since this is a subjective/observer-assessed but not fully objective outcome), the authors provide reassuring balance-of-behavior data reducing the likelihood of a differential trial-context effect. Given lack of blinding is unavoidable (placebo sunscreen deemed unethical) but with data suggesting balanced co-interventions/behaviors, we assign **Some concerns**, acknowledging some residual uncertainty because self-reported behavioral outcomes are themselves subject to bias and full assurance of no differential context effect cannot be firmly established.

---

## 3. Bias Due to Missing Outcome Data

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Of 903 eligible participants, 886 (98%) had at least one skin replica, but only 604 contributed replicas at **both** time points (1992 and 1996); 213 had only baseline (1992) data, and 69 had only follow-up (1996) data. This means **nearly one-third of participants (282/886, ~32%) had missing data at one time point**, which is a substantial proportion — well above the ~5% threshold generally considered negligible.
- The authors explicitly examined missingness: "participants who contributed only 1 [replica]... were more likely to have severe photoaging of the neck and 2 to 5 total sunburns," indicating that missingness may be related to prognostic factors associated with photoaging severity — raising the possibility that missingness could depend on the (true) outcome value.
- However, the authors report that "having a missing microtopography grade in 1996 was not associated with baseline microtopography grade" and that "treatment allocation was not associated with missing grades." This suggests missingness was **not differential between intervention groups**, reducing (but not eliminating) the likelihood of bias in the between-group comparison.
- The authors used GEE modeling (using all available data, an approach that can reduce but not eliminate bias from missing data if the missingness mechanism is not fully accounted for), performed a complete-case sensitivity analysis (n=604), and conducted multiple imputation — both sensitivity analyses were reported to be consistent with the main results. This constitutes reasonable evidence that the result was not substantially biased by missing data.
- Despite these reassurances, because (a) the proportion of missing data was large (~32%), (b) missingness was related to a prognostic factor (severity of photoaging/sunburn history, albeit not to treatment allocation or baseline grade specifically), some residual concern remains regarding whether the sensitivity analyses fully capture the plausible range of missing-data mechanisms. This supports a judgment of **Some concerns** rather than "Low risk."

## 4. Bias in Measurement of the Outcome

**Judgment: Low risk**

**Motivation:**
- **Appropriateness of measurement method:** The outcome (photoaging) was assessed using silicone skin-surface replicas graded on the validated Beagley and Gibson microtopography scale, which the authors and prior validation studies (cited: refs 19–21) have shown correlates well with histological photoaging (dermal elastosis) and clinical skin cancer risk. This is an appropriate, validated, objective measurement method (not inappropriate).
- **Comparability of measurement across groups:** The same replica collection and grading procedure was applied identically to all participants regardless of allocation, with no indication that ascertainment methods differed between arms.
- **Blinding of outcome assessors:** The article explicitly states that "experienced assessors who were unaware of treatment allocation graded replicas," confirming blinded outcome assessment. Additionally, intra- and inter-grader reliability was high (weighted κ 0.81 and 0.86), supporting measurement reliability.
- Because outcome assessors were blinded and using a validated objective grading instrument, knowledge of intervention assignment could not have influenced the assessment. This supports a judgment of **Low risk of bias** for this domain.

---

## 5. Bias in Selection of the Reported Result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- The trial is registered (Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry: ACTRN12610000086066), and the trial design/protocol was published previously (reference 25, Control Clin Trials 1994). This provides some basis for comparing intended versus reported outcomes.
- The primary outcome (change in microtopography grade) is clearly pre-specified and reported in a manner consistent with the described analysis plan (ordinal logistic regression via GEE), with statistical methods described in detail and multiple sensitivity analyses (complete case, multiple imputation) reported transparently and consistently.
- However, several aspects raise **some** — though not definitive — concern:
 - It is **not explicitly confirmed** whether the statistical analysis plan (specifically the GEE/REPOLR ordinal regression approach and the specific covariate-adjustment model - "Model 2") was finalized and date-stamped **before** unblinded outcome data were available, since this level of detail (a dated SAP) is not provided in the article.
 - Subgroup analyses by baseline photoaging severity (grades 3–4 vs. 5–6) were described as "exploratory," and it is unclear whether these were pre-specified in the original protocol or added post hoc based on observing an interaction. The authors themselves label this analysis "exploratory," raising the possibility that this particular breakdown (which showed a much stronger, statistically significant protective association in the milder-photoaging subgroup) may have been selected partly because of its interesting/significant finding, although the score test for heterogeneity itself was reported (P=0.51 for β-carotene interaction, non-significant for consistency across subgroups for sunscreen).
 - Two analytic models (Model 1, unadjusted; Model 2, adjusted for sunburns and neck photoaging) are presented, with results being highly consistent between them — this transparency mitigates concern of hidden multiplicity, but the fact that adjustment covariates were chosen based on their association with "missingness" (rather than pre-specified confounders) suggests some analytic flexibility.
- Given the lack of an explicitly available, date-stamped pre-specified statistical analysis plan for the primary outcome model and the post hoc framing of subgroup analyses by baseline severity, but with otherwise consistent, transparent reporting of primary results across multiple pre-specified sensitivity checks, this domain is judged as **Some concerns** rather than "Low" or "High."

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Rationale (per RoB 2 rules):**
Individual domain judgments were:
1. Randomization process: **Some concerns**
2. Deviations from intended interventions: **Some concerns**
3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**
4. Measurement of outcome: **Low risk**
5. Selection of reported result: **Some concerns**

According to the RoB 2 algorithm, an overall judgment of "High risk of bias" is warranted when a study is judged to have "some concerns" for **multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result," even in the absence of any single domain rated "High risk."** Here, four of the five domains (1, 2, 3, and 5) were rated "Some concerns." The cumulative effect of: (a) unclear allocation concealment, (b) an inherently unblinded intervention with only partial reassurance regarding balanced co-interventions/behaviors, (c) a substantial proportion (~32%) of participants with missing outcome data at one time point (with some evidence that missingness related to a prognostic factor, i.e., photoaging severity/sunburn history), and (d) uncertainty regarding whether the primary/subgroup analytic approach was fully pre-specified before unblinded data access — together substantially reduce confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported treatment effect (relative odds 0.76 [95% CI, 0.59–0.98]) for the sunscreen-photoaging outcome.

**Limitation to note explicitly:** This assessment is limited by the absence of the full original trial protocol/statistical analysis plan with clear date-stamping, and by incomplete reporting of the exact allocation-concealment mechanism in the article itself; these ambiguities could not be resolved from the text provided and are reported here as limitations of this assessment rather than assumptions.
