**1. Bias arising from the randomization process**
**Judgment:** Some concerns
**Motivation:** The study mentions that "Students were randomized into three groups using a computer program," which indicates a random sequence generation was used (Low risk for sequence generation). However, there is no information provided regarding the concealment of the allocation sequence before and until assignment (e.g., whether the computer program was administered centrally or remotely). Baseline characteristics (sex, age, school age) appeared balanced across groups, but the lack of clear allocation concealment methods raises some concerns.

**2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions**
**Judgment:** Low risk of bias
**Motivation:** Given the nature of the educational intervention (2D vs. 3D images vs. 3D printed models), blinding of the participants (students) and personnel (teachers) was inherently impossible. However, there is no evidence to suggest that there were any deviations from the intended interventions that arose due to the trial context. All students completed their assigned teaching module and the subsequent examination. The analysis appropriately included all 120 randomized participants, consistent with intention-to-treat principles.

**3. Bias due to missing outcome data**
**Judgment:** Low risk of bias
**Motivation:** The authors state that "a total of 120 medical students (54.2% females) participated, and no one dropped out." Since data for the outcomes (exam scores and time taken) were completely available for all randomized participants, there is no risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

**4. Bias in measurement of the outcome**
**Judgment:** Low risk of bias
**Motivation:** The primary outcomes were the sum score of correct answers on a multiple-choice anatomy quiz and the time taken to complete it. These are objective measures. Although the participants were aware of their intervention group, it is highly unlikely that this knowledge influenced their actual ability to select the correct anatomical answers on a structured quiz or the time recorded. The evaluative questions (pleasure, assistance, effect, confidence) were self-reported and could be influenced by lack of blinding, but for the primary objective knowledge and time outcomes, the measurement is appropriate and comparable across groups.

**5. Bias in selection of the reported result**
**Judgment:** Some concerns
**Motivation:** There is no mention of a pre-specified protocol or statistical analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. While the reported outcomes (score and time) clearly align with the stated objectives of the study, the absence of a registered protocol or analysis plan means it cannot be fully verified whether multiple analyses or different outcome measurements were explored and selectively reported based on statistical significance.

**6. Overall risk of bias**
**Judgment:** Some concerns
**Motivation:** The study is judged to raise "Some concerns" overall. This is driven by "Some concerns" in Domain 1 (lack of information on allocation concealment) and Domain 5 (lack of a publicly available, pre-specified protocol or statistical analysis plan to rule out selective reporting). The other domains are at low risk of bias. Confidence in the objective results (quiz scores and time) remains reasonably high, but the methodological reporting omissions represent limitations.
