# RoB 2 Assessment Report

**Preliminary Considerations**

- Study design: This study is described as an experimental intervention with random allocation ("crew members were divided at random into two groups"), but it does not strictly present as a conventional parallel-group RCT report with full CONSORT elements. The RoB 2 tool is applied to the randomized comparison component (Period B: allopurinol vs placebo).
- Experimental intervention: Allopurinol 300 mg/day, 3 h before racing
- Comparator: Placebo
- Outcome assessed: (1) Creatine kinase (CK) activity change; (2) Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activity change; (3) Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels change — pre- vs post- second round of Louis Vuitton Cup (Period B)
- Numerical result assessed: CK: allopurinol 445.8→333.6 U/L (p<0.05) vs placebo 275.8→237.3 U/L (ns); AST: no significant change either group; MDA: allopurinol 1.55→1.03 μM/mL (ns), placebo no change
- Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), as this is the only estimand reported
- Sources used: Journal article only (no protocol, no SAP, no trial registry record available/mentioned)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Signalling questions:**
- 1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — The text states "crew members were divided at random into two groups according to random numbers." No further detail is given on the method of random sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number table). Given the brevity, response: **Probably yes** (a random component is claimed).
- 1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled? — **No information**. There is no description of any allocation concealment mechanism (e.g., central randomization, sealed envelopes). Given the small, single-site, non-specialized research context (not run by a dedicated clinical trials unit) and no CONSORT-level reporting, this cannot be inferred as "probably yes."
- 1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Group composition by task intensity differed substantially between arms: allopurinol group (8 HIW, 4 MIW, 1 LIW) vs placebo group (2 HIW, 4 MIW, 4 LIW). This is a marked imbalance in a key prognostic factor (physical work intensity), which the paper itself demonstrates is strongly associated with muscle damage marker levels (baseline CK differed by >2-fold according to work-intensity group in Period A data). Baseline CK values in Period B also differ substantially between groups (445.8 vs 275.8 U/L at pre-race, a ~62% relative difference), consistent with imbalance in a known, important prognostic factor. Response: **Yes** — there is a clear imbalance in a key prognostic factor (task-intensity distribution) unlikely to be due to chance alone, particularly given the very small sample (n=13 vs n=10) and its known strong correlation with the outcome.

**Judgment:** Given no information on allocation concealment AND clear evidence of imbalance in a key prognostic factor (work intensity distribution, and correspondingly baseline CK) that is highly plausible to bias the result — **High risk of bias**.

**Motivation:** The trivial description ("divided at random into two groups according to random numbers") lacks methodological detail on sequence generation and, critically, no information on concealment is provided. Compounding this, the two arms had markedly different distributions of high-, medium-, and low-intensity task groups — a factor the authors' own data show to be strongly predictive of the outcome (CK/AST/MDA levels). Baseline CK differed substantially (445.8 vs 275.8 U/L) between arms before the intervention was administered, raising serious concern that randomization failed to produce comparable groups on this key prognostic variable, especially in such a small sample (n=23 total).

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention) — **SOME CONCERNS**

**Signalling questions:**
- 2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — The paper states "Sailors were blind regarding the group assignment," but no description of matching placebo formulation, packaging, or blinding of care providers/investigators administering the drug is given. Response: **Probably no** (claimed blinding, but no verification/detail provided).
- 2.2 Were carers/people delivering the intervention aware of assigned intervention? — No information on who administered allopurinol/placebo and whether they were blinded. Response: **No information**.
- 2.3–2.5: Given uncertain blinding of personnel, deviations from intended intervention arising from trial context cannot be excluded, but no evidence is presented that any occurred (e.g., no report of non-adherence, co-intervention imbalance, or contamination). Response: **No information/Probably no**.
- 2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — The analysis appears to include all randomized participants per group (13 vs 10, matching the reported denominators), consistent with an ITT-type approach, although no CONSORT flow diagram is provided and it is unclear if any additional dropouts occurred beyond the four who "declined to take part" (excluded before randomization/allocation, so this does not constitute post-randomization exclusion). Response: **Probably yes**.

**Judgment:** **Some concerns**, primarily due to insufficient reporting on blinding of care/administering personnel and absence of detail on protocol adherence, but no clear evidence of high risk of deviation-related bias.

**Motivation:** The claim that "sailors were blind" is not substantiated with methodological detail (e.g., identical placebo appearance, blinded dispensing). No information is given regarding whether those administering the drug/placebo or monitoring participants were blinded, and no discussion of non-adherence or co-interventions is provided. This ambiguity — explicitly reported here as a limitation of the assessment — prevents a "Low risk" judgment but does not reach "High risk" absent evidence of actual deviations.

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **SOME CONCERNS**

**Signalling questions:**
- 3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Four sailors "declined to take part in this phase of the study" prior to group allocation (this occurred before randomization into the 13/10 groups, so these are not missing outcome data in the randomized sample, but the description is ambiguous as to whether the "23" already excludes these four or whether further dropout occurred within the 13/10 groups during Period B). No CONSORT flow diagram is given, and it is unclear if all 23 analyzed participants completed both pre- and post-measurements. Response: **No information**.
- 3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No sensitivity analyses or additional evidence presented. Response: **No**.
- 3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Plausible (e.g., sailors experiencing adverse effects from allopurinol, or severe fatigue/injury, might be differentially lost), but not explicitly discussed. Response: **No information**.
- 3.4 Is it likely missingness depended on true value? — Insufficient information to judge as likely. Response: **No information**.

**Judgment:** **Some concerns.**

**Motivation:** The article does not provide a clear accounting (e.g., a flow diagram) of participant numbers from randomization through final analysis for Period B, nor does it explicitly confirm complete outcome data for all 23 analyzed participants. This ambiguity is noted explicitly as a limitation of this assessment. Absent evidence of a specific problem (e.g., differential or high dropout), a "High risk" judgment is not supported, but the lack of transparent reporting prevents a "Low risk" judgment.

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **LOW RISK OF BIAS**

**Signalling questions:**
- 4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — CK, AST measured spectrophotometrically via validated automatic analyzer with reported intra-assay CV (4.5%, 4.2%); MDA measured via validated HPLC method. These are objective, standard laboratory methods appropriate for the outcomes. Response: **No**.
- 4.2 Could measurement/ascertainment of outcome differ between intervention groups? — All samples processed identically, blood drawn at same time-points (3h post-race) for both groups. Response: **No**.
- 4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — These are objective, automated/instrumental laboratory assays (not participant-reported, not requiring clinical judgment); laboratory personnel analyzing samples had no direct interaction with participants and results are objective quantitative values. Response: **Probably no** (unlikely lab technicians were aware of/influenced by group assignment given the objective nature of biochemical assay).
- 4.4/4.5: Not applicable, as this is an observer-reported outcome not involving judgement (automated/spectrophotometric assay).

**Judgment:** **Low risk of bias.**

**Motivation:** CK, AST, and MDA are objective biochemical measures obtained via standardized, validated laboratory assays (spectrophotometry, HPLC) with reported quality-control parameters (intra-assay CV). These outcome measurements do not involve subjective judgement, and it is highly unlikely that laboratory analysis of blood samples was influenced by knowledge of participants' allopurinol/placebo assignment, as sample processing is objective and automated.

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **SOME CONCERNS**

**Signalling questions:**
- 5.1 Were the data analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No trial protocol, registration, or statistical analysis plan is available or referenced. Response: **No information**.
- 5.2 Is the result likely selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements? — CK, AST, and MDA are all reported for both periods; however, no time points other than pre/post are reported, and no other measurement instruments are described, reducing likelihood of selection among measurements. Response: **No information/Probably no**.
- 5.3 Is the result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — Only one analysis approach (unpaired/paired t-tests) is presented for each biomarker, with no evidence of alternative adjusted/unadjusted models or subgroup-selective reporting beyond the pre-planned HIW/MIW-LIW split, which appears consistently applied across periods. Response: **No information/Probably no**.

**Judgment:** **Some concerns.**

**Motivation:** No trial registration, protocol, or statistical analysis plan is available to confirm that outcome measurements and analyses were pre-specified prior to unblinding. While there is no overt evidence of selective outcome or analysis reporting (all three biomarkers are reported consistently for both study periods, including non-significant results such as AST and MDA changes with allopurinol), the complete absence of any pre-registration document makes it impossible to fully rule out selective reporting. This ambiguity is reported explicitly as a limitation of the assessment.

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement — **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Rationale:** Per RoB 2 rules, a judgement of "High risk of bias" in at least one domain mandates an overall "High risk of bias" judgement, regardless of the assessments in other domains. In this study:

- Domain 1 (Randomization process): **High risk of bias** — driven by absence of allocation concealment information combined with a demonstrable and substantial imbalance in the key prognostic factor (task-intensity subgroup distribution and corresponding baseline CK values) between the allopurinol and placebo arms, in an already very small sample (n=13 vs n=10).
- Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions): Some concerns
- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data): Some concerns
- Domain 4 (Measurement of outcome): Low risk
- Domain 5 (Selection of reported result): Some concerns

**Conclusion:** Because Domain 1 is judged at High risk of bias, and this is compounded by "Some concerns" in three additional domains (2, 3, 5), confidence in the reported comparative effect of allopurinol vs. placebo on muscle damage/oxidative stress markers is substantially undermined. The overall risk-of-bias judgement for this result is **High risk of bias**.

**Key limitations/ambiguities explicitly noted (as required, rather than assumed):**
1. No description of random sequence generation method or allocation concealment procedure was reported.
2. No trial protocol, registration record, or statistical analysis plan was available for cross-checking pre-specified outcomes/analyses.
3. No CONSORT-style flow diagram was provided to confirm the fate of all randomized participants through analysis.
4. Details on blinding of personnel administering allopurinol/placebo (versus only participant blinding) were not reported.
5. The very small sample size (13 vs 10) substantially limits confidence that randomization achieved balance, and the observed imbalance in task-intensity subgroup allocation is a documented, material threat to the validity of this comparison.
