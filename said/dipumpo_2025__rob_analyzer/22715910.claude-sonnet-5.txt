# ROB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Poureslami I, et al. "Effectiveness of Educational Interventions on Asthma Self-Management in Punjabi and Chinese Asthma Patients: A Randomized Controlled Trial." J Asthma 2012;49(5):542-51.

**Result assessed:** Effect of assignment to intervention (ITT-type effect) on primary outcomes — proper inhaler use technique, knowledge of asthma symptoms/triggers, and understanding of physician's instructions, comparing pretest to 3-month post-intervention follow-up, across the four groups (community video, knowledge video, both videos, pamphlet).

---

## 1. Bias Arising from the Randomization Process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Signalling Q1.1 (random sequence): The article states subjects were "assigned at random" to groups but provides **no description whatsoever of the method of sequence generation** (no mention of computer randomization, random number tables, etc.). The registered protocol also does not clarify this. → No information, but given this is a modest, investigator-driven academic trial without CONSORT flow diagram detail on randomization, a definitive "Probably yes" cannot be confidently assigned.
- Signalling Q1.2 (allocation concealment): **No information** is provided regarding how allocation was concealed from enrolling personnel/facilitators.
- Signalling Q1.3 (baseline imbalance): The authors explicitly state "initial pretest analyses showed no statistically significant differences among the four study groups and also between gender and ethnic groups." This supports no evidence of imbalance-related problems. However, Table 1 shows meaningfully different baseline group sizes (22, 21, 20, 22) and unequal ethnic distribution within groups (e.g., Group 3: 9 Chinese vs 11 Punjabi), which, combined with the small sample size, leaves residual uncertainty, though not to a degree suggesting failure of randomization.

Because there is no information on sequence generation or allocation concealment, and no imbalance evidence to raise a high-risk flag, this domain defaults to **Some concerns** per the RoB 2 algorithm (No information on concealment + no evidence of imbalance = Some concerns).

---

## 2. Bias Due to Deviations from Intended Interventions (Effect of Assignment to Intervention)

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- **2.1/2.2 (Awareness):** Participants and interviewers/facilitators were necessarily aware of intervention group assignment because the intervention itself (watching a specific video type or reading a pamphlet) could not be blinded. The protocol registration confirms "SINGLE" masking (participant only, and even this is implausible given the nature of the intervention — participants inherently know whether they watched a video or read a pamphlet). Personnel administering pretest/post-test interviews and delivering the intervention were not blinded to allocation.
- **2.3/2.4/2.5 (Trial-context deviations):** The article does not report whether any non-protocol interventions, cross-contamination between groups, or engagement effects occurred because of trial context. No information is given on whether groups received differential attention, encouragement, or exposure to materials beyond their assigned condition. This is a critical gap given the community-based, in-person, interactive nature of the intervention delivery (facilitators from the same community conducted interviews and might have varied their engagement).
- **2.6/2.7 (Analysis appropriateness):** Critically, **the paper does not report a true intention-to-treat analysis**. Of 92 randomized, only 85 completed the study (Figure 1 notes 3 excluded and 4 discontinued), and the primary analyses (ANOVA/ANCOVA) appear to be based on completers only, not on all randomized participants with imputation or appropriate handling of dropouts. There is no evidence that participants were retained in their randomized group with appropriate account for missingness, and no mention of sensitivity analyses.
- Given (a) lack of blinding of participants and personnel, (b) absence of information on whether trial-context deviations occurred and were balanced, and (c) an analysis strategy that appears to exclude participants who discontinued without appropriate ITT correction, this domain is judged **High risk**.

---

## 3. Bias Due to Missing Outcome Data

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- Of 92 enrolled/randomized participants, only **85 completed the study** (per the abstract and Figure 1 note: "3 were excluded and 4 discontinued intervention" — this only accounts for 7, yet the difference between 92 and 85 is also 7, but reporting is inconsistent and unclear which arm(s) these participants belonged to).
- The article provides **no breakdown of missing outcome data by study group**, no reasons for the specific dropouts/exclusions beyond the vague note in Figure 1, and no comparison of baseline characteristics between completers and non-completers.
- There is no discussion of whether missingness could be related to the true outcome value (e.g., participants with poorer asthma control or lower literacy dropping out preferentially from certain arms).
- No sensitivity analysis addressing missing data was performed or reported.
- Given the lack of information on the proportion of missing data by arm and reasons for missingness, combined with a non-trivial dropout rate (~7.6%) in a small sample (n=92, with individual arms as small as 20-22), missingness could plausibly bias the between-group and pre-post comparisons. This meets criteria for **High risk of bias** under signalling question 3.1 (data not available for all/nearly all) combined with no information to exclude dependence of missingness on true outcome value (3.3/3.4 default to high given no information provided).

## 4. Bias in Measurement of the Outcome

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- **4.1 (Appropriateness of measurement method):** The outcome measures (functional knowledge questionnaire, 9-step inhaler observation checklist) appear reasonable and were piloted, so this alone is not a major concern.
- **4.2 (Differential measurement/ascertainment):** Not clearly a source of differential bias since the same questionnaire/checklist was applied to all groups.
- **4.3 (Outcome assessor awareness):** This is the critical issue. The **outcome assessors were the same bilingual/bicultural community facilitators who conducted the intervention and the interviews**, and by necessity were aware of which intervention (video type vs. pamphlet) each participant received, since they delivered it. The article states facilitators were "not aware of the study hypothesis," but this does not equate to being unaware of intervention *assignment* — they clearly knew, as they administered the specific educational material.
- **4.4/4.5 (Potential and likely influence on assessment):** The primary outcomes (inhaler technique scored via an observational checklist, and knowledge/understanding assessed via interviewer-facilitated oral questionnaire) involve **considerable judgment** by the assessor (observer-reported outcomes involving judgment, and participant-reported outcomes elicited through an unblinded interviewer). Knowledge of which intervention a participant received (especially given the hypothesis-driven design comparing video vs. pamphlet) plausibly could influence how facilitators scored responses or observed technique, especially given facilitators were recruited from and invested in the community-participatory project.
- No independent, blinded outcome assessment or adjudication process is described (despite the claim of "two observers," both were part of the study team and non-blinded).

Given the outcome assessors were unblinded and outcomes required subjective judgment, and there is no evidence to exclude the likelihood of assessment being influenced by knowledge of intervention received, this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Bias in Selection of the Reported Result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- **5.1 (Pre-specified analysis plan):** There is a registered trial protocol (NCT01474928) with a stated primary outcome ("medication adherence"/understanding of physician instructions) and secondary outcomes. However, the published article reports **multiple additional outcomes** (inhaler technique, knowledge of symptoms, knowledge of triggers, knowledge of factors that worsen asthma) as apparently co-primary, and it is unclear whether all these analyses were pre-specified with a finalized statistical analysis plan before unblinded data were available. No SAP was found/referenced.
- **5.2 (Selective outcome measurement):** Multiple sub-outcomes and multiple subgroup stratifications (by gender, age, ethnicity, education) are reported with numerous p-values, some significant and some not, raising the possibility that subgroup findings reported were selected partially based on statistical significance (e.g., detailed reporting of "Chinese improved more than Punjabi," "Punjabi males more than Punjabi females," "patients <60 years improved more") without a clear a priori hypothesis structure for all these comparisons.
- **5.3 (Selective analysis):** The switch between ANOVA and ANCOVA, with covariates added post hoc ("Then interaction terms were added to see if the effect...differed"), suggests an exploratory, iterative analytic approach rather than one strictly pre-specified, increasing risk that the analyses reported were those yielding significant/interesting results.
- However, there is no direct evidence (e.g., discrepancy with a published protocol/registry outcome list) proving definitive selective reporting; the extensive subgroup reporting appears to stem from genuine exploratory secondary analyses acknowledged as such by the authors, rather than concealment of a primary null result.

Given the absence of a clear, dated, pre-specified statistical analysis plan and evidence of multiple exploratory subgroup/analytic strategies without clear pre-specification, but no clear proof of outcome suppression, this domain is judged **Some concerns**.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall Judgment: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Rationale (per RoB 2 rules):**
The overall judgement is driven by the rule that *"the study is judged to be at high risk of bias in at least one domain."* In this assessment, **three domains** (Deviations from Intended Interventions, Missing Outcome Data, and Measurement of the Outcome) were independently judged **High risk of bias**, and the remaining two domains (Randomization Process, Selection of Reported Result) were judged **Some concerns**. 

This combination — multiple high-risk domains plus additional domains with concerns — substantially undermines confidence in the reported treatment effects. The principal drivers of this judgment are:
1. Lack of blinding of participants, intervention deliverers, and (critically) outcome assessors, in a context where outcomes require considerable subjective judgment;
2. Absence of a true intention-to-treat analysis with unclear handling of the ~8% of randomized participants who did not complete the study;
3. No breakdown of missing data by arm or reasons for dropout, precluding assessment of whether attrition was related to the outcome;
4. Absence of clear pre-specification of the multiple outcome analyses and subgroup comparisons presented.

**Note on ambiguity/limitations of this assessment:** This judgment is based solely on the published journal article, its supplementary tables/figures, and the publicly available ClinicalTrials.gov registration (NCT01474928). No trial protocol document, statistical analysis plan, or CONSORT flow diagram with complete attrition detail by arm was available for review. Specifically: (a) the exact randomization method and allocation concealment procedure were not described in any available source; (b) the discrepancy between the stated primary outcome in the registry ("medication adherence") and the outcomes emphasized in the publication (inhaler technique, knowledge domains) could not be fully reconciled without the original protocol/SAP; (c) exact reasons and group-specific numbers for the 7 participants lost between randomization (n=92) and completion (n=85) were not reported. These ambiguities are reported as limitations of this assessment rather than assumptions, in accordance with RoB 2 methodology.
