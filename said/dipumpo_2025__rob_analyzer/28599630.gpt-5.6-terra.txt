## RoB 2 assessment

**Result assessed:** Effect of assignment to fish-oil supplementation (1.08 g EPA + 0.72 g DHA daily) versus soybean-oil placebo on the **primary outcome: prevalence of depressive symptoms at 4–6 weeks postpartum**, defined as **EPDS score ≥11**. The assessment targets the **intention-to-treat effect** in the 60 randomized women (fish oil: n=32; placebo: n=28).

**Sources used:** Published trial article, reported tables/flowchart information, and the supplied ClinicalTrials.gov record. The registry record is for the parent observational cohort, is retrospectively registered, and does not provide a randomized-trial protocol or statistical analysis plan.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The article states that eligible women “were randomly assigned” and that “randomization was performed by a researcher not involved in the data collection” after stratification by EPDS score and previous depression history. However, it does not state how the allocation sequence was generated: for example, computer-generated random numbers, random-number tables, random permuted blocks, or another method with a random component. Under RoB 2, the statement that a study was randomized is insufficient by itself to establish that the sequence was random.

- **1.2 Was allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The article identifies that the person performing randomization was not involved in data collection, but it does not describe a central allocation process, a secure computerized allocation system, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. It is therefore not possible to establish whether investigators enrolling women or determining eligibility could foresee the next allocation.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  Baseline data were reported for all 60 randomized women. There were no statistically significant baseline differences. Skin colour differed numerically (non-white: 59.4% fish oil versus 82.1% placebo; p=0.055), but this isolated difference is not sufficient evidence that the randomization process failed. The groups were otherwise broadly comparable for age, gestational age, education, income, parity, BMI, total dietary PUFAs, baseline EPDS score, and history of depression.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The reported baseline data do not indicate a failure of randomization. However, neither random sequence generation nor allocation concealment is described adequately. In particular, independence of the person who performed randomization from data collection does not establish allocation concealment. In accordance with RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment, with baseline balance not suggesting a problem, leads to **some concerns**.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  The study is explicitly described as double blind. Fish-oil and placebo capsules were reported to be identical in colour, shape, size, and packaging, and both were deodorized. These measures support successful participant blinding.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention? — Probably no.**  
  The article states that “participants and all research assistants and technicians responsible for running both the cohort study and the RCT were blinded to the study allocation.” The intervention was delivered in identical daily packages, supporting blinding of trial personnel.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The article reports discontinuations, losses, and imperfect adherence, but does not report differential non-protocol co-interventions, treatment switching, or trial-context deviations that would have occurred because participants or staff knew allocation. Participants were instructed not to change usual diet or use other supplements, and there is no reported evidence that this instruction was differentially breached by group.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  The authors state that the ITT population included “all women randomized, regardless of whether or not they dropped out or fully adhered to the intervention.” The primary results are reported for the ITT groups of 32 fish-oil and 28 placebo participants. Analysis according to random assignment is appropriate for the assignment effect. The separate per-protocol analyses do not replace the reported ITT analysis.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
This was a placebo-controlled, double-blind trial with physically comparable capsules. Although adherence was incomplete and some participants discontinued supplementation or did not receive it after randomization, these facts do not themselves introduce bias in the effect of assignment to intervention when the ITT effect is the target estimand. The article reports no evidence of unbalanced trial-context deviations likely to affect postpartum EPDS status. The stated ITT approach retained participants in their randomized groups. Therefore, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for the postpartum EPDS ≥11 outcome available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants? — Probably yes, but reporting is unclear.**  
  The ITT postpartum results are presented using the full randomized denominators: 8/32 (25.0%) in the fish-oil group and 7/28 (25.0%) in the placebo group. This presentation suggests that the postpartum binary outcome was available, or treated as available, for the randomized groups. The article also explicitly reports that two women in the fish-oil group missed the postpartum appointment. It does not clearly reconcile this statement with the T3 ITT denominators and percentages.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No information.**  
  The article does not describe an imputation method, a missing-data model, or a sensitivity analysis for the postpartum EPDS outcome.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  EPDS is a self-reported depressive-symptom outcome. Missing a postpartum appointment, ceasing participation, or being lost to follow-up could plausibly be related to women’s postnatal mental-health status. The article does not provide sufficient outcome-specific reasons for missing postpartum EPDS measurements to exclude this possibility.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — No information / probably no.**  
  The report does not establish that missingness was related to depressive symptoms, and the explicitly reported missed postpartum appointments were few. However, the reporting does not fully clarify which randomized women provided postpartum EPDS data, which were included in the stated ITT analysis, and how unavailable data were handled.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The main limitation is inconsistency and lack of transparency in the outcome-data reporting. The trial reports 32 completers, multiple post-randomization losses and discontinuations, and two missed postpartum appointments, while also presenting T3 ITT prevalence using all 60 randomized women as denominators. No procedure for handling missing EPDS data is reported. The amount of definitively documented missing postpartum outcome data may be small, but it is not possible to determine clearly whether all randomized women contributed observed T3 EPDS data or whether missing values were handled analytically. Since missingness in a postpartum self-reported depression outcome could plausibly relate to the true outcome, this raises **some concerns**, rather than establishing low risk.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  The outcome was measured using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), a validated self-report scale. The article reports use of the Portuguese EPDS and cites validation data. The prespecified postpartum threshold of EPDS ≥11 was used to identify moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  EPDS was administered at the same scheduled time points in both groups, including 4–6 weeks postpartum. No group-specific outcome-assessment procedure is reported.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  For this participant-reported outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor. Participants were reported to be blinded, and the two capsule types were identical in relevant appearance and packaging. Research staff were also reported to be blinded.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary outcome was assessed with the same validated instrument and scheduled assessment procedure in both groups. The placebo-controlled double-blind design, including identical capsules, supports that participants did not know their allocated intervention while completing the EPDS. There is no evidence that outcome ascertainment differed by group. Therefore, risk of bias in measurement of the outcome is **low**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were data analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The trial registration number was retrospectively registered on 24 May 2012. The supplied registration record describes the parent study as a prospective observational cohort of 299 women, not the nested randomized placebo-controlled trial. It does not specify the trial’s randomized arms, its EPDS endpoint, the EPDS ≥11 threshold, timing of analysis, ITT method, model specifications, subgroup analyses, or a statistical analysis plan. No trial protocol or date-stamped SAP for the RCT is available in the supplied material.

- **5.2 Is the assessed result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements? — No information.**  
  EPDS was measured at multiple times: cohort baseline (T0), second trimester/trial baseline (T1), third trimester (T2), and postpartum (T3). The article identifies prevalence of EPDS ≥11 as the primary outcome and reports results at all time points. However, without a prospectively finalized RCT analysis plan, it cannot be verified that the postpartum time point and EPDS threshold were pre-specified before unblinded data were available.

- **5.3 Is the assessed result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses? — No information.**  
  The article reports unadjusted comparisons, longitudinal random-intercept logistic regression, linear mixed-effects models, adjusted and unadjusted models, ITT analyses, per-protocol analyses, and subgroup analyses among women with a previous history of depression. The report does not provide evidence that the particular primary analysis was selected on the basis of results, but the absence of a prospective RCT analysis plan prevents exclusion of selective analysis reporting.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The publication labels postpartum EPDS ≥11 prevalence as the primary outcome and reports the main null finding. There is no direct evidence that the reported primary result was selectively chosen because of its magnitude or statistical significance. Nonetheless, a pre-specified, prospectively finalized RCT protocol or SAP is unavailable. The supplied registry entry is retrospectively registered and describes only the parent observational cohort. Since there were multiple measurement times and multiple possible analyses, the risk of selective selection cannot be excluded. This domain is judged as **some concerns**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**  
The study is at low risk of bias regarding deviations from intended interventions and measurement of the EPDS outcome. However:

1. there is insufficient information about random sequence generation and, especially, allocation concealment;
2. reporting of the availability and handling of postpartum EPDS data is unclear in the context of post-randomization losses and two missed postpartum assessments; and
3. no prospectively finalized randomized-trial protocol or statistical analysis plan is available to verify that the reported result was selected according to pre-specified analysis intentions.

No individual domain is judged at high risk of bias. However, the concerns in the randomization process, missing outcome data, and selection of the reported result mean that the result is not at low risk of bias across all domains. Under the specified RoB 2 rules, the appropriate overall judgement is therefore **Some concerns**.
