# ROB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Three-dimensional printing models improve understanding of spinal fracture—A randomized controlled study in China

**Study design:** Individually-randomized parallel-group trial (three arms: CT, 3D, 3Dp)

**Outcome assessed:** Sum score of correct answers to the 10 key anatomical questions (primary knowledge outcome)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- The article states, "Students were randomized into three groups using a computer program," which supports a response of "Yes" to sequence generation being random.
- However, no information is provided about the method of **allocation concealment**. There is no description of whether the allocation sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants until assignment (e.g., no mention of central randomization, sealed envelopes, or any concealment mechanism).
- Baseline characteristics (age, sex distribution, school age) were balanced across groups (Table 2), with no statistically significant differences reported, suggesting no evidence of a problem with the randomization process from an imbalance perspective.
- Per the RoB 2 algorithm: when the allocation sequence is random but there is no information on concealment, and baseline imbalances are compatible with chance, the judgment defaults to "Some concerns."

**Limitation reported:** The absence of any description of allocation concealment procedures is a specific ambiguity that could not be resolved from the text; this is reported as a limitation rather than assumed.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- This is an educational intervention trial where blinding of participants (students) and personnel (teachers) to intervention group was not feasible, and blinding was not attempted or claimed. Students inherently knew which teaching modality (CT, 3D, or 3Dp) they were using.
- However, lack of blinding here is not expected to lead to deviations from intended intervention arising from the "trial context" in the sense addressed by this domain. There is no evidence of contamination between groups (e.g., students accessing another group's teaching modality), no crossover, and no non-protocol interventions described. Each subgroup was assigned dedicated resources (six computers or 12 models) and taught in isolation from other groups.
- The analysis appears to include all randomized participants in each group as assigned ("no one dropped out"), consistent with an appropriate (ITT-consistent) analysis.
- There is no indication of failures in implementing the module protocol; each group received the specified module (CT images, 3D images, or 3Dp models) as intended, with a teacher providing the same interpretive information across groups.

**Limitation reported:** The paper does not explicitly discuss safeguards against contamination between groups (e.g., students discussing content across groups), though the subgroup separation described mitigates this concern to some extent.

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- The article explicitly states: "a total of 120 medical students (54.2% females) participated, and no one dropped out." All 120 randomized students (40 per group) completed the examination paper, and outcome data (correct answers) are reported for all participants in Table 3 (N = 22 females + 18 males in CT; 21 + 19 in 3D; 22 + 18 in 3Dp), matching the total randomized per group.
- Since outcome data were available for 100% of randomized participants, missing outcome data could not have biased the result.

---

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- The outcome (score on 10 anatomical questions) was assessed via a standardized written examination paper administered identically to all three groups, using the same 10 questions with predefined correct answers (Table 1). This is an objective, low-judgement outcome measure (multiple-choice/binary answers), reducing the potential for assessor-related measurement bias, as scoring against a fixed answer key involves little subjective judgement.
- However, outcome assessors (i.e., those grading the examination) are not explicitly described, nor is it stated whether grading was performed blind to group allocation. Given that group assignment is often identifiable from students' seating arrangement or module type during testing, and that grading of open-format questions (even multiple-choice/short answer) could theoretically be influenced if graders were aware of the group, this raises minor concern.
- Since the correct answers are fixed and objectively verifiable (Table 1), it is unlikely that assessor awareness of allocation could have materially influenced scoring. However, no explicit statement of blinded marking is provided, resulting in "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."
- The measurement method itself (a validated set of anatomical questions with fixed correct answers) is not inappropriate for the outcome domain being measured.

**Limitation reported:** No information was provided regarding whether examination papers were scored by assessors blinded to group assignment; this ambiguity is reported as a limitation of the assessment rather than assumed to be adequate or inadequate.

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- No trial protocol, registration record, or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is referenced or available in the article. There is no evidence that outcome measures and analyses were specified in advance of unblinded data becoming available.
- The primary outcome (sum score of 10 questions) and secondary/evaluative outcomes (4 evaluation questions) appear to be reported in full, and results are presented for all pre-defined comparisons (TM group and sex) without apparent selective omission of measures within the described outcome domain.
- However, the absence of any pre-specified protocol or trial registry means it cannot be confirmed that the reported analyses (e.g., choice of post hoc tests, specific comparisons highlighted such as "3D vs CT" and "3Dp vs CT" but not all pairwise sex-by-group interactions) were not selected based on the results obtained.
- The reporting of only certain post hoc pairwise comparisons (e.g., highlighting the male vs. female difference in the 3D group specifically, while other subgroup comparisons are less emphasized) raises some concern that the emphasis on statistically significant findings could reflect selective highlighting, though the underlying data appear to be fully reported in the tables.

**Limitation reported:** Lack of a publicly available trial protocol or registration record prevents verification of whether the analysis was conducted in accordance with a pre-specified plan; this is reported as a limitation rather than assumed either way.

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
Applying the RoB 2 rules for overall judgement:
- Domain 1 (Randomization process): Some concerns
- Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions): Low risk
- Domain 3 (Missing outcome data): Low risk
- Domain 4 (Measurement of the outcome): Some concerns
- Domain 5 (Selection of the reported result): Some concerns

No domain was judged to be at **High risk of bias**. However, **three domains** (1, 4, and 5) raise **"Some concerns."** According to RoB 2 guidance, "Some concerns" in multiple domains may lead to an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" if this substantially lowers confidence in the result. In this case, the concerns identified (lack of reported allocation concealment, lack of explicit blinded outcome assessment, and absence of a pre-specified analysis plan) are related to reporting gaps rather than clear evidence of manipulation, selective reporting, or detected bias. Given that:
1. The outcome measure is objective and low-judgement (fixed correct-answer key），minimizing the practical impact of measurement domain concerns,
2. Missing data and deviation-from-intervention domains are at low risk, and
3. The concerns in randomization and reporting stem primarily from underreporting rather than demonstrated flaws,

the cumulative effect of these concerns is judged as **not substantially lowering confidence** to the level of "High risk," but is sufficient to prevent a "Low risk of bias" judgement overall.

**Final overall judgement: Some concerns.**

**Reported limitation:** This assessment is limited by the absence of a trial protocol, registration, or statistical analysis plan, and by incomplete reporting of allocation concealment procedures and blinding status of outcome assessors. These ambiguities could not be resolved through further inquiry and are reported here as specific limitations of the risk-of-bias assessment, in accordance with instructions not to make assumptions beyond what is explicitly reported in the article.
