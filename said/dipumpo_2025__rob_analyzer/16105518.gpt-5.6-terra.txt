## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

This is an individually randomized, three-group, parallel-group educational trial of **functional-order (whole-task) practice**, **random part-practice**, and **blocked part-practice** in 28 novice medical students.

Because RoB 2 is outcome- and result-specific, this assessment targets the principal reported positive result for the comparison:

- **Experimental intervention:** functional-order/whole-task practice  
- **Comparator:** blocked part-practice  
- **Outcome/result assessed:** change from pre-test in the operation-specific **checklist score**, analysed across immediate and 30-minute delayed post-tests. The article reports greater improvement in the functional-order group than the blocked group (mean difference scores 5.72 [SE 0.83] versus 2.22 [SE 0.84]; group effect *P*<0.05).
- **Effect of interest:** effect of assignment to the practice schedule (intention-to-treat effect).

Only the journal article was available. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, registry entry, or other pre-results source was provided. The article does not identify a single primary outcome. Therefore, this RoB 2 assessment applies specifically to the checklist result above; the final-product, global-rating, and drilling-time results could require separate assessments.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The report states that, after the pre-test, participants “were randomly assigned to three experimental groups.” However, it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated. There is no information on a computer-generated sequence, random-number table, random permuted blocks, or another process containing a random component.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The report provides no information on allocation concealment. There is no description of central randomization, an independent allocator, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another method that prevented personnel enrolling participants from knowing the forthcoming assignment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No information.**  
  The article reports that all 28 participants were first- or second-year medical students inexperienced in bone plating, and that all completed a pre-test. However, it does not report baseline characteristics or pre-test performance separately by randomized group. Consequently, it is not possible to examine whether groups were balanced at baseline or whether any imbalance suggested a failure of randomization.

**Motivation for judgement**

The study is described as randomized, but the sequence-generation method and allocation-concealment procedure are not reported. There is also no group-specific baseline information to assess whether randomization produced comparable groups. There is no direct evidence that the allocation process was non-random or manipulated, so a high-risk judgement is not justified. Nevertheless, the missing information about key safeguards in the randomization process raises **some concerns** under RoB 2.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Participants necessarily knew whether they practised the complete task in its functional order, practised the five skills in random order, or practised three consecutive trials of each skill in a blocked schedule. The interventions were distinct educational practice arrangements and could not realistically be concealed from participants.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably yes.**  
  An orthopedic faculty member “circulated within the group systematically to provide feedback to the trainees” during acquisition. Since feedback was delivered within the practice groups and the schedules differed visibly, the faculty member delivering feedback was likely aware of the practice schedule.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  The article specifies the intended duration and frequency of skills practice and states that all participants practised each skill three times for 2 minutes and 10 seconds. However, it does not report whether the practice schedules were implemented as intended in each group, whether feedback differed between groups in content or amount, whether non-protocol co-interventions occurred, or whether participants received practice beyond that specified by the intervention.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  The reported analysis compared randomized practice groups using mixed ANOVAs of change from pre-test to each post-test. The report does not describe analyses that reassigned participants according to the intervention actually received or exclude participants because of non-adherence. Although the report does not explicitly label the analysis as intention-to-treat and does not give a participant flow diagram, the group analysis appears to be by assigned practice schedule. Any issue arising from unreported missing data is addressed in Domain 3.

**Motivation for judgement**

Participants and the faculty member delivering feedback were aware of allocated practice schedules. Awareness alone does not establish bias for the effect of assignment, but it makes differential feedback, additional practice, or other trial-context deviations possible. The report does not provide sufficient information to determine whether deviations from the intended schedules occurred, whether feedback was truly comparable between groups, or whether any such deviations could have affected the checklist outcome. The analysis appears appropriate for assignment to intervention, but the absence of information on deviations leads to **some concerns**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No information.**  
  The paper states that 28 students consented and were randomly assigned, but it does not report the number allocated to each group, the number who completed immediate and delayed post-tests, the number included in each checklist analysis, or whether any outcome data were missing. No CONSORT-style participant flow diagram is provided.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No sensitivity analysis, imputation approach, analysis addressing missing outcome data, or other evidence demonstrating robustness to missing data is reported.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — No information.**  
  The article does not report whether checklist scores were missing, why measurements might have been missing, or whether missingness differed between groups. Therefore, it is not possible to exclude the possibility that absence of outcome data was related to performance, for example if poor performance, fatigue, inability to complete testing, or withdrawal affected availability of post-test checklist scores.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — No information.**  
  Reasons for missing outcome data and group-specific amounts of missingness are not reported.

**Motivation for judgement**

The paper does not report the participant flow from randomization through immediate and delayed testing, nor the denominators used for the checklist analysis. Therefore, it is impossible to establish that outcome data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants. It is also impossible to determine whether missingness was balanced, whether it was related to checklist performance, or whether it could have changed the group comparison. Under RoB 2 guidance, absence of information on the extent of missing outcome data usually leads to a **high risk of bias** judgement for this domain.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  The checklist was developed from actions identified by a panel of three orthopedic surgeons as necessary for effective performance of the procedure. A structured, operation-specific checklist is a plausible method for evaluating task performance. The report does not establish formal validity or reliability for this particular checklist, but it does not provide evidence that the method was unsuitable for the outcome it was intended to assess.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  All participants completed the same whole bone-plating task at the pre-test, immediate post-test, and delayed post-test. The same broad assessment procedure was used across groups, and the article does not report group-specific measurement methods or test conditions.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — No information.**  
  “Two experienced orthopedic surgeons” evaluated participants at each test phase, but the article does not state whether they were blinded to group allocation, whether assessment occurred independently, or whether assessors had access to information that could reveal the practice schedule.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably yes.**  
  The checklist involved assessment of detailed, operation-specific procedural actions. Such ratings involve observer judgement. If assessors knew whether a participant had received whole-task, random, or blocked practice, that knowledge could influence assessment.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — No information.**  
  The report offers no blinding procedures, assessor instructions, inter-rater reliability data, independent adjudication process, or other safeguard against assessor expectations. It therefore does not permit a determination that outcome assessment was protected from knowledge of intervention allocation.

**Motivation for judgement**

The checklist measurement itself is not evidently inappropriate, and the post-test task appears to have been administered similarly across groups. However, the outcome is based on observer ratings involving judgement, and the article does not report whether the orthopedic-surgeon assessors were blinded to practice allocation. No information is provided to show that knowledge of the educational intervention could not, or did not, influence checklist scoring. According to the RoB 2 algorithm, lack of information about blinding and about the influence of knowledge on a judgement-based outcome leads to **high risk of bias** in outcome measurement.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The report describes the statistical analyses but does not provide a protocol, statistical analysis plan, registry entry, date-stamped pre-specified analysis plan, or statement that the analysis plan was finalized before outcome data were examined.

- **5.2 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  Participants were assessed at pre-test, immediate post-test, and delayed post-test. The article presents a mixed ANOVA using two post-test change scores, but no pre-specified hierarchy or rationale identifies which time point or contrast was primary. The report also contains multiple performance outcomes: checklist scores, final-product analysis, global ratings, and drilling time. Although these are distinct measures rather than necessarily interchangeable measures of one outcome domain, the available report does not permit confirmation that the checklist result was selected independently of its statistical findings.

- **5.3 Is the reported result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The article reports change-from-baseline scores analysed by mixed ANOVA. It does not provide a pre-specified rationale for choosing change scores rather than, for example, analysis of post-test scores adjusted for baseline performance, nor does it report whether alternative analytic choices were examined. There is no direct evidence that the reported analysis was selected because it was favourable; however, the absence of a pre-specified analysis plan prevents this possibility from being excluded.

**Motivation for judgement**

The report does not provide pre-results documentation specifying the primary outcome, primary time point, planned group contrasts, or planned analysis. The paper reports several outcomes, of which only checklist and final-product results showed significant group effects, while global ratings and drilling times did not. This pattern alone is not proof that the reported checklist result was selected based on its findings, so a high-risk judgement is not warranted. However, the lack of a protocol or analysis plan and the presence of multiple outcomes, time points, and possible analyses produce **some concerns** about selective reporting of the assessed result.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

The overall judgement is **high risk of bias** because:

1. **Domain 3 (missing outcome data)** is at **high risk of bias**: the paper does not report the number of randomized participants with available checklist data at either post-test, losses/exclusions, reasons for missingness, or the analysed denominators; and  
2. **Domain 4 (measurement of the outcome)** is at **high risk of bias**: the judgement-based checklist outcome was scored by orthopedic surgeons, but blinding of assessors to the allocated practice schedule and safeguards against expectation-related scoring bias were not reported.

In addition, there are **some concerns** regarding the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and selection of the reported result. Under the specified RoB 2 overall-judgement rule, high risk of bias in at least one domain requires an overall judgement of **high risk of bias**.
