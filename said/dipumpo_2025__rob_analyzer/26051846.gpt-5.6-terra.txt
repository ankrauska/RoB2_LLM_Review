## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

- **Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial (99 participants).
- **Result assessed:** The **between-group effect on pain measured by the visual analogue scale (VAS) immediately post-treatment**, specifically the comparison of **iontophoresis with dexketoprofen versus conventional ultrasound**. The reported post-treatment difference was 1.2 VAS points in favour of ultrasound (reported 95% CI: 0.2 to 2.2 when expressed as ultrasound minus iontophoresis; *P* = 0.012).
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment to intervention** (intention-to-treat effect).
- **Sources used:** Article, reported tables/figures, and ClinicalTrials.gov record NCT01748188. No full protocol, statistical analysis plan, or supplementary participant-flow figure was available in the supplied material.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states that participants were assigned using “a numbered list generated through the random permuted blocks method,” applied by a statistician not involved in data collection or analysis. Random permuted blocks is a random allocation method.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The report identifies who generated the allocation list but does not describe how allocation was implemented at recruitment. There is no statement of central allocation, an independent allocation service, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another procedure that would prevent recruiters or treating personnel from foreseeing the upcoming assignment. Separation of the statistician from data collection/analysis does not itself demonstrate allocation concealment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  The groups were of similar size (32, 33, and 34 participants), consistent with the intended approximately equal allocation. Table 1 shows no statistically significant differences in reported baseline characteristics: age, sex, symptom duration, dominant-side involvement, or lesion category. The reported baseline VAS, DASH, and CMS values were also stated to be similar between groups. No reported imbalance clearly suggests a failure of randomization.

**Judgement: Some concerns.**

**Motivation:** Random sequence generation was adequately described, and reported baseline data do not indicate a randomization failure. However, allocation concealment was not reported. Under RoB 2, lack of information on concealment, despite otherwise compatible baseline characteristics, raises **some concerns** rather than supporting a low-risk judgement.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  The trial is described as single-blind, and the trial registry specifies that participants were masked. The article does not describe the practical method used to maintain participant masking, which is a reporting limitation, but the registry explicitly identifies participants as the masked group.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention? — Yes.**  
  The physiotherapists delivered distinct interventions: ultrasound, phonophoresis with dexketoprofen, or iontophoresis with dexketoprofen. Those delivering these treatments necessarily knew which modality they administered.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  All groups received a prespecified standardized treatment sequence: exercises, the allocated physical agent, and cryotherapy. The article states that physiotherapists followed standardized protocols and did not collect outcome data or evaluate results. It also states that all participants completed 20 sessions. No non-protocol co-interventions, treatment switching, differential additional care, or protocol-implementation failures arising from the trial context are reported.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  The analyses compared the randomized treatment groups using ANOVA and repeated-measures ANOVA. The article reports 99 randomized participants and states that all completed the 20 treatment sessions; it does not report crossovers or post-randomization exclusions from the treatment groups. The available report is therefore consistent with analysis according to randomized assignment. The separate uncertainty regarding whether all outcome data were available is addressed under Domain 3.

**Judgement: Low risk of bias.**

**Motivation:** Treating physiotherapists knew the allocated intervention, but this alone does not establish bias for the effect of assignment. The interventions were protocolized, all participants reportedly completed treatment, common exercise and cryotherapy were provided in every group, and the article reports no trial-context deviations likely to affect pain differently between groups. The reported group-based analysis is consistent with estimation of the effect of assignment. The incomplete description of the masking procedure is a reporting limitation but does not, on the information available, demonstrate deviations from the intended interventions.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No information.**  
  The study randomized 99 participants and states that all participants completed 20 treatment sessions. However, it does not report the number of participants with VAS data at the post-treatment assessment, the number with VAS data at the 1-month assessment, or the number included in each analysis. The supplied article does not include a participant flow diagram, numbers analysed at each time point, losses to follow-up, withdrawals, exclusions, or reasons for missing outcome data. The article refers to a supplementary selection/recruitment figure, but this figure was not supplied.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No sensitivity analysis, imputation analysis, weighting approach, or other analysis addressing missing VAS data is reported. No evidence is presented showing that the result is robust to plausible missing outcome values.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  VAS is a participant-reported pain outcome. For such a follow-up outcome, non-completion or non-attendance could plausibly be related to the participant’s pain status, treatment response, dissatisfaction, or clinical deterioration. The report provides no information that would exclude this possibility.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — No information.**  
  The report provides no numbers or reasons for missing VAS data by group. Therefore, the actual relationship between missingness and pain outcome cannot be evaluated.

**Judgement: High risk of bias.**

**Motivation:** The report does not provide the information needed to establish that VAS data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants. The statement that all participants completed the 20 treatment sessions is not equivalent to reporting complete post-treatment outcome data or complete analysis populations. Because no post-treatment VAS denominators, attrition data, reasons for missingness, or robustness analyses are reported, and because missing participant-reported pain data could depend on pain status, the RoB 2 algorithm leads to **high risk of bias** in this domain. This is a limitation arising from incomplete reporting; it does not establish that outcome data were actually missing, but it prevents a lower-risk judgement.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  Pain was measured using a 0–10 VAS, a conventional participant-reported measure of pain. The report does not identify an inherent inadequacy of the VAS for the stated outcome.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  VAS was assessed at the same scheduled time points—baseline, post-treatment, and 1 month post-treatment—in all three randomized groups. No differential ascertainment method or differential follow-up schedule is reported.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — No information.**  
  For VAS, the participant is the outcome assessor because pain is participant-reported. The registry identifies participants as masked, whereas the article states that assessments were undertaken by a blinded rehabilitation doctor but does not clearly state that participants themselves remained blinded or explain how masking was maintained despite visibly and perceptibly different modalities and treatment durations. Thus, the available sources do not provide a clear, consistent account sufficient to establish that the VAS outcome assessors were unaware of allocation.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.**  
  Pain on a VAS is subjective and participant-reported. Knowledge or beliefs about receiving ultrasound, drug phonophoresis, or drug iontophoresis could influence reported pain.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  The trial registry reports participant masking, and all groups received an active physiotherapeutic treatment plus the same exercises and cryotherapy. However, the article does not explain masking procedures or verify their success. The available information therefore does not justify a high-risk judgement, but it does not support low risk either.

**Judgement: Some concerns.**

**Motivation:** The measurement method and timing were comparable and appropriate. Nevertheless, the result is based on a subjective participant-reported outcome. The report is ambiguous about who was blinded: the registry says participants were masked, while the article specifically identifies the rehabilitation doctor as blinded and does not provide a masking method for participants. Since the outcome could be influenced by awareness of treatment but actual influence is not established, this domain raises **some concerns**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The trial registry specifies pain measured by VAS as the primary outcome and DASH and Constant scores as secondary outcomes. However, the supplied registry record does not specify the assessment time point for the primary analysis, the statistical model, handling of missing data, whether analysis would use post-treatment values or change from baseline, covariate adjustment, or planned pairwise comparisons. No dated statistical analysis plan was supplied.

- **5.2 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — Probably no.**  
  For the VAS pain outcome, the article reports results at all stated measurement occasions: baseline, post-treatment, and 1 month post-treatment. There is no direct evidence that the immediate post-treatment VAS comparison was selectively reported from among unreported eligible VAS time points or pain instruments.

- **5.3 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The article describes repeated-measures ANOVA, between-group ANOVA, and post-hoc Scheffé tests, but no pre-specified analysis plan is available to determine whether this reported result was selected from multiple analyses, models, outcome metrics, adjustment approaches, or missing-data approaches. The registry is too nonspecific to resolve this issue.

**Judgement: Some concerns.**

**Motivation:** There is no direct evidence that the reported VAS result was selectively chosen, and the VAS data are displayed at each of the stated time points. However, there is no sufficiently detailed, date-stamped pre-specified analysis plan. The registry does not define the primary time point or analysis method, and the report does not establish that the reported between-group comparison was the sole eligible analysis. Therefore, **some concerns** remain.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement — **High risk of bias**

**Overall judgement: High risk of bias.**

**Motivation:** The trial is judged at **high risk of bias in Domain 3 (missing outcome data)** because the available report does not establish the completeness of post-treatment VAS data, does not report the analysed sample size or reasons for missing data, and does not provide analyses demonstrating that missing data could not have biased the result. Under RoB 2, a high-risk judgement in any domain leads to an overall **high risk-of-bias** judgement for the assessed result.

Additional concerns are present regarding allocation concealment (Domain 1), possible influence of uncertain participant masking on participant-reported pain (Domain 4), and absence of a sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan (Domain 5).
