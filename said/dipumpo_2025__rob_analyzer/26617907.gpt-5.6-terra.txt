## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

- **Study design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial.
- **Experimental intervention (Group A):** Percutaneous microwave ablation.
- **Comparator (Group B):** Laparoscopic resection.
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to microwave ablation versus laparoscopic resection (intention-to-treat effect).
- **Result assessed:** **3-year survival**, reported as 15/45 (33.33%) in Group A and 17/45 (37.78%) in Group B (Table 4).
- **Source assessed:** The supplied journal article only. No protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial-registry record, or other prespecified analysis document was available in the supplied material.

The assessment is necessarily specific to the reported 3-year survival result. Risk-of-bias judgements for recurrence and complications could differ, especially for measurement of recurrence, which is less objectively defined in the report.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The Methods state that the 90 participants “were divided into group A and group B (n=45) by using a random number table.” Use of a random-number table is a random component in sequence generation.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The article does not report who generated the random-number table, who enrolled participants, how allocation was communicated, or whether investigators could foresee upcoming assignments. There is no description of central allocation, independent allocation, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another allocation-concealment mechanism.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  The article reports broadly similar distributions of sex, age, and tumour size between groups and states that there were no statistically significant differences in the reported “general information.” No clear imbalance demonstrating failure of randomization is reported. However, baseline reporting is incomplete: the reported numbers for single and multiple nodules sum to only 35 participants in each group, rather than 45. Thus, the baseline data do not fully account for all randomized participants.

**Motivation for judgement**

Although the reported use of a random-number table supports genuinely random sequence generation, allocation concealment is wholly unreported. Without information on concealment, it cannot be established that enrolment personnel were unable to predict or manipulate allocation before assignment. The incomplete accounting of baseline nodule status is an additional reporting limitation, although it does not itself demonstrate a major baseline imbalance. In accordance with RoB 2, random sequence generation combined with absent information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The interventions were percutaneous microwave ablation and laparoscopic resection. The article does not report participant blinding, and the very different nature of these procedures makes participant blinding implausible based on the information reported.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The clinicians delivering microwave ablation and laparoscopic surgery necessarily knew which procedure they were providing. No blinding of care personnel is reported.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  The report does not state whether participants crossed over between procedures, received alternative treatments, underwent incomplete or modified procedures, received differential co-interventions, or discontinued the allocated strategy. It also does not report whether any deviations were related to the conduct of the trial.

- **2.4 and 2.5 — Not applicable.**  
  Because the existence of trial-context deviations was not reported, their effect on survival and balance between groups cannot be assessed.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  Table 4 retains 45 participants in each randomized group for the survival analysis, which is consistent with analysing participants in their assigned groups. The article states that “all cases were followed up,” and no post-randomization exclusions or as-treated analyses are reported. However, an explicit intention-to-treat analysis is not stated.

**Motivation for judgement**

Participants and treatment personnel were aware of treatment allocation. For the effect of assignment, lack of blinding does not by itself establish bias; the relevant issue is whether trial-context deviations occurred and affected the outcome. The article provides no data on crossovers, implementation failures, non-protocol treatments, or differential postoperative care. The use of the randomized denominators in Table 4 provides some support for an assignment-based analysis, but the absence of information about deviations means that bias from this source cannot be excluded. Therefore, this domain is judged as raising **some concerns**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably yes.**  
  The article states that “all cases were followed up for 1-3 years by telephones and outpatient services.” Table 4 reports 3-year survival using the original randomized denominators of 45 participants in each group: 15/45 in Group A and 17/45 in Group B. No participant losses, withdrawals, or exclusions from the 3-year survival analysis are reported.

- **3.2–3.4 — Not applicable.**  
  Since the report presents 3-year survival data using all randomized participants in both groups, further missing-data questions are not required under the RoB 2 algorithm.

**Motivation for judgement**

For the assessed survival outcome, results are presented for all 45 randomized participants per group, with no stated losses to follow-up or exclusions. The report does not provide a participant flow diagram or reasons for any potential missing data, and it describes follow-up as lasting “1-3 years,” which does not explain how uniform 3-year survival status was established. Nevertheless, the reported 3-year survival counts explicitly use the full randomized denominators. On the evidence reported, missing outcome data are not shown to be sufficient to materially bias this result. This domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  The outcome is survival at 3 years. Survival is an objective outcome, and the article reports follow-up through telephone contact and outpatient services. No evidence is presented that the survival outcome was measured with an unsuitable method.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The same stated follow-up approaches—telephone and outpatient follow-up—were reported for “all cases.” The article provides no indication that survival ascertainment procedures differed by allocated group.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Probably yes.**  
  The article does not describe blinded outcome assessment. Given the procedural differences and absence of a blinding statement, awareness cannot be excluded.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — No.**  
  For 3-year survival, the outcome is all-cause survival/death, which is an observer-reported outcome involving little or no judgement. Knowledge of whether a participant underwent microwave ablation or laparoscopic surgery is not expected to alter whether the participant was alive.

**Motivation for judgement**

The report does not establish blinded outcome assessment. However, the assessed endpoint is survival, an objective outcome for which outcome-assessor knowledge of treatment assignment is not plausibly capable of changing the underlying status of alive or dead. The article does not report differential ascertainment methods between groups. Therefore, for this specific 3-year survival result, bias in outcome measurement is judged at **low risk of bias**.

**Motivation for judgement**

However, no document establishes the investigators’ prespecified outcome definitions, time points, or statistical methods before unblinded outcome data were available. There is also no basis for determining whether the presented unadjusted analysis was selected from multiple analyses. There is no direct evidence that the result was selected because of its direction, magnitude, or statistical significance; therefore, the domain is not judged high risk. Nevertheless, the absence of a pre-specified analysis plan creates **some concerns**.
