## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

RoB 2 is result-specific. I assessed the randomized comparison in **racing period B**: **allopurinol 300 mg/day versus placebo**, for the reported **change in serum creatine kinase (CK) from before to after the second round of the Louis Vuitton Cup**. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to allopurinol versus placebo** (intention-to-treat effect).

Period A is an observational within-crew comparison and is not the randomized comparison assessed here.

A material reporting ambiguity is present: the article states that crew members were randomized, then that four sailors “finally declined” participation, and reports 13 participants receiving allopurinol and 10 placebo. It does not state the originally randomized allocation, the treatment group of the four who declined, whether they declined before or after allocation, or provide a participant flow diagram.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states: “crew members were divided into two groups according to random numbers.” This describes a random component in sequence generation.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  No information is provided on who generated the random numbers, who held the allocation sequence, whether allocation was centrally administered, or whether allocation was concealed from persons enrolling sailors or administering study treatment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  The randomized groups differed in the distribution of work-intensity roles: the allopurinol group comprised 8 high-intensity workers, 4 medium-intensity workers and 1 low-intensity worker, whereas the placebo group comprised 2 high-intensity workers, 4 medium-intensity workers and 4 low-intensity workers. Baseline CK was also higher in the allopurinol group (445.8 U/L) than in the placebo group (275.8 U/L). These differences are potentially relevant because work intensity was associated with CK levels in period A. However, the report gives no formal baseline comparison for the randomized groups, no data on all key baseline characteristics by treatment arm, and no evidence that the observed imbalance was incompatible with chance in this small sample.

**Motivation**

Random sequence generation is reported, but allocation concealment is entirely unreported. Under RoB 2, lack of information on concealment raises **some concerns** when there is not clear evidence of a failure in randomization. The imbalance in work roles and baseline CK reinforces concern about comparability but, from the information reported, does not by itself establish that randomization was compromised.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  The article states that “Sailors were blind regarding the group assignment.” The study used a placebo comparator.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention? — No information.**  
  The article does not state whether investigators, clinical personnel, personnel dispensing tablets, laboratory personnel, coaches, or other personnel involved in treatment delivery were blinded.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  The article provides no adherence data, no information on whether the tablets were taken as intended, and no report of co-interventions or deviations from protocol.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — No.**  
  The article reports separate before-and-after comparisons within each group: CK decreased significantly in the allopurinol group (445.8 to 333.6 U/L; *p* < 0.05), whereas the within-group change in the placebo group was not statistically significant (275.8 to 237.3 U/L; *p* > 0.05). This does **not** estimate the randomized allopurinol-versus-placebo effect. A valid analysis for the effect of assignment should compare the randomized groups, for example by comparing post-race CK adjusted for baseline CK or comparing change in CK between groups. The article does not report such a between-group treatment-effect estimate.

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of failure to analyse participants in the groups to which they were randomized? — Probably yes.**  
  Four sailors reportedly declined participation after the statement that crew members had been randomly divided into groups. Only 23 of 27 crew members are reported in the two analysed groups. Their allocated groups, outcome data, and reasons for non-inclusion by group are not reported. In a randomized sample of only 27 sailors, exclusion of four participants could substantially affect the result, particularly given the baseline imbalance in CK and work-intensity roles.

**Motivation**

Although sailors were reportedly blinded, the analysis used does not appropriately estimate the effect of randomized assignment to allopurinol versus placebo. The study’s conclusion is based on significance testing within each arm rather than a randomized between-group comparison. In addition, four sailors were excluded from the intervention phase after randomization was described, without sufficient information to establish that analysis retained all randomized participants. These are material problems for the intended intention-to-treat effect.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  The study began with 27 crew members. In period B, four sailors “finally declined to take part,” leaving 13 allopurinol and 10 placebo participants. Thus, outcomes are reported for 23 rather than all 27 crew members described as having been randomized.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No sensitivity analysis, imputation, inverse-probability weighting, or other analysis addressing the four absent participants is reported.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — No information.**  
  The report does not state when the four sailors declined relative to randomization and treatment, their assigned groups, their baseline CK values, their work roles, their reasons for declining, or whether any post-race blood samples were obtained. Therefore, it cannot be determined whether their absence was unrelated to CK outcomes.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — No information.**  
  The reported information is insufficient to assess this. The absence of group-specific reasons and outcome data prevents a conclusion that missingness was unrelated to outcome.

**Motivation**

Four of 27 sailors were not included in the intervention-phase analysis, approximately 15% of the crew. In this very small trial, that number cannot be regarded as too small to affect the estimated effect. The study does not report the randomized allocation or outcome status of those four sailors, nor any analysis demonstrating robustness to their exclusion. Under RoB 2, this lack of information on potentially consequential missing outcome data supports a **high risk of bias** judgement.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  CK activity was measured spectrophotometrically using commercially available reagents and an automatic analyser. The reported intra-assay coefficient of variation for CK was 4.5%. No evidence is reported that the measurement method was unsuitable for serum CK.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The report describes one laboratory method for CK measurement and does not indicate that different methods, thresholds, or time points were used in the allopurinol and placebo groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — No information.**  
  Laboratory-assessor blinding is not explicitly reported.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  CK is an objectively measured laboratory enzyme activity obtained using an automatic analyser. The analytical measurement itself does not involve subjective judgement that would plausibly be influenced by knowledge of treatment assignment.

**Motivation**

The measured outcome was an objective laboratory value, assessed with the same described automated spectrophotometric procedure. Although blinding of laboratory staff is not explicitly stated, knowledge of treatment allocation would not plausibly influence the automated CK result. Therefore, risk of bias in measurement of CK is judged **low**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospective trial registration is cited or provided. The article does not establish that the reported analyses were prespecified before outcome data were available.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  CK was measured before and after racing, but no prespecified primary time point, primary CK metric, or hierarchy of outcome measurements is reported. The information is insufficient to exclude selection among possible CK measurements or time points.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  Several eligible analytic approaches were available, including comparison of post-race CK between groups, comparison of baseline-adjusted post-race CK, and comparison of within-person changes between groups. The article reports separate within-group pre/post comparisons but does not provide a prespecified plan or explain why this analysis was selected. There is no direct evidence that selection was based on the results, but selective reporting cannot be excluded.

**Motivation**

There is no protocol, registry entry, or dated statistical analysis plan to verify that the reported CK analysis was planned before outcome data were available. The article also uses a within-group significance approach rather than reporting the most relevant randomized between-group effect. This does not demonstrate selective reporting, but it leaves **some concerns** under RoB 2.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation**

The result is at **high risk of bias** because:

1. **Domain 2 is at high risk of bias:** the reported analysis does not estimate the randomized allopurinol-versus-placebo effect and there is potential for substantial impact from post-randomization non-inclusion of four sailors.
2. **Domain 3 is at high risk of bias:** outcome data are not reported for all randomized sailors, and the report does not provide enough information to determine whether the missingness could have biased the CK result.
3. There are additionally **some concerns** about allocation concealment and selection of the reported analysis.

According to the specified RoB 2 overall-judgement rules, high risk of bias in at least one domain requires an overall judgement of **high risk of bias**.
