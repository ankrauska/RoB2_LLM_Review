## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

- **Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group, double-blind randomized-withdrawal relapse-prevention trial.
- **Result assessed:** Primary outcome, **time to OCD relapse during the 24-week double-blind period**, comparing continuation of escitalopram with switch to placebo. The reported Cox-model estimate was a hazard ratio of **2.74 for placebo versus escitalopram** (*p*<0.001), indicating more relapse under placebo.
- **Effect of interest:** **Effect of assignment to intervention** at randomization: assignment to continue escitalopram at the fixed dose versus assignment to the protocol-specified taper/switch to placebo.
- **Sources available for this assessment:** The submitted journal article, including its described tables and figures. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, registry entry, or other pre-specified analysis document was provided.

This assessment applies to the randomized, responder-enriched population (320 participants who responded during the preceding open-label escitalopram phase), not to all patients initially entering the open-label phase.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The report states that eligible responders were assigned to escitalopram or placebo “according to a **computer-generated randomisation list**.” This is an explicitly random sequence-generation method.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  The report states that the randomization-series details “were unknown to any of the investigators” and were contained in “**sealed opaque envelopes**.” At each centre, sequentially enrolled participants received the lowest available randomization number. Although the report does not state all extended safeguards for envelopes—such as tamper-proof seals or confirmation that envelopes were opened only after irreversible participant registration—the reported opaque-envelope system, the investigators’ lack of access to the sequence, and sequential assignment support a judgement that allocation was probably concealed.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between groups suggest a problem with randomization? — No.**  
  The report explicitly states that there were no differences in demographic and clinical parameters between groups at randomization. The reported demographic data are compatible with this: mean age was 35.8 versus 35.4 years, male sex was 52.2% versus 49.1%, and other reported characteristics were similar. There is no reported imbalance indicating a failure of randomization.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The allocation sequence was computer generated. Allocation concealment was reported through opaque sealed envelopes with the sequence unknown to investigators, and baseline characteristics did not indicate a problem with randomization. The lack of fuller technical details about envelope safeguards is a reporting limitation, but the information provided supports adequate concealment rather than a material risk of bias.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  The study drugs were described as tablets of identical appearance, taste, and smell. The report states that participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire study.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  The report states that all study personnel were blinded throughout the study, and the randomization series was unknown to investigators.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No.**  
  No deviations caused by knowledge of allocation, trial personnel behaviour, or other trial-context effects are reported. The one-week reduction from 20 mg escitalopram to 10 mg before placebo was a specified feature of the placebo-assignment strategy, not a deviation from it. Likewise, withdrawal after a participant met relapse criteria was specified by the protocol.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  The report states that efficacy analyses were conducted using an ITT population. The randomized group sizes and the ITT population are both reported as 320 participants (163 escitalopram and 157 placebo), indicating that the primary comparison included all randomized participants. The primary time-to-event analysis used the log-rank test and Cox proportional-hazards model according to randomized assignment.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The double-blind phase used matching placebo tablets, and both participants and study personnel were reported to be blinded. There is no report of differential co-interventions, treatment switching, or other deviations arising from the trial context that could bias the effect of assignment. The primary analysis was reported as ITT and was conducted according to randomized groups.

There is an internal wording ambiguity in the Results section: it states that the ITT population “comprised 320 patients due to the exclusion of two patients that were randomised without having met the Y-BOCS response criterion.” As written, this is not fully coherent because 320 is also the total number reported as randomized. However, the reported randomized totals equal the ITT totals, and no numerical evidence is presented that eligible randomized participants were excluded from the primary analysis. This ambiguity does not provide evidence of a material analysis-related bias in the reported primary result.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably no.**  
  The primary outcome was time to relapse. Participants who relapsed were withdrawn, which is appropriate because relapse is the event of interest. However, during the double-blind period, withdrawals for reasons other than relapse occurred in 13/163 (7.9%) escitalopram participants and 14/157 (8.9%) placebo participants. These participants were censored in the time-to-relapse analysis and therefore had incomplete subsequent relapse follow-up.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The report does not provide an analysis demonstrating robustness of the primary result to informative censoring or to missing outcome data among participants withdrawn for non-relapse reasons. The reported analyses censoring relapses during the first 7 or 14 days after randomization address possible discontinuation effects, not the potential effect of non-relapse withdrawals and censoring.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  In a relapse-prevention study of OCD, withdrawal before the end of follow-up could plausibly be associated with worsening symptoms or evolving relapse status, even where relapse had not yet been formally recorded. The report does not provide sufficient individual-level reasons or timing for all non-relapse withdrawals to exclude this possibility.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably no.**  
  The proportions of non-relapse withdrawal were very similar between groups (7.9% and 8.9%). The report also states that adverse-event withdrawals were few (two on escitalopram and three on placebo) and that the non-relapse withdrawal rates were comparable. These observations do not establish that censoring was non-informative, but they do not make it likely that outcome missingness depended on the unobserved relapse outcome in a materially differential way.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The amount of non-relapse withdrawal was not negligible for a time-to-event analysis, and participants withdrawn before study completion were censored. No sensitivity analysis was reported to evaluate whether plausible outcomes among censored participants could alter the primary result. Although withdrawal proportions were similar between trial groups and there is no clear evidence that missingness was outcome-dependent, this cannot be excluded from the available report. This produces **some concerns**, rather than high risk, because the article does not show differential withdrawal or reasons strongly indicating that missingness was likely related to unobserved relapse.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  Relapse was prospectively defined as either a ≥5-point increase from randomization in Y-BOCS total score or investigator-judged lack of efficacy. The Y-BOCS was assessed repeatedly, and the report states that only investigators who had participated in rater training before participant inclusion were permitted to rate patients. The same investigator assessed each participant at visits whenever possible.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  Both randomized groups had the same scheduled assessment visits: at weeks 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 after randomization and every four weeks thereafter until the final double-blind dose. The same outcome instruments and relapse definition were applied to both groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of intervention received? — No.**  
  The report states that all study personnel and participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire study. Investigators assessing Y-BOCS and making the clinical lack-of-efficacy judgement were therefore reported to be blinded.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable.**  
  Because outcome assessors were reported to be blinded, differential outcome assessment due to knowledge of assigned intervention is not supported.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The principal relapse outcome was based almost entirely on the defined Y-BOCS criterion: 116 of 119 relapses (97%) met this criterion. The Y-BOCS was assessed by trained raters, at equivalent scheduled times in both groups, and assessors were reported to be blinded. Although the alternative “unsatisfactory treatment effect” component involves clinical judgement, it accounted for only three reported relapses and was also evaluated under blinded conditions. There is no evidence that outcome ascertainment differed across groups or that knowledge of treatment assignment influenced outcome assessment.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data producing this result analysed according to a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The article describes the time-to-relapse analysis as “prospectively defined” and identifies it as the primary efficacy analysis. However, no dated protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial registry entry, or other document finalized before unblinded outcome data were available was supplied. The article alone does not establish that the analysis plan was finalized before access to unblinded outcome data.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The primary outcome is specifically described as time to relapse based on a defined relapse criterion over 24 weeks. Nevertheless, the report also presents a post-hoc analysis restricted to Y-BOCS-defined relapses and describes analyses using 7-day and 14-day censoring periods. Without an externally available pre-specified plan, it cannot be verified whether alternative eligible outcome definitions or time-to-relapse specifications were considered and selectively reported.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The report presents several analyses related to relapse, including log-rank testing, Cox modelling, crude proportions, per-protocol analysis, post-hoc restriction to Y-BOCS-defined relapse, and analyses censoring early relapses. These analyses do not, by themselves, demonstrate selective reporting, particularly because the primary log-rank analysis is clearly identified. However, in the absence of a protocol or SAP finalized before unblinding, it cannot be confirmed that the reported primary result was selected without knowledge of the findings.

**Motivation for judgement**  
The article identifies the primary outcome and analysis clearly, but the available material does not include a pre-unblinded, date-stamped analysis plan. The presence of additional post-hoc and sensitivity analyses does not establish that the primary result was selected on the basis of its statistical significance or direction. Therefore, there is no basis for a high-risk judgement. However, low risk cannot be established because the pre-specified analysis intentions cannot be independently verified. This results in **some concerns**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation for overall judgement**  
The study is at low risk of bias in the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and outcome measurement domains. However:

1. **Missing outcome data:** participants who withdrew for non-relapse reasons were censored in the time-to-relapse analysis, and no analysis was reported to establish that this censoring could not materially bias the result; and  
2. **Selection of the reported result:** no protocol, registry record, or finalized statistical analysis plan was available to verify that the primary time-to-relapse result was analysed according to pre-specified intentions established before unblinded data were available.

These concerns do not provide evidence of high risk of bias in any individual domain. They also do not, on the information available, substantially lower confidence enough to justify an overall high-risk judgement. Therefore, under RoB 2 criteria, the appropriate overall judgement for the reported primary time-to-relapse result is **Some concerns**.
