# ROB 2 Assessment Report

**Study:** Omega-3 supplementation from pregnancy to postpartum to prevent depressive symptoms: a randomized placebo-controlled trial

**Outcome assessed:** Prevalence of EPDS ≥11 (primary outcome) at postpartum (T3), Intention-to-Treat analysis, overall sample (fish oil vs. placebo)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (ITT)

---

## Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Sequence generation: The report states randomization was performed "by a researcher not involved in the data collection using the participant identification (subject ID) after stratification for EPDS score and previous history of depression." No description of the actual method used to generate the random sequence (e.g., computer-generated, random number table) is provided → No information on random component (Signalling Q1.1: No information, leaning "Probably yes" given stratified design context, but insufficiently detailed).
- Allocation concealment: No explicit description of the concealment mechanism (e.g., sealed envelopes, central allocation) is provided. Given that supplements were dispensed in individual pre-packaged capsules and both participants/personnel were blinded, concealment is plausible but not explicitly confirmed → No information (Q1.2).
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows a significant difference in skin color between groups (White: 40.6% fish oil vs 17.9% control; p=0.055, borderline) and imbalance in education (p=0.070) and parity (p=0.138), though none reached the conventional 0.05 threshold except skin color trended close. No imbalance reached strong statistical significance (all p>0.05), so baseline differences appear compatible with chance, though the skin-color difference is a marginal concern given the very small sample (n=60).
- **Conclusion:** Absence of detailed information on sequence generation and allocation concealment, combined with borderline baseline imbalance in skin color, leads to a judgment of "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk," as there is insufficient information to confirm adequate concealment despite plausible randomization.

## Domain 2: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- **Blinding:** The trial was double-blind; participants and personnel (including research assistants) were blinded to allocation. Capsules were identical in color, shape, size, and packaging (fish oil vs. soybean oil placebo) — Q2.1/Q2.2: "No" (probably not aware).
- Given successful blinding, deviations from intended intervention arising from the trial context are unlikely (Q2.3: Probably no).
- **However**, a substantial issue arises in the analysis: the study explicitly performed and reported comparisons in **"per-protocol"** analyses alongside ITT, and importantly, a large fraction of randomized participants (10/60, 16.7%) never received the intervention after randomization due to losses occurring between randomization and supplementation start (transfers, miscarriage/stillbirth, exceeding gestational window, withdrawal). While the authors describe their approach as "ITT," this population differs from a strict ITT population (all who initially consented and were randomized): they explicitly note these women "did not receive the intervention" and were seemingly excluded from the analytic ITT population reported in Table 1 (n=60 total in flowchart, but n=32+28=60 reported in Table 1 — consistent with full randomized sample). Reviewing Fig. 1, the ITT analysis appears to include all 60 randomized women appropriately.
- **Appropriate analysis (Q2.6):** The study reports both ITT and per-protocol analyses, with ITT analyses retaining the full randomized sample principle. This is appropriate for estimating the assignment effect.
- **Concern:** Given the double-blind design, deviations from intended intervention due to trial context appear minimal. However, the report does not clearly document whether non-protocol interventions (e.g., other supplements) were monitored/prevented equally in both arms, and compliance, while similar between groups (~74%), was less than optimal (about 25% non-adherence on average), raising some possibility of non-differential dilution of effect — though since this is addressed via the appropriate ITT analysis, it does not elevate risk substantially.
- **Conclusion:** Overall "Some concerns" due to incomplete reporting on how deviations/non-protocol interventions were monitored despite blinding, and lack of clarity on whether findings were driven by unbalanced dropout patterns after randomization (see Domain 3 also).

## Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- Of 60 randomized women, only 32 completed the trial to the T3 (postpartum) endpoint — an overall loss of 46.7%, which is substantial and well above the "small" threshold that would exclude concern.
- **Differential attrition:** Losses were not perfectly balanced between arms. In the fish oil group: 6 lost immediately post-randomization + 1 declined + 7 dropped during intervention + 1 excluded + 2 missed postpartum visit = considerable attrition. In control: 4 lost immediately + 5 dropped + 1 excluded + 1 stillbirth. While numerically similar in magnitude, reasons differed (e.g., stillbirth/miscarriage occurred in both groups but numbers/reasons are not symmetric), and the proportions retained differed: fish oil retained 32→ final report suggests roughly 22-23 completers vs control ~17, according to Table 3/4 per-protocol n's (fish oil n=15, control n=17 at postpartum per-protocol for overall sample) — indicating an important difference in completion rates between arms (~47% retention in fish oil vs ~61% in control based on per-protocol n's relative to initially randomized 32 and 28 respectively).
- **Reasons for missingness:** Reasons include transfer of care, miscarriage/stillbirth, exceeding gestational window for eligibility, lost capsules, preeclampsia, and missed appointments — a mix of reasons, some plausibly unrelated to the outcome (administrative), but others (stillbirth, preeclampsia) could plausibly relate to underlying health status correlated with depressive symptoms, raising the possibility that missingness could depend on the true (unobserved) outcome value.
- No sensitivity analysis was conducted by trial investigators to assess robustness of the result to missing data assumptions (e.g., imputation under plausible MNAR scenarios).
- The sample size is very small (only 60 randomized, 32 analyzed per-protocol), amplifying the potential impact of missing data on the primary dichotomous outcome (EPDS ≥11).
- **Conclusion:** Given (i) substantial proportion of missing outcome data (~47%), (ii) no evidence/sensitivity analysis demonstrating the result is robust to missingness, (iii) plausible relationship between reasons for missingness (stillbirth, preeclampsia, dropout) and the true outcome value, and (iv) some difference in retention proportions between groups, this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

## Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- The outcome (EPDS score/EPDS≥11) is a participant-reported outcome measured via a validated, widely used self-report scale (EPDS), validated for the Brazilian population with specified sensitivity/specificity cutoffs.
- The method of measurement was not inappropriate — EPDS is an internationally validated and commonly used tool for this purpose, allowing comparability across studies.
- Measurement/ascertainment procedures were identical between groups (same instrument, same timing of assessment at T0, T1, T2, T3) — no evidence measurement differed by group.
- Outcome assessors: Since EPDS is self-reported, the participant is the outcome assessor. Given the double-blind design (participants unaware of assigned intervention, and no differential side effects reported that would unblind participants — only nausea/vomiting reported symmetrically in both groups: 4 in each group), knowledge of intervention assignment is unlikely to have influenced self-report.
- No signals of unblinding (e.g., differential taste, differential side-effect profile) that would compromise the blinding of the outcome assessor (participant) were reported; side effects were balanced between groups.
- **Conclusion:** Given validated instrument, comparable measurement procedures, and effective blinding of participants (who are also the outcome assessors) with no evidence of differential unblinding, this domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

## Domain 5: Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- The trial was retrospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (registered 24 May 2012, well after enrollment began in November 2009 and after the last follow-up visit in July 2012, i.e., registered essentially after data collection was completed). This raises significant concern that outcome measures and analyses may have been chosen or adjusted after unblinded outcome data were available/known.
- The registered protocol (linked to the parent cohort, NCT01660165) lists the primary outcome as "Major depressive episode" (implying MINI-based diagnosis), while the published trial's primary outcome is instead "prevalence of EPDS≥11" — a discrepancy between the registered outcome and the reported primary outcome, suggestive of potential outcome-switching.
- The manuscript reports multiple analyses (ITT and per-protocol; overall sample and subgroup of women with prior depression history; multiple regression models Model 1 and Model 2 with different covariate adjustments; multiple time-point comparisons T0-T1, T1-T2, T1-T3, T0-T3) without a clearly pre-specified analysis hierarchy. The one nominally "significant" finding highlighted in the abstract/discussion (reduction in EPDS score in the subgroup with previous depression history, ITT, T1-T3) emerged from a subgroup analysis that was one of many comparisons performed, increasing the risk that this particular result was selectively emphasized based on its statistical significance (p=0.038, p=0.029) among numerous non-significant comparisons reported throughout Tables 3–6.
- No statistical analysis plan (SAP) date-stamped prior to unblinding was available/cited to confirm that this subgroup analysis and the specific model adjustments were pre-specified rather than data-driven.
- **Conclusion:** Due to (i) retrospective registration with a discrepant primary outcome relative to the published report, (ii) multiple outcome measures/analyses/subgroups reported without clear evidence of a pre-specified plan, and (iii) selective emphasis on the one significant subgroup finding among many non-significant results, this domain is judged **Some concerns** (not "High," since the core primary ITT overall analysis — the pre-specified focus of this ROB assessment — was reported regardless of significance, i.e., the null overall result was not suppressed).

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

# **Overall judgment: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Justification:**
Per the RoB 2 decision rules, an overall judgment of "High risk of bias" is warranted if any single domain is rated "High risk," or if multiple domains are rated "Some concerns" in a manner that cumulatively undermines confidence in the result.

In this trial:
- **Domain 3 (Missing outcome data)** was independently judged **High risk of bias**, due to the very high overall attrition (~47%), the plausible relationship between reasons for dropout (stillbirth, preeclampsia) and the underlying outcome (depressive symptoms/general health), the small sample size amplifying this impact, and the absence of any sensitivity analysis to demonstrate robustness of the ITT result to missing data assumptions.
- Additionally, **Domains 1 and 5** were rated "Some concerns," further compounding uncertainty about the trustworthiness of the reported result.

This combination of a "High risk" domain (missing outcome data) together with "Some concerns" in randomization and selective reporting domains substantially lowers confidence in the reported estimate of effect for the primary outcome (prevalence of EPDS≥11).

**Note on limitation:** This assessment is limited by lack of access to a full trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or CONSORT flow diagram details beyond what is reported in the manuscript figures/text; several signalling question judgments (e.g., exact randomization sequence method, exact allocation concealment mechanism) were based on inference from partial reporting rather than explicit statement, which is reported here transparently as an ambiguity rather than an assumption of adequacy or inadequacy.
