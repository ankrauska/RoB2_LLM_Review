## RoB 2 assessment

**Result assessed:** comparison of **short-bore (1.5 T) versus horizontal open (1 T) MRI** for spinal image quality, focusing on the reported qualitative overall image-quality result across available spinal MRI sequences (short-bore mean 1.92 [SD 0.74] versus open MRI mean 3.16 [SD 0.77]; lower score indicates better quality; *P*<0.0001).  
**Effect of interest:** effect of **assignment** to short-bore versus open MRI at randomization. This is the effect that randomization can validly estimate and is the conventional intention-to-treat effect.  
**Design:** individually randomized, parallel-group trial.  
**Sources assessed:** article, supplied trial-registry record (NCT00715806), and the article’s reported protocol information.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article explicitly states that eligible patients were “**randomly assigned (computer-generated sequence) in a 1:1 ratio**.” A computer-generated sequence contains a random component and meets the RoB 2 criterion for random sequence generation.

- **1.2 Was allocation concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned? — Probably yes.**  
  The authors state that “**allocation was concealed (using sealed envelopes)**.” Although the report does not provide the extended envelope safeguards requested by RoB 2—such as sequential numbering, opacity, tamper-proof sealing, and confirmation that envelopes were opened only after irreversible enrolment—it expressly reports allocation concealment. There is no reported indication that recruiters or participants could foresee upcoming allocation.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization? — No.**  
  Table 1 shows no important baseline imbalance between randomized groups. The groups were similar in sex, age, body size, BMI, anatomical region imaged, baseline claustrophobia score, and pre-scan state anxiety; all reported *P* values were non-significant. No marked imbalance in group size or key prognostic variables is evident among the 93 analysed spinal-imaging participants.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The sequence was explicitly computer generated, allocation concealment was reported, and baseline characteristics do not indicate that randomization failed. The incomplete technical description of the sealed-envelope process is a reporting limitation, but, on the information reported, it does not provide evidence of an inadequately concealed allocation sequence. Therefore, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention? — Yes.**  
  The report states: “**Patients could not be blinded to the assigned study group due to the MR imaging setting.**” The two scanners had materially different physical configurations.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of assigned intervention? — Yes.**  
  Scanner operators and personnel necessarily knew whether the participant was being imaged on the open or short-bore MRI system.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention arising because of the trial context? — Probably no / not the decisive issue.**  
  The protocol specified cross-referral when patients could not tolerate their initially allocated scanner due to claustrophobia. Thus, cross-referral was a planned management procedure, rather than an unreported or clearly trial-induced co-intervention. The report does not establish that personnel deliberately delivered different non-protocol care because of knowledge of allocation.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — No.**  
  The primary image-quality analysis was not performed according to original randomized assignment. Instead, the article reports results by the scanner on which imaging was actually performed: “**48 examinations on the short-bore MR scanner and 55 examinations on the open MR scanner**.” The analysed patient groups in Table 1 were 44 assigned to short-bore and 49 assigned to open MRI, whereas the imaging results include 48 short-bore and 55 open examinations. These differences occurred because patients were cross-referred to the other scanner after a claustrophobic event. The article explicitly states that “**examinations performed after cross-referral, with or without sedation, were included in the primary analysis**.”

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of failure to analyse participants in their randomized groups? — Yes.**  
  There were **25 examinations performed after cross-referral**, representing approximately one quarter of the 103 examinations reported in Table 2. This is not a trivial amount of switching. Moreover, switching was determined by inability to tolerate the initially assigned scanner because of claustrophobia. Claustrophobia, scan completion, sedation, motion, and image artefacts are relevant to the image-quality comparison. The article itself identifies motion artefacts as relevant and notes that longer scan times, pain, and claustrophobia could contribute to such artefacts. Therefore, an as-treated analysis after substantial, outcome-relevant crossover can materially alter the estimated contrast.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The central issue is not lack of blinding alone. It is that the reported primary comparison of image quality is based on the **scanner actually used**, including cross-referred examinations, rather than on the scanner to which patients were randomized. Cross-referral was frequent and was related to claustrophobia—a characteristic that could affect movement during imaging and thus image quality. The analysis therefore does not appropriately estimate the effect of randomized assignment, and the scale and nature of crossover could substantially affect the result. This domain is at **high risk of bias**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants? — No.**  
  Of 106 patients with an indication for spinal MRI, **93 underwent MRI and were included** in this image-quality analysis. Thus, image-quality data were unavailable for 13 patients with a spinal-MRI indication. In addition, one participant who started short-bore imaging aborted after acquisition of one sequence; that sequence “**could thus not be included in the analysis**.”

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No analysis is reported that demonstrates robustness of the image-quality comparison to missing data. The report does not present an appropriate sensitivity analysis addressing plausible image-quality results among patients who could not undergo spinal MRI or whose scan was aborted.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  Missing image-quality data occurred because some patients could not complete or begin MRI owing to claustrophobia. Claustrophobia may be related to patient movement, scan interruption, need for sedation, and inability to obtain usable images. These factors are relevant to the measured image-quality outcomes, particularly artefacts and qualitative image quality.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  The study reports that claustrophobic events prevented or interrupted imaging and discusses increased motion as a relevant source of image artefacts. Patients unable to tolerate imaging are plausibly those for whom completion and image quality would be most problematic. The report also does not provide a complete outcome-data accounting by originally randomized group for the spinal-image-quality result. The numerical reporting is not entirely transparent: 106 patients are stated to have a spinal indication, while the figure description reports 52 and 53 allocated patients (105 total), and Table 1 includes 44 and 49 analysed patients (93 total).

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Outcome data were absent for more than a negligible number of patients, including patients who could not undergo imaging due to the clinical issue central to the trial—claustrophobia. Missingness was therefore not demonstrably unrelated to image quality. No sensitivity analysis or other analysis addresses the potential impact of these missing data, and the reporting does not fully reconcile the relevant denominators. Given that inability to tolerate MRI can be associated with interruption and motion-related degradation of images, missingness is likely related to the unobserved image-quality outcome. This domain is judged at **high risk of bias**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  The study used pre-specified qualitative ratings of overall image quality, contrast, contour sharpness, noise, and artefacts; quantitative signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR); and quantitative contour-sharpness measures. These are appropriate methods for comparing technical spinal MRI image quality.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment differ between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The same categories of spinal sequences were acquired in both scanner groups, and the investigators state that the sequence setup aimed to obtain similar voxel sizes and imaging times. The study used the same image-analysis framework for images from both scanners. The scanner systems necessarily differed in field strength, gradient strength, coil configuration, and acquisition time, but these are characteristics of the interventions being compared—not differential outcome-assessment procedures.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of intervention received? — No.**  
  The report states: “**At the time of the analysis the examiners were blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity.**” Qualitative assessments were performed by two examiners in consensus, and quantitative analysis by one examiner.

- **4.4 Could outcome assessment have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable.**  
  The reported assessor blinding means there is no evidence that assessors knew scanner assignment while grading or measuring images.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The technical and qualitative image-quality assessments were appropriate for the stated outcome. Crucially, the image assessors were explicitly blinded to imaging technique and patient identity. The article supplies no evidence that image assessment methods differed by group in a way that could introduce differential measurement error. This domain is at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data analysed according to a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The trial was registered before completion and a design/protocol publication is cited. However, the supplied registry record identifies claustrophobic reactions preventing MRI as the primary outcome and subsequent clinical management as the secondary outcome; it does not specify this spinal image-quality outcome, the image-quality measurement hierarchy, the specific analysis population, or the statistical analysis approach for the reported result. No dated statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied material.

- **5.2 Is the assessed result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements? — No information.**  
  Image quality was measured in many eligible ways: overall qualitative image quality, contrast, contour sharpness, noise, artefacts, SNR, several CNR contrasts, and quantitative contour sharpness. Measurements were also made across sagittal T1-weighted, sagittal T2-weighted, and axial T2-weighted sequences and across cervicothoracic and thoracolumbar regions. The article reports a substantial amount of these results in Tables 3 and 4, which reduces concern that only one favourable individual measure was presented. However, without a sufficiently detailed pre-specified plan, it cannot be confirmed that the highlighted overall summary result was selected independently of its findings.

- **5.3 Is the assessed result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses? — No information.**  
  The report includes primary analyses incorporating cross-referred examinations and subgroup analyses excluding cross-referred examinations and assessing sedated patients. It does not provide a pre-specified hierarchy identifying which analysis should be primary for the image-quality outcome. In particular, the choice to include cross-referred examinations in the reported primary comparison was not supported by a pre-specified analysis plan supplied for this assessment.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
There is no direct evidence that the authors selected the presented image-quality result because it was more favourable to short-bore MRI, and the paper reports numerous sequence-specific qualitative and quantitative results. Therefore, a **high-risk** judgement is not warranted. Nevertheless, no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan for this derived spinal image-quality analysis is available, despite multiple possible outcome measures, sequence-specific results, anatomical subgroups, and analytic populations. This leaves **some concerns** about selection of the reported result.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement — **High risk of bias**

The overall judgement is **high risk of bias** because:

1. **Bias due to deviations from intended interventions is high:** the primary image-quality comparison was conducted according to the scanner actually used, not according to randomized assignment, despite 25 cross-referred examinations. Cross-referral was related to claustrophobia and therefore could be related to image quality and artefacts.

2. **Bias due to missing outcome data is high:** image-quality data were unavailable for patients unable to undergo MRI, including due to claustrophobia, and no analysis demonstrates that their exclusion could not affect the result.

The assessment of image quality itself was blinded and methodologically appropriate, and the randomization process appears adequately conducted. However, these strengths do not overcome the high risk arising from the non-randomized analysis after crossover and outcome data missing in relation to MRI non-completion.
