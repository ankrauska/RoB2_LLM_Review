# ROB2 Analysis Report: Percutaneous Microwave Ablation vs. Laparoscopic Resection in Liver Cancer Prognosis

**Preliminary Note on Assessment:** This trial compares two active surgical/procedural interventions (percutaneous microwave ablation vs. laparoscopic resection). The assessment below addresses the effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), as no per-protocol analysis or specification of deviations was reported. Multiple outcomes are assessed collectively where methodology is common (surgical time, blood loss, complications, recurrence, survival), with domain-specific distinctions noted where relevant.

---

## 1. Bias Arising from the Randomization Process

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- The paper states patients were divided using a "random number table method," which, if true, would normally constitute an adequate random sequence generation method (signalling question 1.1: Probably Yes).
- However, no information is provided regarding **allocation concealment** (signalling question 1.2). There is no description of any mechanism (central allocation, sealed envelopes, etc.) to prevent foreknowledge of assignment by enrolling personnel or participants. Given the nature of the interventions (ablation vs. laparoscopic surgery) and the absence of any concealment description, this raises serious concern about the ability of investigators to manipulate enrollment/assignment order.
- Critically, baseline characteristics show a numerical imbalance in tumor size ranges (Group A: 2–5 cm; Group B: 1–5 cm) and imbalance in single/multiple nodule distribution, though these differences are not tested statistically nor deemed significant explicitly. More importantly, the **near-identical group sizes (45/45) with no explanation of how exact balance was achieved using a "random number table"** is unusual and raises suspicion of non-genuine or manipulated randomization, particularly in an unblinded surgical trial comparing highly different procedures.
- No trial registration, protocol, or CONSORT flow diagram was available to verify the randomization process, and no further methodological detail is provided (e.g., sequence generation algorithm, block sizes, stratification).
- The combination of (a) absent allocation concealment information, (b) lack of detail on the "random number table" implementation, and (c) an unblinded, highly manipulable surgical setting supports a judgment of **High risk of bias** for this domain, as it is plausible that knowledge of the upcoming allocation could have influenced patient selection into either the ablation or surgical group (e.g., directing patients with more favorable/unfavorable disease characteristics into one group).

**Limitation identified:** The absence of any description of allocation concealment methods, and the ambiguity of "random number table" implementation in an open, non-blinded surgical comparison, constitutes a significant reporting gap that could not be resolved from the text.

---

## 2. Bias Due to Deviations from Intended Interventions (Effect of Assignment to Intervention)

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- **Participants and personnel were not blinded** (signalling questions 2.1/2.2: Yes) — blinding is impossible in this context, since one intervention is a percutaneous ablation procedure and the other is laparoscopic surgery; participants and surgeons/interventionalists necessarily knew which treatment was being delivered.
- Because blinding was not feasible, the tool requires assessment of whether deviations from intended intervention arose because of the trial context and affected the outcome (signalling question 2.3-2.5). The article provides **no information** on this issue — there is no discussion of whether choice of treatment was influenced by physician or patient preference post-randomization, or whether cross-over/non-protocol interventions occurred.
- No CONSORT flow diagram or explicit description confirms that all 90 randomized patients were analyzed in the group to which they were originally assigned in an ITT manner. The methods section does not clarify whether any patients assigned to one treatment arm crossed over to the other (e.g., due to intraoperative findings during laparoscopy revealing unresectable disease, or ablation failure necessitating conversion to surgery), which is a common occurrence in comparative studies of this type.
- Given the substantial clinical and procedural differences between the two interventions, the potential for performance-related bias (e.g., differential co-interventions, perioperative care standards, or clinician bias in patient selection/handling based on known assignment) is high, and this was not addressed, controlled for, or discussed by the authors.
- No appropriate statistical analysis (e.g., ITT vs. per-protocol) was specified; the paper does not report the analytical approach used to handle any deviations.

**Limitation identified:** The complete absence of information about intervention fidelity, cross-over, or protocol deviations in an inherently unblinded comparative surgical trial is a major reporting deficiency that prevents a lower risk judgment.

## 3. Bias Due to Missing Outcome Data

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- The paper states that "all cases were followed up for 1-3 years," implying complete follow-up data for all 90 randomized patients (45 per group) for recurrence and survival outcomes (signalling question 3.1: Probably Yes).
- However, **no explicit accounting of dropout, loss to follow-up, or withdrawal is provided** — there is no CONSORT flow diagram, and the manuscript does not explicitly confirm that outcome data (surgical time, blood loss, complications, recurrence, 1/2/3-year survival) were available for all 90 originally randomized participants without exclusions.
- The lack of a flow diagram or explicit patient accounting throughout the follow-up period (especially given the extended 1-3 year follow-up window, during which loss to follow-up is common) creates uncertainty. While the narrative implies completeness, this is not independently verifiable from the reported data.
- There is no information suggesting that missingness (if any existed) was related to the true outcome value, and no evidence of differential dropout between groups is reported, but this cannot be fully confirmed due to lack of explicit accounting.

**Limitation identified:** Absence of a participant flow diagram or explicit statement confirming zero loss to follow-up/dropout for all outcomes across the 1-3 year follow-up period introduces uncertainty, but there is no direct evidence of bias, warranting "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

---

## 4. Bias in Measurement of the Outcome

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- For outcomes involving clinical judgment—such as **complications** (bile leakage, pleural effusion, postoperative bleeding) and **local/total recurrence**—the method of ascertainment is not clearly described, but such assessments typically require imaging (CT/ultrasound) interpretation and clinical judgment (observer-reported outcomes involving some judgment).
- The outcome assessors are very likely to have been the treating physicians or investigators who were **not blinded to intervention assignment**, since blinding was not feasible or attempted in this open surgical/procedural comparison (signalling question 4.3: Yes — assessors aware of intervention received).
- Assessment of complications and local recurrence plausibly could be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received (signalling question 4.4: Yes), particularly given that:
  - The measurement/ascertainment of outcomes could differ between groups: post-surgical complications are actively monitored differently than post-ablation site changes; surveillance imaging protocols and thresholds for diagnosing "local recurrence" after ablation (residual/recurrent enhancement at ablation margin) versus after surgical resection (margin recurrence) are inherently different diagnostic paradigms, raising concern about differential outcome ascertainment (signalling question 4.2: Yes).
  - There is a plausible motivation (demonstrating superiority of one technique) that could influence subjective judgment calls in borderline recurrence cases.
- No information is provided about whether an independent, blinded outcomes adjudication committee assessed recurrence or complications.
- Given the likely differential measurement approach between an ablation-treated liver (interpreted via imaging follow-up) versus a surgically resected liver (different anatomical/imaging follow-up), combined with unblinded assessment, this domain is judged as **High risk of bias**, particularly affecting the recurrence and complication outcomes.

**Limitation identified:** No description of outcome assessment methodology, blinding status of assessors, or standardized/independent adjudication criteria for recurrence and complications was reported, which is a critical gap given the inherently different follow-up modalities for the two interventions.

## 5. Bias in Selection of the Reported Result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- No trial registration record, published protocol, or statistical analysis plan is referenced or available for this study, making it impossible to verify whether outcome measures and analyses were pre-specified prior to unblinded data access (signalling question 5.1: No information).
- The outcomes reported (surgical time, blood loss, complications, local/total recurrence, 1/2/3-year survival) appear to be standard, clinically relevant outcomes for this comparison, and there is no overt evidence of selective outcome reporting (e.g., no indication of unreported pre-specified outcomes, unusual subgroup analyses, or selectively reported time points).
- However, the complete absence of a protocol or registry entry means selective reporting **cannot be ruled out** — for instance, it is unclear whether other outcomes (e.g., quality of life, cost, additional complication types, or other follow-up intervals) were collected but not reported, or whether multiple statistical models/cut-offs were tested for recurrence classification.
- No signalling evidence suggests deliberate selection based on results (e.g., no unusual composite outcome construction or inconsistent outcome definitions across the text), so a "High risk" judgment is not warranted; however, the lack of verifiable pre-specification supports "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

**Limitation identified:** Absence of a trial registration or protocol document prevents confirmation that reported analyses and outcomes match pre-specified intentions.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgment

**Overall Judgment: High Risk of Bias**

**Rationale:**
Per the RoB 2 decision rules, an overall judgment of **High risk of bias** is warranted because the study was judged to be at **High risk of bias in multiple individual domains** (Domain 1: Randomization process; Domain 2: Deviations from intended interventions; Domain 4: Measurement of the outcome). Any single "High risk" domain judgment is sufficient to drive the overall result to "High risk of bias," and here three domains independently meet this threshold. Additionally, "Some concerns" in the remaining two domains (missing outcome data; selection of reported result) further compound the overall lack of confidence in the trustworthiness of this trial's results.

**Key drivers of the High-risk judgment:**
1. Unverifiable and potentially inadequate randomization/allocation concealment process in an unblinded surgical comparison.
2. No information on protocol deviations, cross-overs, or fidelity to assigned intervention in a context where such deviations are clinically plausible and consequential.
3. High likelihood of differential, non-blinded outcome assessment (particularly for recurrence and complications) between two procedurally and diagnostically distinct interventions.

**Overall limitation:** This study suffers from substantial underreporting of essential methodological details (randomization mechanics, allocation concealment, protocol adherence, blinding status of outcome assessors, and trial registration/protocol availability), which collectively undermine confidence in the reported comparative findings between percutaneous microwave ablation and laparoscopic resection for liver cancer.
