## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

- **Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial.  
- **Comparison:** Dementia family care coordinator support programme for 2 years versus conventional/usual community care.  
- **Outcome/result assessed:** Time from enrolment to placement in long-term institutional care over 24 months, analysed using Kaplan–Meier methods and a time-dependent Cox regression model. The reported Cox model gave an intervention-group hazard ratio of **0.12 (95% CI 0.02–0.93; P=.042)**, with a significant group-by-time interaction (**HR 1.18 per month, 95% CI 1.02–1.36; P=.028**), indicating that the apparent early benefit declined over time.  
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to the intervention (intention-to-treat effect).  
- **Sources available for this assessment:** The supplied article, tables, and figure descriptions only. No protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospective trial-registration record was provided.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The article states that 100 envelopes were prepared, 53 containing intervention allocation and 47 containing control allocation, and that patients/caregivers drew an envelope. However, it does not report how the allocation labels were ordered among envelopes or confirm that a random process was used to generate their order. A statement that participants “drew one envelope” does not itself establish that the allocation sequence was random.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The envelopes were reported as “sealed nontransparent envelopes,” which provides some information supporting concealment. However, the article does not report that envelopes were sequentially numbered, tamper-proof, opened only after irreversible enrolment, or otherwise protected from selection or manipulation. The dementia family care coordinator, who delivered the intervention, prepared the envelopes. The report therefore does not provide sufficient detail to establish adequate allocation concealment under RoB 2 criteria.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  Baseline patient and caregiver characteristics were reported for both groups. There were no statistically significant differences in age, sex, dementia diagnosis, MMSE score, Barthel Index, living arrangement, caregiver characteristics, or caregiver GHQ score. The difference in severe dementia prevalence (36% intervention versus 24% control) was not statistically significant (**P=.244**) and is not, on the reported information, evidence of an imbalance beyond chance.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The reported baseline data do not indicate that randomization failed. However, the report does not establish either a random allocation sequence or adequately concealed allocation. In accordance with RoB 2, absence of information on allocation concealment, with baseline characteristics compatible with chance, leads to **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.

A further methodological feature is that the first 86 participants were allocated 1:1, whereas the final 14 participants were allocated 2:1 in favour of intervention. The authors acknowledge that the 1:1 ratio should ideally have been retained. Unequal allocation does not itself establish bias, but it adds to the lack of clarity regarding the randomization process.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The intervention involved frequent contact with a dementia family care coordinator, counselling, telephone availability, home visits, assistance arranging services, and annual training courses. Participants and caregivers necessarily knew whether they were receiving this programme.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The dementia family care coordinator delivered the intervention and thus knew the assigned intervention. Usual-care arrangements also could not have been blinded to the intensive case-management programme.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  The article describes the intervention delivered to the intervention group and states that control participants received usual community services. It does not report whether trial-context deviations occurred, such as intervention participants receiving unplanned additional services because of trial participation, or control participants receiving intervention-like support because they were trial participants.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The article reports that there were no dropouts during follow-up and presents analyses by randomized group. Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses were performed for intervention versus control groups, without reported exclusions due to non-adherence, treatment received, or post-randomization events. Thus, the reported analysis is consistent with analysing participants in their allocated groups for the effect of assignment.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
This was necessarily an unblinded support intervention. Unblinding alone does not create high risk of bias for the effect of assignment; high risk would require evidence that trial-context deviations occurred, affected the outcome, and were unbalanced. Such evidence is not reported. However, the report does not provide information allowing assessment of whether important deviations from intended care arose because of the trial context. Therefore, this domain raises **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  There were no reported study dropouts. However, the analysis censored participants at death if they died in community care before institutionalization. By 24 months, the text reports deaths at home in **9/53 (17%)** intervention participants and **8/47 (17%)** control participants. These participants could no longer experience institutionalization and their subsequent time-to-institutionalization outcome was therefore not observed.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No sensitivity analysis, competing-risk analysis, inverse-probability weighting, or other analysis addressing the potential effect of censoring at death was reported. The article used Kaplan–Meier and Cox methods with censoring at death.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  Death before institutionalization is directly related to patients’ health status and precludes subsequent placement in long-term care. Therefore, censoring at death could be related to the underlying likelihood or timing of institutionalization.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — No information.**  
  The article reports numbers of deaths but does not report causes of death, patients’ clinical condition before death, or analyses showing that censoring at death was non-informative. It therefore does not establish that the assumption required for censoring in the Kaplan–Meier/Cox analysis was appropriate.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary outcome was analysed as time to institutionalization, with death at home treated as a censoring event. Death before institutionalization affected approximately 17% of participants in each group by 24 months, which is not negligible. For the reported Kaplan–Meier and Cox analyses, censoring at death requires an assumption that the censoring mechanism is non-informative with respect to subsequent institutionalization. The study does not provide evidence to support that assumption or analyses showing that the result is robust to it.

There is also an internal inconsistency in the reported participant accounting. The article text states that by 2 years **17 intervention patients (32%)** and **14 control patients (30%)** had been institutionalized, whereas the supplied Figure 1 description reports **9 intervention patients** institutionalized at 2 years and gives intervention totals of 37 at home, 9 institutionalized, and 9 deceased—totalling 55 participants despite 53 having been randomized. This inconsistency means that the number of institutionalizations cannot be verified unambiguously from the supplied report. It further undermines confidence in the completeness and clarity of outcome data reporting.

Given the substantial death-related censoring, absence of an analysis addressing potentially informative censoring, and inconsistency in reported two-year participant counts, this domain is judged at **high risk of bias**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  Institutionalization in long-term care was a clearly defined endpoint. The information was obtained from the Social Insurance Institution register. This is an appropriate source for determining whether and when placement in long-term institutional care occurred.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  The article reports the same register-based source of outcome information for both groups. It does not report different outcome definitions, different measurement methods, or different follow-up schedules between groups for the ascertainment of institutionalization.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — Probably no.**  
  The article states that permanent institutionalization decisions were made by groups of experts, usually including a chief physician, head nurse, and social worker. These groups were reported to be “blind to the allocation and generally unaware” that the patient was participating in the study.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable.**  
  The reported outcome decision-makers were blinded to group allocation. In addition, institutionalization was registered through an external administrative register.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The outcome was ascertained using the same administrative register in both groups, and the decision-making groups responsible for institutionalization were reported to be blinded to allocation and generally unaware of study participation. The article does not identify an inappropriate measurement method or differential ascertainment between groups. Therefore, risk of bias in outcome measurement is **low**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospectively registered analysis plan was supplied. The article describes its statistical methods but does not establish that these methods, the handling of deaths as censoring events, the time-dependent Cox model, or the subgroup analyses were pre-specified before outcome data were available.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The outcome was reported at several time points (6, 12, and 24 months), and the study also presented subgroup survival curves according to dementia severity. The supplied information does not establish whether these time points and subgroup results were pre-specified or whether other eligible outcome measurements were assessed but not reported.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The report provides Kaplan–Meier estimates, a time-dependent Cox model, and descriptive comparisons at 12 and 24 months. It does not provide a pre-specified plan to show why the reported model, including the group-by-time interaction, was selected over other eligible analyses. It also does not provide a pre-specified strategy for handling death before institutionalization.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
There is no evidence that the reported result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable to the intervention; therefore, a high-risk judgement is not warranted. However, the absence of a dated pre-specified analysis plan, together with multiple reported time points, subgroup analyses, and analytical approaches, prevents confirmation that the reported Cox-regression result was selected independently of the results. This domain is therefore judged as **some concerns**.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**  
Under RoB 2, a result is judged at **high risk of bias** when at least one domain is judged at high risk of bias. The result is at high risk of bias because of **bias due to missing outcome data**.

The principal concern is the handling of deaths before institutionalization. Death at home occurred in approximately 17% of participants in both groups and was treated as censoring in the Kaplan–Meier and Cox analyses. The report does not provide evidence that this censoring was non-informative, nor does it present a sensitivity or competing-risk analysis. In addition, the supplied report contains inconsistent two-year institutionalization and participant-count information. These issues substantially lower confidence in the reported estimate of the intervention’s effect on time to institutionalization.
