# ROB 2 Assessment: BACE Intervention on Agitation in Dementia Patients

## Preliminary Considerations
- **Study design:** Individually-randomized parallel-group trial
- **Experimental intervention:** BACE (Balancing Arousal Controls Excesses)
- **Comparator:** Usual care (no alteration to activity schedule)
- **Outcome assessed:** Agitation (visual analog scale, 0-100), pretest to posttest
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (ITT-type approach appears intended, though deviations exist—see Domain 2)
- **Numerical result assessed:** Group × Time interaction, F(1,69) = 4.26, p = .043, partial η² = .06

---

### **Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process**
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- The paper states random assignment "in time blocks of 1 week," which constitutes a random component (1.1 = Probably Yes), though the specific method of sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated, random number table) within blocks is not described in detail.
- No information is provided on allocation concealment procedures (1.2 = No information). There is no description of a mechanism preventing foreknowledge of upcoming block assignments (e.g., central randomization, sealed envelopes), which is important given cluster/block-based assignment could be predictable if block boundaries were known to staff or researchers involved in assessing eligibility.
- Critically, baseline data reveal a **statistically significant imbalance** in a key prognostic variable: time spent in arousal imbalance at pretest (Experimental M = 107.4 min/12hr vs Control M = 84.6 min/12hr; t = -2.584, p = .012). This is directly relevant because arousal imbalance is the core mechanism targeted by the intervention and a strong predictor of the outcome (agitation). The authors acknowledge this imbalance was not resolved by randomization ("random assignment did not equalize the groups on arousal imbalance") and had to statistically adjust for it as a covariate.
- This imbalance in a key prognostic factor (p = .012) is compatible with the "Yes" answer to signalling question 1.3 (baseline imbalance suggesting a problem), though it may plausibly be due to chance given the relatively small sample and the fact that other baseline variables (MMSE, age, LOS, gender, FAST, pretest agitation) were not significantly different.
- Because sequence generation and concealment are not clearly described (No information), combined with evidence of imbalance in a key prognostic factor, this leads to a judgment of **Some concerns** rather than Low risk, per the algorithm (No information on concealment + baseline imbalance suggesting a problem → Some Concerns, not automatically High, since it is not certain that randomization itself failed rather than resulting from chance in a limited final sample of 78).

**Limitation noted:** The specific method used to generate the randomization sequence (e.g., computer-based, table of random numbers) and the method of allocation concealment were not reported in the article.

---

### **Domain 2: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions**
**Judgment: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- **Blinding:** The article explicitly states, "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions." However, it is implausible that nursing staff and the geriatric nurse practitioner (GNP) who implemented the new activity schedule could be blinded, since they had to consciously alter or maintain the participant's schedule. Thus, personnel delivering the intervention were aware of group assignment (2.2 = Yes). Blinding of participants (persons with severe dementia, median MMSE = 3) is largely not meaningful cognitively, but nursing staff's awareness is the operative issue.
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** The study reports that the intervention was withheld or only partially delivered in a substantial number of cases: 10 participants were dropped entirely for not receiving any intervention (nurses reported being short-staffed, busy, or having other emergent needs), and among those included, only 72% received the "full dose" of prescribed activity schedule changes, while 22% received 50-75% and 6% received less than 50% of the prescribed dose. This represents non-implementation of the intended intervention that is directly tied to the trial/care context (staffing, workload) — a deviation likely affecting the outcome (agitation), since dose was intended to correct arousal imbalance, the core causal pathway to reduced agitation.
- **Analysis approach:** Ten participants who were assigned to the intervention group but received no dose of the intervention were **excluded from the final analysis** (contributing to the final N of 78 from 102 participants who began). This exclusion of eligible, randomized participants based on adherence/implementation status is not consistent with intention-to-treat principles. This is an inappropriate analysis for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention (signalling question 2.6 = No), because participants were excluded post-randomization for reasons related to intervention implementation, not because of ineligibility determined independently of the intervention process.
- Given that the exclusion of 10 non-dosed participants (out of the original N=102, representing ~10% of the sample and a meaningfully sized subgroup relative to the final N=78) could have a substantial impact on the effect estimate (signalling question 2.7 = Yes, potential for substantial impact, since these were specifically people for whom intervention could not be delivered due to real-world contextual factors, likely correlated with more severe illness/practical barriers), this leads to a judgment of **High risk of bias** for this domain.
- Additionally, deaths (n=12) and missing/erroneous data (n=2) further contributed to attrition affecting the intention-to-treat principle, compounding this concern (though death is addressed further under Domain 3).

**Limitation noted:** The report does not clarify whether the 10 individuals dropped for "not being given the intervention" differed systematically from those who received it, nor does it present a true ITT analysis including these participants.

### **Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data**
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- Of 102 participants enrolled, only 78 were included in the final analysis: 12 died, 10 were dropped for non-receipt of intervention (addressed under Domain 2), and 2 were excluded for erroneous/missing data. This represents outcome data availability of 78/102 (~76%), which does not meet the threshold of "all or nearly all" participants (signalling question 3.1 = No).
- No sensitivity analyses were reported to demonstrate that the results are robust to assumptions about the missing data (3.2 = No/No information).
- Missingness due to **death (n=12)** could plausibly be related to the true value of the outcome (severity of illness, which may correlate with agitation levels) — this represents a situation where missingness could depend on the true outcome value (3.3 = Yes/Probably yes). However, there is no clear evidence that death rates differed between experimental and control groups, nor that reasons for missingness differed systematically between groups in a way clearly likely to bias the result (3.4 = Probably no/No information) — the article does not report a breakdown of these exclusions (deaths, erroneous data) by treatment allocation.
- Given the absence of a clear differential pattern of missingness by group and lack of information linking missingness definitively to outcome values in a group-differential manner, but also absence of any sensitivity analysis or complete outcome data, this is judged as **Some concerns** rather than High risk.

**Limitation noted:** The article does not report the distribution of deaths or missing/erroneous data exclusions (n=12 and n=2, respectively) by treatment group, precluding a definitive assessment of differential attrition.

### **Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome**
**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

**Motivation:**
- The method of measuring the outcome (a visual analog scale from 0-100, informed by the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory items and direct behavioral observation with defined intensity parameters — Table 3) appears appropriate and was based on established conceptual definitions of agitation, with rigorous training (interrater reliability .85+ required, achieved reliabilities of .95-.984) (4.1 = No, method not inappropriate).
- Measurement/ascertainment methods were identical across groups: observations occurred at the same time points (8 a.m.–8 p.m.), same duration (3 min every 15 min), and same protocol for both groups, with exclusion of atypical days (baths, doctor visits, weekends) applied uniformly (4.2 = No, did not differ between groups).
- Critically, the article explicitly states: "**Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions**." Since the outcome assessor here is the RA conducting direct behavioral observation (an observer-reported outcome involving some judgment), and these assessors were blinded to group allocation, the risk of differential/biased outcome assessment is low (4.3 = No, assessors unaware).
- Because outcome assessors were blinded, subsequent questions regarding potential influence of unblinding do not apply.

**Limitation noted:** The article does not elaborate on the specific procedural safeguards used to maintain blinding of data collectors (e.g., whether RAs had any contact with nursing staff that could reveal group assignment), which is a minor ambiguity, but given the explicit statement of blinding and the objective/standardized nature of the observational protocol, this is not considered sufficient to raise the judgment beyond Low risk.

### **Domain 5: Bias in selection of the reported result**
**Judgment: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**
- No trial registry record, published protocol, or statistical analysis plan is referenced or made available in the article, making it impossible to verify whether the analyses reported (e.g., the specific repeated measures ANCOVA with arousal imbalance and MMSE as covariates) were pre-specified prior to unblinded outcome data being available (signalling question 5.1 = No information).
- The outcome domain (agitation) was measured using a single, consistently defined instrument (the visual analog scale), and there is no indication of multiple alternative agitation measures being selectively reported (5.2 = No/Probably no) — the initial plan to use the ABMI was abandoned during training (before outcome data collection) for a priori psychometric reasons (poor interrater reliability), which is a legitimate, results-independent modification unrelated to the trial's outcome findings.
- However, regarding analysis (5.3), multiple analytic models are presented (the primary ANCOVA, a model with therapeutic activities as second covariate, three additional stratified 2×2 ANCOVAs for MMSE/sleep/verbal subgroups, paired t-tests stratified by arousal balance/imbalance, and a regression analysis of "dose"). While these appear to be presented transparently (including non-significant results, e.g., the dose analysis and interaction models), the absence of a pre-specified analysis plan makes it impossible to confirm that the primary outcome analysis (the Group × Time interaction ANCOVA controlling for arousal imbalance at pretest) was the originally intended primary analysis rather than one selected among several possible covariate-adjustment strategies, especially given that arousal imbalance was added specifically as a covariate after being identified as imbalanced at baseline.
- Because there is no information available to confirm pre-specification, but there is no overt evidence of selective suppression of unfavorable results (multiple null results are reported openly), this is judged as **Some concerns** rather than High risk.

**Limitation noted:** No trial protocol, registration, or statistical analysis plan was cited, so it cannot be determined whether the covariate-adjusted primary analysis was pre-specified or determined post hoc after observing baseline imbalance and/or outcome data.

## **6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement**

### **Overall Judgment: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Summary of domain judgments:**
| Domain | Judgment |
|---|---|
| 1. Randomization process | Some concerns |
| 2. Deviations from intended interventions | **High risk** |
| 3. Missing outcome data | Some concerns |
| 4. Measurement of the outcome | Low risk |
| 5. Selection of the reported result | Some concerns |

**Rationale:** Per the RoB 2 decision rules, a judgment of High risk of bias in any single domain mandates an overall judgment of High risk of bias, regardless of ratings in other domains. Domain 2 was judged High risk because personnel delivering the intervention could not have been blinded (they actively altered care schedules), a meaningful proportion of the intervention group did not receive the full (or any) prescribed intervention due to contextual factors (staffing/workload), and — most importantly — 10 randomized participants who received no intervention dose were excluded from the analysis entirely, violating intention-to-treat principles in a manner that could substantially bias the effect estimate. This is compounded by "Some concerns" in three additional domains (randomization process, missing outcome data, and selection of the reported result), which further substantially lowers confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported result even independently of the Domain 2 rating. Therefore, the overall risk of bias for this result (Group × Time interaction on agitation) is **High**.
