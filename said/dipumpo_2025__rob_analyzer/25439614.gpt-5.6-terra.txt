## Scope of assessment

- **Design:** Multicentre randomized parallel-group clinical trial (eye-level treatment comparison).
- **Comparison:** Intravitreal ranibizumab plus **prompt** focal/grid laser versus intravitreal ranibizumab plus **deferred** focal/grid laser (deferred for at least 24 weeks).
- **Outcome/result assessed:** Best-corrected visual-acuity letter-score change from baseline at 5 years. The principal reported longitudinal estimate was **prompt minus deferred = −2.6 letters** (95% CI −5.5 to +0.4; P=0.09), adjusted for baseline visual acuity.
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to the randomized treatment strategies (intention-to-treat effect).
- **Sources available for this assessment:** The supplied 5-year article and its tables/figures. Although the article states that the protocol was available on the DRCR.net website, neither the protocol nor a dated statistical analysis plan was supplied for this assessment.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The article states that eyes/participants were “assigned randomly,” but does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random permuted blocks, stratification, or central randomization).

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  No allocation-concealment method is reported in the supplied article. There is no statement of central randomization, an independent allocation service, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No information.**  
  The supplied Table 1 compares 5-year completers with non-completers separately within each treatment group. It does not provide a baseline comparison of the two randomized prompt-versus-deferred groups for all randomized eyes. Therefore, it cannot be used to assess whether baseline imbalances between randomized groups indicated failure of randomization.

**Motivation for judgment**

The study is described as randomized, but the reported article does not provide the information required to establish either random sequence generation or allocation concealment. In addition, the available baseline table does not permit an assessment of balance between the randomized groups. Under RoB 2, where there is no information to answer the randomization-process signalling questions, the appropriate judgment is **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably yes.**  
  Prompt laser at initiation versus deferred laser for at least 24 weeks is an intervention contrast that participants were likely able to distinguish. The article does not state that participants were masked to whether and when laser treatment was given.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of assigned intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  Clinicians delivering ranibizumab and focal/grid laser necessarily knew whether laser was to be administered promptly or deferred.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The central treatment contrast was implemented through protocol-defined strategies: prompt laser in one group and deferred laser, only under specified persistence/non-improvement criteria, in the other group. Different amounts of laser and ranibizumab injections between groups are expected components of the assigned strategies and are not, by themselves, deviations from intended intervention.  
  The article reports that other DME treatment was received by 9 prompt-laser completers, 7 of whom met protocol futility/failure criteria; 2 treatments were described as not per protocol. However, the report does not state that these departures arose because of the trial context or that they systematically differed due to knowledge of group allocation in a way that undermined the assigned-strategy comparison.

- **2.4 Were such deviations likely to have affected the outcome? — Not applicable.**
- **2.5 Were such deviations balanced between groups? — Not applicable.**

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  The reported primary longitudinal analysis compared the originally assigned ranibizumab-plus-prompt versus ranibizumab-plus-deferred laser groups, using all available visit data. It did not report an as-treated analysis or a naïve per-protocol analysis as the primary 5-year comparison.  
  Fourteen participants from one site were excluded from non-safety analyses because their baseline OCTs were subsequently judged by central reading-center manual grading not to meet the OCT eligibility criterion. This is reported as exclusion of participants who did not meet a baseline eligibility criterion, rather than exclusion based on post-randomization response, treatment adherence, or outcome. The article does not report a reassignment of participants according to treatment received.

**Motivation for judgment**

Participants and treating personnel were likely aware of allocation because laser timing could not realistically be masked. However, for the effect of assignment to intervention, lack of blinding leads to bias only when it produces outcome-relevant deviations from the intended protocol that arose because of the trial context and were unbalanced between groups. The reported difference in laser exposure and injection burden reflects the protocol-defined treatment strategies. The primary comparison retained randomized-group assignment and used a longitudinal analysis of available data. Therefore, the available evidence does not identify bias due to deviations from intended interventions for the assignment effect.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants? — No.**  
  The 5-year completion rate, excluding deaths, was:
  - **76%** of the original prompt-laser group (124 of 163), and
  - **74%** of the original deferred-laser group (111 of 150).

  Thus, approximately one quarter of randomized participants in each group did not contribute 5-year outcome data. This is not “all or nearly all” participants for a visual-acuity outcome.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The article reports longitudinal models using available data and adjusted analyses including selected baseline covariates. However, it does not report sensitivity analyses demonstrating that the estimated 5-year visual-acuity difference was robust to plausible values of missing visual-acuity data. The article explicitly states that “it is not possible to know whether incomplete data were related to unobserved factors that could have affected the results in either direction.”

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  The principal reason for absent 5-year data was that years 4 and 5 were an extension and some participants chose not to consent to continued follow-up. In a long-term study of visual outcomes and repeated ocular treatment, a decision not to continue in the extension could be related to unobserved health, visual acuity, treatment need, perceived benefit, burden, or other factors related to the outcome.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably no.**  
  The proportions completing 5-year follow-up were similar between groups (76% and 74%), and the article does not report outcome-related reasons for withdrawal that differed between groups. Although completers and non-completers differed on some baseline characteristics, the available report does not establish that missingness was likely dependent on unobserved 5-year visual acuity.

**Motivation for judgment**

Missing 5-year visual-acuity data affected roughly 24% to 26% of the original randomized participants. The article identifies baseline differences between completers and non-completers, including age, prior panretinal photocoagulation, lens status, and diabetic-retinopathy severity. Although adjusted models were presented, no missing-data sensitivity analysis establishes robustness of the result. The report also acknowledges that the relation between incomplete follow-up and unobserved factors cannot be determined. This creates **some concerns**, but the available evidence does not demonstrate that missingness was likely outcome-dependent to the degree required for a high-risk judgment.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  The outcome was best-corrected visual-acuity letter score, a clinically established and appropriate measure for visual function in diabetic macular edema.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The article describes visual-acuity assessment at protocol-defined follow-up visits in both groups. No evidence indicates different visual-acuity measurement methods, thresholds, or assessment procedures between prompt and deferred laser groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — No.**  
  The report explicitly identifies “masking of the visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians” as a study strength. Thus, the individuals measuring visual acuity were masked to treatment assignment.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable.**
- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable.**

**Motivation for judgment**

Visual acuity was measured using an appropriate outcome method, and the visual-acuity examiners were masked. The article provides no indication that the measurement process differed between groups. Therefore, the 5-year visual-acuity result is at **low risk of bias in measurement of the outcome**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The supplied report describes the analyses used, including continuous-time longitudinal models, adjustment for baseline visual acuity, truncation of visual-acuity changes to ±30 letters, and additional adjusted analyses. However, neither a dated statistical analysis plan nor sufficient protocol content is available in the supplied material to verify that this exact 5-year analysis was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  Visual acuity was measured at multiple time points through 260 weeks, and results were reported using mean change, categorical gains/losses, and subgroup analyses. The article states that best-corrected visual acuity at the 5-year visit was the main outcome measure, but the absence of the protocol/SAP prevents verification that the selected 5-year result and metric were pre-specified without selection based on results.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The article reports an unadjusted-by-additional-covariates longitudinal model adjusted for baseline visual acuity and a model additionally adjusted for baseline central subfield thickness, age, prior panretinal photocoagulation, lens status, and retinopathy severity. It also reports completer-only results and multiple categorical visual-acuity analyses. No pre-specified SAP is available to establish which analysis was selected in advance as the primary analysis for the specific result assessed.

**Motivation for judgment**

The article reports a plausible primary 5-year visual-acuity analysis and does not provide direct evidence that the reported numerical result was selectively chosen because of its direction, magnitude, or statistical significance. However, low risk of bias requires verification that the analysis followed a pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded data were available, and that selection from multiple eligible measurements or analyses was unlikely. This cannot be established from the supplied article alone. The appropriate judgment is therefore **some concerns**, not high risk.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgment — **High risk of bias**

**Overall judgment: High risk of bias.**

No individual domain was judged at high risk. However, the result has **some concerns in multiple domains**:

1. insufficient reporting of sequence generation, allocation concealment, and baseline randomized-group comparability;
2. substantial missing 5-year visual-acuity data, with approximately one quarter of randomized participants not completing the 5-year assessment and no sensitivity analysis showing robustness to plausible missing outcomes; and
3. inability to verify that the reported 5-year visual-acuity analysis was selected according to a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available.

Taken together, these multiple concerns substantially lower confidence in the reported 5-year visual-acuity comparison. In accordance with the specified RoB 2 overall-judgment rule, the result is therefore judged to be at **high overall risk of bias**.
