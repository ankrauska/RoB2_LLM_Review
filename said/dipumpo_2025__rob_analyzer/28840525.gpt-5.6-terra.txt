## RoB 2 assessment

**Target result assessed:** effect of assignment to **silastic-ring laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (SR-LRYGB)** versus **laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG)** on **type 2 diabetes remission at 12 months**, defined as **HbA1c <42 mmol/mol (<6.0%) without glucose-lowering medication** (SR-LRYGB 29/56 [52%] versus LSG 26/53 [49%]; *p*=0.85).  
**Effect of interest:** intention-to-treat effect (effect of assignment to surgery at randomization).  
**Design:** individually randomized, parallel-group trial.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states that randomisation used “**computer-generated random number codes**,” with minimization according to age, BMI, diabetes duration, insulin treatment, and ethnicity. A computer-generated sequence with a random element is an appropriate randomization method under RoB 2.

- **1.2 Was allocation concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  The codes were “**managed by an independent study member**” and “**disclosed after anaesthesia induction**.” This indicates that the allocation was not disclosed until after the participant had proceeded to surgery and could no longer be selected into or out of the trial according to the impending allocation. The exact operational mechanism of concealment is not described (e.g., central electronic system or sequentially numbered opaque envelopes), which is a reporting limitation; however, the reported independent management and disclosure only after anaesthetic induction support a judgement that concealment was probably maintained.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  The groups were similar in reported baseline age, BMI, HbA1c, diabetes duration, insulin use, and ethnicity. There was a borderline difference in baseline HADS anxiety score (*p*=0.05), but this isolated finding is compatible with chance and does not constitute evidence of a problematic randomization process. No substantial imbalance in group numbers was present (56 versus 58 randomized), and there is no reported imbalance in a key prognostic factor that is clearly unlikely to have occurred by chance.

**Judgement: Low risk of bias.**  
The allocation sequence was computer generated, allocation appears to have been concealed through independent management until anaesthesia induction, and baseline data do not suggest failure of randomization.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  The trial is described as double blind, with participants masked to allocation. Identical port placement and use of a methylene blue leak test regardless of procedure were used, which supports the effort to maintain participant blinding.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The surgeons necessarily knew which surgical procedure was performed. Therefore, the people delivering the intervention were aware of assigned intervention.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The article reports a standardized post-operative medication approach: all diabetes drugs were discontinued after surgery subject to endocrinologist review, and subsequent metabolic medication decisions followed a standard medical review protocol delivered by endocrinologists blinded to surgical allocation. The report does not identify differential, non-protocol co-interventions or failures in protocol implementation attributable to the trial context.

  Surgical complications and subsequent clinical interventions occurred in both groups. These events are reported as consequences of clinical care and complications, not as trial-context deviations designed or likely to undermine the assigned comparison.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  The reported diabetes-remission result includes all participants with available 12-month outcome data in their randomized groups: 56/56 SR-LRYGB participants and 53/58 LSG participants. There is no indication that participants were analysed according to surgery actually received rather than randomized surgery, or that eligible participants with observed outcomes were excluded because of adherence, crossover, or post-randomization clinical events. The analysis is therefore consistent with a modified intention-to-treat analysis excluding participants with missing outcome data. Bias from those missing data is assessed separately in Domain 3.

**Judgement: Low risk of bias.**  
Although surgeons were unblinded, participants and outcome assessors were intended to be blinded, post-operative diabetes treatment was protocolized and delivered by endocrinologists blinded to allocation, and no trial-context deviations likely to affect diabetes-remission outcomes are reported. The available-case analysis retains participants in their randomized groups.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  At 12 months, outcome data were available for all 56 SR-LRYGB participants but for only 53 of 58 LSG participants. Thus, 5/58 (8.6%) randomized LSG participants had no 12-month data, compared with 0/56 SR-LRYGB participants. The result for diabetes remission is based on 109 of 114 randomized participants (95.6%) overall, but the missingness is entirely confined to one randomized group.

  The observed between-group difference for the selected diabetes-remission definition is small: 52% versus 49%. The five missing LSG outcomes could therefore plausibly change both the magnitude and direction of the comparison. Consequently, the missing data cannot be regarded as sufficiently few to have made no important difference to this result.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  No sensitivity analysis, imputation analysis, inverse-probability-weighted analysis, or other analysis addressing the possible impact of the five missing LSG outcomes is reported.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  The reported reasons for LSG loss to follow-up were “failure to make contact and/or to attend appointments beyond 3 months post-operatively.” Non-attendance or loss of contact could be associated with postoperative health status, glycaemic control, treatment satisfaction, complications, use of medication, or other factors related to the true 12-month diabetes-remission outcome. The article does not provide evidence that the missingness was unrelated to outcome.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Yes.**  
  Missingness was unbalanced: 5/58 participants were missing in LSG and none in SR-LRYGB. Under RoB 2, a difference between intervention groups in the proportion of missing outcome data is evidence supporting the possibility that missingness depended on the true outcome value. In this trial, that imbalance is particularly important because the observed remission rates are very similar between groups and the missing LSG participants could materially alter the comparison. No analysis demonstrates robustness to plausible outcomes among those missing participants.

**Judgement: High risk of bias.**  
The outcome was missing for 8.6% of LSG participants and for no SR-LRYGB participants. The reasons for missingness do not exclude an association with true diabetes-remission status, missingness is differentially distributed by group, and no sensitivity analysis evaluates the impact. This creates a high risk that missing outcome data biased the 12-month diabetes-remission comparison.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  Diabetes remission was assessed using HbA1c thresholds and use or non-use of glucose-lowering medication. HbA1c is an appropriate objective laboratory measure of glycaemic status for the stated outcome definition.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  The article applies the same HbA1c thresholds and medication-free criterion to both groups at the same 12-month time point. It does not report differential laboratory methods, timing, thresholds, or ascertainment procedures between groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — No.**  
  The study is explicitly described as double blind with “patient and assessor” blinding. Further, clinical data were extracted through medical-record review, and endocrinologists responsible for postoperative medication review were blinded to treatment allocation.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable.**  
  Outcome assessors were reported to be blinded. In addition, HbA1c is an objective biochemical measure, reducing scope for subjective assessment bias.

**Judgement: Low risk of bias.**  
The outcome definition used objective laboratory measurement and medication status, was applied comparably between groups, and was assessed in a trial reported as having blinded outcome assessors.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

The article states that the protocol’s primary outcome was HbA1c <42 mmol/mol without glucose-lowering treatment at **5 years**, whereas the current article reports several 12-month HbA1c thresholds. No finalized, date-stamped statistical analysis plan is available in the provided material to establish that the exact 12-month result assessed was pre-specified before unblinded data were available.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The article reports diabetes remission at 12 months using four thresholds: HbA1c ≤38, <42, <48, and <53 mmol/mol, each without medication. It also reports repeated HbA1c measurements at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. These constitute multiple possible measurements/time points within the glycaemic-control/diabetes-remission domain. The available protocol and registry information do not provide sufficient detail to determine whether all relevant threshold definitions and time points were pre-specified or whether the reported results were selected based on findings.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The article describes analyses using Fisher’s exact test for remission proportions and a repeated-measures mixed model for serial HbA1c and BMI. However, the available material does not include a finalized pre-specified analysis plan defining the preferred analysis for each remission definition, handling of missing outcomes, or the exact analysis population. Therefore, it cannot be determined whether the reported numerical result was selected from multiple analyses.

**Judgement: Some concerns.**  
There is no evidence that the specific 12-month diabetes-remission result was analysed according to a detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available. Multiple HbA1c thresholds and multiple follow-up time points were reported, but the available registration and protocol information do not allow selection of the reported threshold, time point, and analysis to be excluded.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement — **High risk of bias**

The overall judgement is **high risk of bias** because the result is at **high risk of bias in Domain 3 (missing outcome data)**.

The primary concern is the complete absence of 12-month diabetes-remission data for 5/58 LSG participants, while all SR-LRYGB participants had data. The loss to follow-up was unbalanced, its reported reasons do not demonstrate that it was unrelated to the true remission outcome, and no sensitivity analysis tested whether plausible outcomes among the five missing participants would change the conclusion. Since the observed remission proportions were nearly identical (52% versus 49%), the missing outcomes could materially affect the estimated comparative effect.
