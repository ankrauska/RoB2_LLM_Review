## RoB 2 assessment

**Study design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial.  
**Comparison assessed:** Fixed-criterion incentive schedule versus percentile incentive schedule.  
**Result assessed:** Maintenance of smoking abstinence, assessed by consecutive breath carbon monoxide (CO) samples <3 ppm during the 60-visit treatment phase, including the reported comparison that fixed-criterion participants maintained abstinence longer than percentile participants (*z* = 2.07, *p* = 0.04).  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to the incentive schedule at randomization (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Sources available for this assessment:** The supplied journal article and its tables/figures only. No protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial registration record, or other prespecified analysis document was supplied.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?** **Probably yes.** The report states that, after the abstinence trial, “HTT participants were randomly assigned” and that assignment was stratified by intention to use cessation medication and order of study entry. Within each group of five participants completing intake, two were assigned to percentile, two to fixed criterion, and one to random incentives. This indicates an intended random component, although the exact sequence-generation method is not described.
- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned?** **No information.** The article does not report central randomization, use of an independent allocator, sealed opaque sequential envelopes, or another method ensuring concealment. The constrained allocation of two percentile, two fixed-criterion, and one random participant within each group of five could potentially be predictable near the end of each block if allocations were known, but the article does not provide enough information to determine whether this occurred.
- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization?** **Probably no.** The groups were reported as statistically similar on intake measures except for vocational/technical/community-college education, which was more frequent in the percentile group than the fixed-criterion group (*χ²* = 8.46, *p* < 0.01). Given the numerous baseline characteristics presented, this isolated difference does not by itself demonstrate a randomization failure. Group sizes for the two principal groups (36 and 40) are also broadly consistent with the intended allocation.

**Motivation for judgment**

The study describes random assignment and stratification, but it does not report how the random sequence was generated in sufficient detail or, critically, how allocation was concealed. Under RoB 2, absence of information about allocation concealment produces **some concerns** when baseline data do not themselves indicate a randomization problem. The observed education imbalance alone is insufficient evidence of a compromised randomization process.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention?** **Yes.** Immediately after randomization, participants were told the incentive contingency for their assigned group. The intervention schedules were intrinsically distinguishable: percentile participants were told the CO criterion would change to help them smoke less, fixed-criterion participants were told incentives were available only for not smoking, and random-incentive participants were told incentives depended on attendance rather than smoking.
- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the intervention aware of assigned intervention?** **Yes.** Study personnel had to determine eligibility for payment and inform participants about the applicable incentive contingencies and subsequent criteria. Consequently, the intervention could not have been delivered without personnel knowing the allocated schedule.
- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?** **No information.** The paper does not report whether knowledge of assignment caused differential co-interventions, encouragement, management, or other deviations from the assigned protocols.
- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?** **No.** The principal maintenance comparison was restricted to participants who had already produced at least one CO sample <3 ppm: 23 of 36 percentile participants and 25 of 40 fixed-criterion participants. The reported longer maintenance in the fixed-criterion group was therefore based on a post-randomization subset rather than all randomized participants.
- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of failure to analyse participants in the groups to which they were randomized?** **Yes.** Approximately 36% of percentile participants and 38% of fixed-criterion participants did not produce any abstinent CO sample and were excluded from the maintenance analysis. Conditioning the comparison on achieving initial abstinence after randomization can alter the comparability achieved by randomization. It can also produce a selected subset whose composition may differ between groups for prognostic factors related to both initiating and maintaining abstinence.

**Motivation for judgment**

The trial was open-label, but the decisive issue is the analysis used for the maintenance result. The reported comparison of abstinence maintenance was not an intention-to-treat analysis of all randomized participants. Instead, it excluded participants who did not achieve at least one abstinent breath CO sample after randomization. This is a post-randomization exclusion and is not an appropriate analysis for the effect of assignment to the intervention schedules. The excluded proportion was substantial. Therefore, this domain is at **high risk of bias**.

This judgment applies to the reported maintenance result. It does not imply that the incentive schedules were not delivered as described; rather, it concerns the validity of the reported randomized-group comparison after selecting participants on a post-randomization outcome.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for the abstinence-maintenance outcome available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants?** **No.** Participants were expected to provide 60 weekday CO samples. Mean numbers of submitted samples were 42/60 (SD 22) in the percentile group and 40/60 (SD 24) in the fixed-criterion group. Thus, on average, approximately 30% and 33%, respectively, of scheduled CO measurements were unavailable. These quantities are not sufficiently small to conclude that missing measurements could not materially affect the result.
- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?** **No.** The article reports attendance totals but provides no sensitivity analysis, no analysis addressing potentially informative missingness, and no evidence showing that conclusions are robust to plausible outcomes for missed visits.
- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?** **Probably yes.** In a smoking-abstinence study requiring repeated attendance and breath CO measurement, failure to attend a scheduled visit may plausibly be related to smoking status, ability to sustain abstinence, motivation, or related factors. The study does not provide evidence excluding this possibility.
- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?** **No information.** Reasons for missed visits, their distribution over time, and their relationship to smoking status are not reported. The article therefore does not allow a supported determination that missingness was or was not related to the unobserved CO outcome.

**Motivation for judgment**

The amount of missing scheduled CO data was substantial in both groups. The study did not report reasons for missed visits, methods to deal with missing outcome data, or sensitivity analyses. For the repeated abstinence outcome, missed visits may be related to the participant’s underlying smoking or abstinence status; this possibility cannot be excluded from the reported information. Under RoB 2, substantial missing outcome data, no evidence that missingness did not bias the result, and unresolved potential dependence of missingness on the true outcome lead to a **high risk of bias** judgment.

The paper also states that missed visits reset the incentive value. However, this does not establish that missing CO outcomes were validly handled for the reported abstinence-maintenance analysis, nor does it address whether the missingness process was related to unobserved smoking status.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?** **No.** Smoking abstinence was assessed using objective expired-air CO measurement with Vitalograph CO monitors. Participants followed a described standardized procedure, and the prespecified abstinence threshold was CO <3 ppm.
- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?** **No.** The same CO-monitoring procedure, abstinence threshold, and weekday visit schedule were described for all groups.
- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received?** **Probably yes.** Personnel administering incentives were likely aware of group assignment because the groups had different payment contingencies. However, the CO reading was generated by a monitor according to a standardized procedure.
- **4.4 Could outcome assessment have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?** **No.** The outcome is an objective instrument-recorded CO concentration. The report does not indicate that staff made discretionary clinical judgments in determining whether the CO value met the <3 ppm abstinence threshold.

**Motivation for judgment**

Although participants and study personnel were not blinded to the assigned incentive schedules, the outcome was based on an objective, standardized breath CO measurement and a defined numerical threshold. The measurement procedure was the same across groups, and outcome classification did not require subjective judgment by an assessor. Therefore, knowledge of intervention allocation was not likely to influence measurement of the outcome. This domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data analysed in accordance with a prespecified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available?** **No information.** No protocol, statistical analysis plan, or dated trial registry entry was available in the supplied material. The article itself does not establish that the reported analyses were specified before outcome data were available.
- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?** **No information.** The study reports several related abstinence outcomes during the 60-visit intervention: at least one abstinent sample, time to first abstinent sample, total number of abstinent samples among initiators, consecutive abstinent samples, the probability of a second consecutive abstinent sample, and figures showing abstinence patterns across visits. There is no prespecified document to establish which measure of abstinence maintenance was primary or whether the reported result was selected based on its findings.
- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses?** **No information.** The article reports analyses across three groups, then additionally performs percentile-versus-fixed-criterion Mann–Whitney comparisons even when the three-group Kruskal–Wallis test was not statistically significant. Sex-adjusted analyses are also reported. Without an available prespecified analysis plan, it cannot be verified whether the specific reported maintenance comparison and analytic approach were selected in advance rather than after inspection of results.

**Motivation for judgment**

There is no direct evidence that the reported abstinence-maintenance result was selected because it was statistically significant or otherwise favorable. Therefore, a high-risk judgment is not justified. However, the absence of a protocol, registry record, or prespecified statistical analysis plan prevents verification that this specific outcome definition, time-related maintenance measure, subgroup restriction, and analysis were prespecified. Given multiple eligible abstinence measures and analyses reported in the article, this domain raises **some concerns**.

A reporting inconsistency also remains unresolved: the abstract refers to 93 hard-to-treat smokers, whereas the Methods, Table 1, and reported randomized group totals indicate 94 participants (36 + 40 + 18). This discrepancy does not itself prove selective reporting, but it adds to uncertainty about the completeness and consistency of reporting.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgment — **High risk of bias**

The overall judgment is **high risk of bias** because at least one domain is at high risk of bias:

- **Bias due to deviations from intended interventions:** high risk, because the maintenance analysis was restricted to participants who achieved initial abstinence after randomization rather than analysing all randomized participants in their assigned groups.
- **Bias due to missing outcome data:** high risk, because a substantial proportion of scheduled CO measurements was missing, with no reported reasons, no analysis addressing informative missingness, and no sensitivity analysis.

In addition, there are **some concerns** regarding allocation concealment and selection of the reported result. Consequently, confidence in the reported finding that fixed-criterion incentives produced better abstinence maintenance than percentile incentives is substantially reduced.
