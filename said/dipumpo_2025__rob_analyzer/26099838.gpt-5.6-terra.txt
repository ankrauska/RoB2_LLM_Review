## RoB 2 assessment

**Result assessed:** Effect of the **3D-printed spinal-fracture model (3Dp)** versus **two-dimensional CT teaching module (CT)** on the **total score of correct answers to the 10 spinal-fracture anatomy questions**, assessed immediately at the end of the teaching session. The reported comparison was 3Dp versus CT: mean difference **3.075** correct answers; *p* < 0.0001.

**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to the teaching module (intention-to-treat effect).

**Design:** Individually randomized, three-group parallel randomized controlled study. The article reports that all 120 randomized students completed the study.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article explicitly states: “**Students were randomized into three groups using a computer program**.” Computer-generated allocation constitutes a random component in sequence generation.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The report does not describe who generated the allocation, whether the computer program was controlled independently, how allocation was communicated, or whether the assignment could have been known by those enrolling students before enrolment. No method of allocation concealment is reported.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  Each group contained 40 students. Sex distribution was similar: CT 22/40 female, 3D 21/40 female, and 3Dp 22/40 female. Reported mean age and years in medical school were also broadly similar across groups. The presented baseline information does not show an imbalance that clearly suggests a failure of randomization. However, baseline comparability was reported only for sex, age, and school age; it does not establish comparability of prior anatomical knowledge.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The sequence was generated using a computer program, and the available baseline data do not indicate a problem with randomization. However, the article provides no information on allocation concealment. Under RoB 2, no information on concealment, in the absence of baseline imbalances suggesting a randomization failure, leads to **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Blinding of students was not feasible: students in the CT group viewed CT images, those in the 3D group used adjustable 3D computer images, and those in the 3Dp group received physical fracture models. Thus, participants necessarily knew the modality to which they had been assigned.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Teachers delivering the sessions necessarily knew the teaching modality used in each group. The report states that “**a teacher provided a detailed interpretation of the fracture in each group**.”

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The three teaching modules were intentionally different and are the interventions being compared. The article reports that each group received its assigned modality and that a teacher interpreted the fracture in each group. It does not report protocol-inconsistent co-interventions, switching between teaching modules, failure to provide the assigned teaching modality, or differential additional instruction arising from the trial context.

- **2.4–2.5 — Not applicable.**  
  Since no trial-context deviations from the intended interventions were reported, their effect on the outcome and balance between groups do not require assessment.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  All 120 students were randomized, and the article states that “**no one dropped out**.” The results include all 40 students allocated to each group. There is no indication that students were excluded after randomization or analysed according to a teaching module other than the one to which they were assigned. Two-way ANOVA was used for the score outcome, with teaching module and sex examined.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Participants and teachers were aware of the allocated teaching intervention, which is inherent to this educational comparison. However, lack of blinding alone does not lead to bias in this RoB 2 domain for the effect of assignment. The report does not identify deviations from the intended teaching modules that arose because of the trial context and could have affected scores. All randomized participants were included in the analysis. Therefore, the result is judged at **low risk of bias** due to deviations from intended interventions.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  The primary assessed outcome was the number of correct answers to 10 predefined anatomy questions. Table 1 provides the questions and explicitly specifies the correct answer for each. A total correct-answer score is an appropriate method for assessing performance on this immediate anatomy-identification test. The article does not report evidence that the test was validated, but it also does not report evidence that the measurement method was unsuitable for its stated outcome.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  At the end of class, “**each student answered 10 questions regarding spinal fractures**” using the same examination paper, which “**contained many ‘3D images’**.” The same question set, answer format, and end-of-class assessment procedure were used across the CT, 3D, and 3Dp groups. The article does not report different scoring thresholds, different examinations, or different assessment times by group.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — No / probably no.**  
  The score was determined by objectively correct or incorrect answers to predefined questions. Table 1 identifies the correct response for each question. The article does not state that scorers were blinded; however, scoring of predefined multiple-choice or binary responses does not require discretionary judgement about the outcome.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — No.**  
  For the total score, the outcome is based on whether each response matched a predefined correct answer. This is an observer-reported outcome not involving substantive judgement in outcome assessment. Knowledge of a student’s assigned teaching modality would not be expected to alter whether an answer was scored correct or incorrect.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
All groups completed the same standardized examination immediately after teaching, and correct responses were predefined. The reported total score was therefore measured comparably across groups and did not require subjective judgement by an assessor. The absence of reported assessor blinding does not create a material risk of measurement bias for this objectively scored outcome.

The result could potentially have been analysed in different eligible ways, including unadjusted group comparisons, models adjusted for age or school age, analyses with or without sex interaction terms, or alternative methods for the bounded total-score outcome. The article does not establish that the reported analysis was selected independently of the results. There is no direct evidence of selective analysis reporting.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
No prospective protocol, registry entry, or statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied article. Consequently, it cannot be verified that the reported result and analysis were chosen according to intentions finalized before unblinded data were available. The article does not provide direct evidence that selective reporting occurred; therefore, this domain is assessed as **some concerns**, not high risk of bias.
