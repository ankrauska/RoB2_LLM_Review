## RoB 2 assessment

**Result assessed:** effect of assignment to survey **delivery method** (postal mail, email, or hybrid) on the **rate of completed questionnaires with no missing item data** during March–April 2012. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)**.

This is an individually randomized, three-group parallel survey trial. RoB 2 is outcome-specific; this assessment applies to the reported completed-survey response-rate result, not to response time or economic cost outcomes.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The report states that pharmacists “were randomized” to postal mail, email, or hybrid delivery and that the sample was stratified by delivery method, questionnaire length, item placement, and gender. However, it does not report how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, random permuted blocks, or another random mechanism). The statement that the sample was “stratified” does not establish that allocation within strata was random.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  The article provides no description of allocation concealment. In particular, it does not state who assigned delivery methods, whether assignment was centrally controlled, or whether investigators handling the samples could foresee delivery assignments.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with randomization? — No information.**  
  Table 1 presents characteristics only for survey respondents, not for all 7,200 randomized pharmacists. Such post-randomization respondent-only characteristics cannot validly establish baseline balance in the randomized groups. Moreover, substantial differences are shown among respondents—for example, the proportion of Black respondents was 3.2% in postal mail, 26.4% in email, and 19.2% in hybrid groups—but these may reflect differential response rather than a failure of randomization. Therefore, the available information cannot determine whether randomization produced comparable groups at baseline.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The report uses the term “randomized,” but does not document either random sequence generation or allocation concealment. The reported respondent-only demographic table does not resolve this uncertainty. Under RoB 2, absence of information on allocation concealment, with no usable baseline data for all randomized participants, leads to **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Participants necessarily knew whether they received a postal questionnaire, an email invitation, or a mailed postcard directing them to an online survey.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably yes.**  
  Survey personnel had to implement different delivery procedures: postal participants received mailed questionnaires and reminders; email participants received electronic invitations and reminders; hybrid participants received postal postcards linking to an online questionnaire. Thus, the personnel delivering the intervention were aware of delivery assignment.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The different reminder procedures were pre-specified components of the three delivery strategies, rather than deviations from them. The report does not describe crossovers between delivery modes, unauthorized co-interventions, or departures from the planned procedures caused by the trial context.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably no.**  
  The analysis excluded 233 randomized pharmacists from the response-rate denominators because questionnaires were undeliverable: 139 in postal mail and 94 in hybrid delivery; no email questionnaires were classified as undeliverable. The authors calculated response rates using the number of “eligible respondents” after subtracting undeliverable questionnaires, rather than using all 2,400 pharmacists assigned to each delivery strategy. The report does not define prospectively whether undeliverable contact information rendered a pharmacist ineligible for the trial outcome, nor does it justify excluding these randomized individuals.

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of failure to analyse all participants in their randomized groups? — Probably no.**  
  The exclusions were unequally distributed across groups, but the reported contrast in completed-response rates was large: postal mail 21.0%, email 6.8%, and hybrid 3.2%. Including undeliverable questionnaires as non-completed responses would reduce the postal-mail response rate from 21.0% (474/2,261) to 19.8% (474/2,400) and the hybrid rate from 3.2% (73/2,306) to 3.0% (73/2,400), while the email rate remains 6.8% (163/2,400). On the available counts, the exclusions do not appear sufficient to reverse or substantially alter the main conclusion that postal mail had the highest complete-response rate.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Lack of blinding is inherent to the delivery-method comparison and, by itself, does not establish bias. The principal concern is that the response-rate analysis excluded undeliverable questionnaires after randomization, with unequal numbers excluded across groups and no clear indication that all such pharmacists were truly ineligible. The available data suggest this had no substantial numerical impact on the principal comparison, so this domain is judged **some concerns**, not high risk of bias.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Yes.**  
  For the outcome assessed—whether a pharmacist returned a questionnaire completed with no missing item data—the flow diagram reports outcome status for all 7,200 assigned pharmacists. It reports 474 complete surveys in postal mail, 163 in email, and 73 in hybrid, as well as non-returned and undeliverable questionnaires. Thus, each randomized participant was classified as having completed the questionnaire or not completed it.

- **3.2–3.4 — Not applicable.**  
  Further assessment of missing outcome data is not required because completion status was available for all randomized participants.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Non-return of a questionnaire is not missing outcome information for the response-rate outcome; it is the outcome category of non-completion. Likewise, returned questionnaires with incomplete item data were identifiable and did not meet the study’s stated definition of a completed questionnaire. The study therefore provides the information required to determine complete-response status for the entire randomized sample. This domain is at **low risk of bias**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  The outcome was operationally defined as return of a questionnaire “with no missing data for any questions.” Recording whether a survey was returned and whether all questionnaire items were completed is an appropriate method for measuring the study’s stated complete-response outcome.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  Although the questionnaire was delivered differently in each group, the same substantive criterion was applied in all groups: a completed questionnaire required no missing data for any question. The article reports completed surveys using this definition for postal, email, and hybrid methods.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Probably yes.**  
  Survey staff could identify whether a completed response arrived by post or through the online system.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — No.**  
  Whether a questionnaire was returned and contained missing items is determined from administrative records and questionnaire completeness. This is an observer-recorded outcome not requiring substantive judgement about participants’ status. Knowledge of delivery method would not reasonably alter whether a returned questionnaire met the stated no-missing-data criterion.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The complete-response outcome was objectively ascertainable from returned postal forms and online survey records. Although personnel were aware of delivery method, the outcome classification did not require judgement likely to be influenced by that knowledge. The domain is therefore judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The article does not cite or provide a dated trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial-registry record, or other document demonstrating that the response-rate analysis was specified before outcome data were available.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The article reports complete-questionnaire response rate, returned-survey counts, incomplete returned surveys, and a combined response rate involving complete and incomplete surveys in the discussion. Since no pre-specified plan is available, it cannot be verified whether the reported complete-response definition was chosen before results were known or selected from alternative eligible response-rate definitions.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The report does not provide a pre-specified plan for the denominator definition, treatment of undeliverable questionnaires, analytic model, or comparison strategy. The reported analysis excludes undeliverable questionnaires from the response-rate denominator. Without an analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available, it is not possible to determine whether this analytic choice or other possible analyses were selected on the basis of the observed findings.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
There is no evidence of an outcome-analysis plan finalized before the investigators had access to unblinded outcome data. The article alone does not establish that the reported response-rate definition and treatment of undeliverable questionnaires were pre-specified. There is also no direct evidence that the investigators selected the result because of its direction, magnitude, or statistical significance. Accordingly, this domain has **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

The complete-survey response-rate result is at low risk of bias for missing outcome data and outcome measurement. However:

1. the report does not provide sufficient information on random sequence generation or allocation concealment;  
2. the response-rate analysis excludes undeliverable questionnaires after randomization, without clearly establishing that these pharmacists were ineligible for the outcome analysis; and  
3. no pre-specified, date-stamped analysis plan, protocol, or registry record is available to verify that the reported response-rate definition and analysis were selected before unblinded data were available.

No individual domain reaches high risk of bias on the information reported. The concerns do not, on their own, establish a sufficiently material threat to justify an overall high-risk judgement. Therefore, following the specified RoB 2 rules, the overall judgement is **Some concerns**.
