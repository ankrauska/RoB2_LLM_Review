## Scope of the RoB 2 assessment

- **Design:** Prospective, randomized, double-blind, within-participant (paired-site) controlled trial. Each of the 35 participants received both formulations, randomly allocated to the left or right cubital fossa.
- **Experimental intervention:** Propanol-based hand rub containing 0.81% (w/w) emollients.
- **Comparator:** Otherwise equivalent propanol-based hand rub without emollients.
- **Result assessed:** ITT comparison of the primary outcome, the summed visual erythema-plus-dryness score across all assessment time points. Reported means were **0.8 ± 2.4** with emollients and **1.5 ± 3.5** without emollients; Wilcoxon signed-rank test **P = 0.022**.
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to the allocated formulation/site (intention-to-treat effect).
- **Sources available for this assessment:** The supplied journal article and figures/tables only. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, prospectively registered trial record, or other pre-unblinding analysis document was supplied.

The trial is a paired within-person design rather than a conventional parallel-group trial. The five standard RoB 2 domains are nevertheless assessed for the specified primary ITT result.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states that allocation of the two coded products to the right and left cubital fossae was performed using “**randomly permuted blocks of fixed size**” with “**validated software RANCODE 3.6**.” This is a random sequence-generation method.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Probably yes.**  
  Participants had already been screened and enrolled before the site-specific allocation was applied. The two formulations were coded as A and B; the article states that the “**blinding codes were kept sealed until closure of final database**.” The randomization scheme was prepared by the Department of Statistics and Data Management. Thus, the identities of A and B were concealed during conduct of the study. Although the article does not provide a separate detailed description of the operational process by which the left/right allocation list was concealed from the study nurse, the reported coding and sealed unblinding process provide reasonable support for concealment relevant to this within-person allocation.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No information.**  
  Baseline demographic characteristics are reported for participants, but not separately for the skin sites allocated to each formulation. In this paired-site design, both interventions were applied to opposite cubital fossae of the same individuals, which substantially limits participant-level baseline prognostic imbalance between formulations. Baseline skin scores by allocated side are not reported; therefore, a direct side-specific baseline comparison cannot be made from the article.

**Judgement and motivation**

The allocation sequence was explicitly computer-generated using random permuted blocks. Product identity was blinded through coded formulations, with codes sealed until database closure. There is no reported indication that allocation was manipulated or that the randomization process failed. The absence of separately reported baseline site-level scores does not itself demonstrate an imbalance or a randomization failure. Therefore, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  The trial is described as “**double-blind**.” The formulations were randomly assigned the codes A and B, and the treatment identities remained blinded until database closure.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no.**  
  All applications were performed by a study nurse, using coded products. The study report states that the products were blinded as A and B and that unblinding took place only after final database closure. There is no report that the nurse knew which code corresponded to the emollient-containing formulation.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Not applicable.**  
  Because participants and intervention personnel were blinded, the RoB 2 signalling pathway concerning trial-context deviations due to awareness is not triggered. The article does not report differential non-protocol co-interventions or implementation failures arising from knowledge of product identity.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The principal result was presented for the ITT population of all 35 participants. Each participant contributed data under both allocated preparations. The three participants with protocol violations were retained in the ITT analysis. The report states: “**Results are presented for the intention-to-treat (ITT) population**” and “**Missing values in 3 participants were replaced by the method of Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF)**.” LOCF concerns handling of missing outcome observations and is considered under Domain 3; it did not reassign treatments or exclude these participants from the ITT result.

**Judgement and motivation**

The report describes a double-blind trial using coded products, and no evidence is reported of differential deviations from the allocated products caused by the trial context. The primary numerical result is based on the ITT population, retaining all 35 participants in their randomized within-person allocations. The three protocol violations led to a separate per-protocol population but did not replace the ITT primary analysis. Therefore, for the effect of assignment to intervention, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably no.**  
  Data were not fully observed for all participants: “**Missing values in 3 participants were replaced by the method of Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF)**.” Three of 35 participants had missing values, corresponding to 8.6% of the ITT population. The article does not state how many values were missing for each participant, at which time points they were missing, or whether missingness occurred for one or both treatment sites.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  LOCF is not, by itself, evidence that the result was protected from bias due to missing outcome data. No sensitivity analysis examining plausible alternative values for missing data is reported.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Probably no.**  
  The article identifies the reasons for the three exclusions from the per-protocol population as protocol violations: one participant took an anti-inflammatory drug and two disinfected the cubital fossa. These are documented deviations from study requirements rather than reported withdrawals due to erythema, dryness, skin deterioration, lack of efficacy, or adverse reactions to either study formulation. No participant is reported to have discontinued because of the primary outcome or a treatment-related adverse event. The reported adverse events were mild, systemic events—headache, common cold, tooth pain, and acute bronchitis—and “**none of them were considered to be related to any of the 2 study preparations**.”

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Not applicable.**

**Judgement and motivation**

Outcome observations were incomplete for three participants and were handled using LOCF; this method does not demonstrate that missing data could not bias the estimate. However, the reported reasons for the missingness/protocol exclusions were intake of antiphlogistics or disinfection of the test site, not reported worsening or improvement in erythema or dryness and not reported treatment-related adverse events. On the information supplied, missingness is not reported to be related to the unobserved true irritation outcome. Consequently, the result is judged at **low risk of bias** due to missing outcome data.

**Limitation of this assessment:** The report does not provide participant-level missing-data patterns, treatment-side-specific missingness, timing of the missing observations, or sensitivity analyses. This limits detailed verification of the LOCF-based ITT analysis, but the documented reasons for the three protocol violations do not indicate outcome-dependent missingness.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  The primary outcome comprised visual assessments of erythema and dryness, both defined using stated ordinal scales. Erythema was graded from 0 to 4, and dryness from 0 to 3. Assessments were made repeatedly before applications and at the specified assessment days. The article identifies erythema and dryness as key symptoms of irritant contact dermatitis. No information is presented indicating that the specified method was invalid or unable to detect relevant changes.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  Both products were applied to comparable anatomical sites—the left and right cubital fossae—of the same participant. Both sides received the same volume, application procedure, application frequency, and assessment schedule. The same dermatologist assessed both sites. There is no report of differential outcome-ascertainment procedures between the formulations.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — Probably no.**  
  The report states: “**All evaluations were conducted blindly by the same investigator**.” Product codes were kept sealed until closure of the final database.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable.**  
  The outcome assessor was blinded.

**Judgement and motivation**

The outcome was measured with pre-described visual scoring scales, at the same time points and with the same procedures for both allocated sites. All evaluations were undertaken blindly by the same dermatologist. The report provides no evidence that the assessment method differed between products or that the blinded assessor knew the product identities. Accordingly, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The article describes an intended primary objective and statistical approach: non-inferiority based on the irritation sum score, a one-sided 97.5% confidence interval using Fieller’s method, a non-inferiority margin of 1.2, and a stated plan to assess superiority after non-inferiority. However, no protocol, dated statistical analysis plan, or trial-registry record finalized before access to unblinded outcome data was available for review. The article alone cannot establish that the reported analysis was finalized before unblinded data were available.

- **5.2 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  The primary outcome was specified in the article as the sum of erythema and dryness scores over all assessment time points. The report also presents separate erythema and dryness results and figures showing repeated time-point results. Because several measurements and time points were available, and no prospectively finalized protocol or analysis plan is available, it cannot be verified whether the reported primary composite result and its time-point aggregation were selected from among multiple eligible measurements on the basis of the observed findings. The article provides no direct evidence that selection occurred.

- **5.3 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  Both ITT and per-protocol results are presented, missing values were handled by LOCF in the ITT analysis, and non-inferiority and superiority analyses were reported. The article states that superiority testing after non-inferiority was contemplated, but no externally verifiable pre-unblinding analysis plan is available. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that the ITT result, LOCF approach, statistical test, and reported analysis were selected without knowledge of unblinded results. There is no direct evidence in the article that the reported analysis was selected because it was favourable.

**Judgement and motivation**

The published report identifies the irritation sum score as the primary endpoint and describes a planned non-inferiority-to-superiority sequence. Nevertheless, **low risk of bias** in this RoB 2 domain requires a sufficiently detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available, together with evidence that the reported measurement and analysis were not selected from multiple eligible alternatives. Such documentation was not supplied. The presence of repeated outcome assessments, component outcomes, ITT and per-protocol populations, and an LOCF method means that multiple measurement and analytic choices were possible. There is no evidence of selective reporting, but there is insufficient information to exclude it. Therefore, this domain is judged as raising **some concerns**.
