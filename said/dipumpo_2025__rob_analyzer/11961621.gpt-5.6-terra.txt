## RoB 2 assessment

**Study design:** Individually randomized parallel-group trial.  
**Comparison:** Laparoscopic-assisted vaginal hysterectomy (LAVH; n=28) versus abdominal hysterectomy (AH; n=20).  
**Result assessed:** The **primary outcome**, postoperative length of hospital stay: median 6.5 days for LAVH versus 10 days for AH (reported as statistically significant, *p*<0.05).  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment to intervention** (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Sources available:** The published article and its tables/figure only. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, registry entry, or other prespecified analysis document was provided.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states: “*Procedure type was assigned by a computer-generated randomization list*.” A computer-generated allocation list includes a random component and meets the RoB 2 criterion for random sequence generation.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Yes.**  
  The report states that “*concealment of randomization was done by telephone inquiry*.” Telephone allocation is a centrally/remote-administered approach and supports adequate allocation concealment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  Baseline data are reported for all 48 randomized women. The groups were similar in age, gravidity, parity, body size measures, and estimated uterine weight according to the authors. Some numerical differences existed—for example, median estimated uterine weight was 283 g in the LAVH group and 369 g in the AH group—but the article reports no statistically significant baseline difference and provides no evidence of an implausible imbalance, marked group-size discrepancy, or other indication of manipulation or failure of randomization. The numerical differences, in this small trial, do not alone demonstrate a randomization problem.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Both random sequence generation and concealment were explicitly described using methods that meet RoB 2 standards. Reported baseline characteristics do not provide evidence that the randomization process failed. Therefore, this domain is judged **low risk of bias**.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Participants could not realistically be blinded to LAVH versus AH. The authors explicitly acknowledge that “*the patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment*.”

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Surgeons and perioperative personnel necessarily knew which surgical procedure was being performed.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information.**  
  No conversions from LAVH to AH occurred, which is reassuring. However, the report identifies a significant imbalance in operator seniority: 20/28 LAVH procedures were performed by attending physicians versus 8/20 AH procedures; correspondingly, residents performed 8/28 LAVH versus 12/20 AH procedures (*p*<0.05). The authors state that the surgeons performing LAVH were “*significantly more experienced*.”  
  The report does not state whether surgeon allocation, supervision, or experience was controlled by the protocol or whether this imbalance arose because of the trial procedures. Consequently, it cannot be established whether this difference represented a trial-context deviation from the intended intervention strategy.

- **2.4–2.5 — Not applicable / cannot be definitively assessed.**  
  Because the occurrence of relevant trial-context deviations cannot be established from the report, their effect on hospital stay and their balance across groups cannot be definitively assessed.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  The hospital-stay result is presented for all 28 randomized LAVH participants and all 20 randomized AH participants. The report does not describe post-randomization exclusions, crossover, or reclassification by treatment received for this outcome. This is consistent with analysis according to assigned groups.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
This was necessarily an open-label surgical comparison. Although all randomized patients appear to have contributed to the hospital-stay analysis and there were no conversions, there was a statistically significant imbalance in surgeon seniority/experience between intervention groups. The article does not provide sufficient information to determine whether this reflected a protocol-consistent feature of the assigned surgical strategies or a deviation attributable to the trial context. This unresolved issue creates **some concerns**, rather than high risk, for the effect of assignment to intervention.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  Hospital stay, measured as number of days from surgery until discharge, is a feasible and relevant measure of postoperative recovery. There is no indication that the measurement method itself was invalid or unable to detect differences.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The same hospital setting and the same general endpoint—discharge from hospital—were used for both groups. The report does not describe different data-collection methods or discharge-recording procedures by treatment group.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Yes.**  
  The outcome was determined by patient readiness for discharge. The report states: “*Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital.*” The patient therefore acted as the key outcome assessor/decision-maker. Patients were aware of whether they had received LAVH or AH.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.**  
  A patient’s decision that she is ready for discharge is inherently subjective and can be influenced by expectations, perceived invasiveness of the procedure, postoperative beliefs, and knowledge of the assigned surgery.

- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Yes.**  
  It is likely in this study. The surgical interventions were visibly and experientially different, the outcome was explicitly based on each patient’s own decision about readiness for discharge, and the authors themselves acknowledge that lack of blinding may have influenced patient-reported recuperation and satisfaction. The trial reports substantially less postoperative pain in the LAVH group, but the unblinded, participant-determined nature of discharge readiness means that the measured hospital-stay difference may have been influenced by knowledge and expectations regarding intervention assignment.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Although hospital stay is recorded as a numerical endpoint, discharge was not determined solely by blinded or objective criteria: each patient decided when she was ready to leave hospital. Because participants necessarily knew their assigned surgical procedure and this knowledge was likely to affect this subjective decision, there is a substantial risk of differential outcome measurement. This domain is judged **high risk of bias**. The likely direction of bias cannot be determined definitively from the report.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling-question assessment**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a prespecified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The article identifies length of hospital stay as the primary outcome and reports a sample-size calculation based on a proposed reduction in median stay from 9 to 7 days. This supports that hospital stay was planned as the primary outcome. However, no protocol, registry entry, or dated statistical analysis plan was available. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that the specific analysis approach—such as use of medians, the statistical test, handling of any data features, and timing of analysis—was finalized before unblinded outcome data became available.

- **5.2 Was the result likely selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — Probably no.**  
  The article clearly defines hospital stay as the primary outcome and reports one hospital-stay result. No alternative hospital-stay definitions, measurement time points, or selectively reported discharge endpoints are described.

- **5.3 Was the result likely selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The published report does not provide a prespecified analysis plan or enough detail to determine whether multiple statistical analyses of hospital stay were conducted and whether the reported analysis was selected based on its result.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary outcome was explicitly named, reducing concern that the outcome itself was selected post hoc. Nevertheless, the absence of a protocol, registry record, or dated statistical analysis plan prevents confirmation that the reported hospital-stay analysis was prespecified before unblinded data were available. There is no direct evidence of selective reporting, but the information is insufficient for a low-risk judgement. This domain is therefore judged as raising **some concerns**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation for overall judgement:**  
The result for the primary outcome, hospital stay, is at **high risk of bias in the domain of measurement of the outcome**. The endpoint was explicitly determined by the patient’s decision regarding readiness for discharge, while patients knew which surgery they had received. Given the subjective and expectation-sensitive nature of this decision, assessment of hospital stay was likely influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

In addition, there are **some concerns** regarding deviations from intended interventions because of the imbalance in surgeon seniority/experience between groups, and **some concerns** regarding selection of the reported result because no prespecified analysis plan was available.

Under RoB 2, a judgement of high risk in at least one domain leads to an overall **high risk of bias** judgement for this specific result: the comparison of LAVH versus AH for postoperative length of hospital stay.
