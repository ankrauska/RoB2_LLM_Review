# RoB 2 Assessment Report
**Study:** Cunningham RM, Chermack ST, Zimmerman MA, et al. "Brief Motivational Interviewing Intervention for Peer Violence and Alcohol Use in Teens: One-Year Follow-up." *Pediatrics* 2012;129:1083–1090. (SafERteens trial, NCT00251212)

**Comparison assessed:** Therapist-assisted Brief Intervention (TBI) vs. Control (brochure)
**Outcome assessed:** Severe peer aggression at 12 months (representative primary outcome; alcohol-outcome-specific comments included where relevant)
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect) — explicitly stated as ITT in the manuscript.

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process
**Judgment: Some concerns**

- **Sequence generation:** The article states participants were "randomly selected... and assigned to 1 of the 3 study conditions (TBI, CBI, control)," stratified by gender and age. No description of the actual method used to generate the random allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number tables) is provided in this report.
- **Allocation concealment:** No information is given about how the allocation sequence was concealed from research assistants approaching and enrolling participants during the ED encounter. This is important because recruitment occurred in a busy, unblinded ED environment, potentially permitting some knowledge of the upcoming allocation.
- **Baseline balance:** The text states "There were no significant differences between conditions on age, race, gender," suggesting successful randomization for demographic variables. However, Table 1 shows some baseline differences in the outcome variables themselves (e.g., severe peer aggression 82.7% [Therapist] vs. 75.5% [Computer] vs. 77.9% [Control]); no formal statistical comparison of these baseline outcome levels is reported, so it cannot be excluded that they exceed chance variation, although given three-arm randomization with reasonable samples this is most plausibly chance imbalance.
- Because concealment is not described (No information) and sequence generation is only vaguely described, and given this is a large NIH-funded academic trial (which increases the plausibility of a reasonably robust randomization process), the situation maps to "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk" or "High risk."

**Limitation/ambiguity to report:** The published article does not report the specific method of sequence generation or allocation concealment; this information may exist in an unpublished protocol/SAP not available for review.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)
**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

- Blinding of participants and personnel was not possible given the nature of the behavioral intervention (participants necessarily knew whether they received a therapist-delivered session, a computer program, or a brochure). This is inherent to this type of intervention and does not, by itself, imply bias in the ITT estimate.
- No evidence is provided that deviations from the assigned intervention occurred because of "trial context" (e.g., no contamination between arms, no report of participants seeking non-protocol interventions due to recruitment/consent procedures).
- **Analysis approach:** The manuscript explicitly states: "An intent-to-treat approach was used. All randomly assigned participants were included whether the intervention was received or not (>95% received their assigned intervention during the ED visit)." This constitutes an appropriate ITT analysis per RoB 2 criteria.
- Given no reported unbalanced non-protocol interventions and an appropriate ITT analysis with high fidelity to assigned condition (>95%), the domain is judged **Low risk**.

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data
**Judgment: Some concerns**

- 12-month follow-up was 83.6% (607/726), meaning 16.4% of randomized participants had missing outcome data — a proportion too large to be dismissed as inconsequential ("nearly all" criterion not met).
- The manuscript does not report whether the amount or reasons for missing data differed between intervention arms (TBI, CBI, control). It only notes "there were initial differences in dropping out of school by condition" (an unrelated covariate), which was controlled for in secondary analyses, but this is not the same as addressing differential loss to follow-up on the outcome itself.
- No sensitivity analyses (e.g., worst-case/best-case, pattern-mixture models) are reported to demonstrate the robustness of the 12-month findings to missing data assumptions.
- GEE was used, which can provide valid inference under a missing-at-random assumption, but no justification of this assumption (e.g., comparison of dropouts vs. completers on baseline characteristics or outcome severity) is presented in the article.
- Because missingness could plausibly be related to the true outcome value (e.g., adolescents with ongoing violence/alcohol problems could be harder to locate or less willing to complete follow-up) and this possibility is neither excluded nor confirmed, but no clear evidence indicates it was *likely* related to the true value or differed by arm, the domain is judged **Some concerns** rather than "High."

**Limitation/ambiguity to report:** The article does not report attrition rates by study arm or reasons for loss to follow-up, precluding a more precise judgment.

---

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome
**Judgment: Some concerns**

- Outcomes (peer aggression, peer victimization, violence consequences, alcohol misuse, binge drinking, alcohol consequences) were based on validated instruments (Conflict Tactics Scale, AUDIT-C, POSIT) administered via self-administered computer survey — an appropriate measurement method, not inherently flawed (Signalling Q4.1: No).
- Measurement procedures were identical across arms (same computerized self-report survey at all time points), so ascertainment method did not differ structurally between groups (Signalling Q4.2: No).
- However, the outcome assessor is, in effect, the participant (self-report). Participants were necessarily aware of which intervention they received (therapist session vs. computer vs. brochure), and self-reported behavioral/psychological outcomes are recognized in RoB 2 guidance as being potentially susceptible to influence from knowledge of intervention assignment (e.g., social desirability, demand characteristics favoring reporting improvement after receiving a more intensive/personal intervention such as the TBI).
- The trial protocol record indicates masking was applied only to "outcome assessors" as a category distinct from participants; this likely refers to blinded research staff processing data, not blinding of the self-reporting participants themselves, since the outcomes are inherently self-reported.
- There is no explicit evidence in the article that assessment was, in fact, influenced by knowledge of the intervention (e.g., no differential timing or interviewer effects reported), but the potential for such influence cannot be excluded given self-report of sensitive, socially-desirable behaviors.
- This corresponds to the "Some concerns" category (potential for influence exists, but no clear evidence that it was *likely* to have occurred).

---

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result
**Judgment: Some concerns**

- The trial was prospectively registered (NCT00251212), with primary outcomes defined as "alcohol misuse and violent behaviors" and secondary outcomes including "illicit drug use, unintentional injury, delinquency, and weapon carriage," evaluated at 3, 6, and 12 months. The outcomes reported in this 12-month article (peer aggression, peer victimization, violence consequences, alcohol misuse, binge drinking, alcohol consequences) are consistent with these pre-specified domains.
- However, a specific methodological choice raises concern: the article states, "At 3 and 6 months, the effects were noted in dichotomous treatment of the variables and not the continuous variables... therefore, only dichotomous outcomes were examined [at 12 months]." This indicates that the analytic approach (dichotomous vs. continuous outcome coding) for the 12-month analysis was chosen based on which coding had previously yielded statistically significant results in earlier follow-ups from the same dataset. This constitutes a plausible instance of selecting an analysis approach informed by prior knowledge of favorable results, which is precisely the type of bias addressed by this domain.
- No full statistical analysis plan or trial protocol with a priori specification of the dichotomous-only analytic strategy for the 12-month time point was available for verification.
- Given partial consistency with registered outcomes but a clear post hoc analytic decision informed by prior result patterns, the domain is judged **Some concerns** rather than "Low."

**Limitation/ambiguity to report:** No statistical analysis plan (SAP) or full trial protocol was accessible to confirm whether the dichotomous-only analytic strategy at 12 months was pre-specified independent of the earlier (3- and 6-month) results.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement
**Overall judgment: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** No single domain was judged "High risk" in isolation. However, four of the five domains (Randomization process, Missing outcome data, Measurement of the outcome, and Selection of the reported result) were each judged "Some concerns," while only one domain (Deviations from intended interventions) was judged "Low risk." Per the RoB 2 decision rules, when a study has "some concerns" in multiple domains in a manner that cumulatively and substantially lowers confidence in the trial result, the overall judgment should be escalated to **High risk of bias**, even though no individual domain independently reached the "High" threshold.

The specific factors driving this overall judgment are:
1. Absence of reported allocation concealment methodology (Domain 1);
2. Non-trivial (16.4%) and group-unspecified missing outcome data at 12 months without sensitivity analyses (Domain 3);
3. Reliance on self-reported, sensitive behavioral outcomes in an unblinded design, creating plausible potential for differential reporting bias (Domain 4);
4. Evidence that the analytic approach (dichotomous vs. continuous outcome variables) for the primary 12-month analysis was selected based on patterns of statistical significance observed in earlier (3- and 6-month) analyses of the same outcomes (Domain 5).

**Stated limitation:** This assessment is based solely on the information reported in the published article; details on randomization procedures, attrition by arm, and the pre-specification of analytic strategy could not be verified against a full trial protocol or statistical analysis plan, which were not available for review. This ambiguity is reported as a limitation of the current risk-of-bias judgment rather than assumed in either direction.
