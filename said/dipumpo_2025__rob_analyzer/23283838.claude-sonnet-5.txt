# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment

**Study:** High-Throughput Clinical Assay for Testing Drug Facilitation of Exposure Therapy (DCS vs. placebo, single-session public speaking exposure in social anxiety disorder)

**Outcome assessed:** Between-session habituation (categorical SUDS response) and overall change in SUDS/Social Phobia Scale scores

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (though analysis is restricted to completers — see Domain 3)

---

## Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgment: Some concerns**

- Sequence generation: The article states participants were "randomly assigned" by a data manager, but no details are provided on the method used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated randomization, block randomization). Signalling question 1.1 = No information.
- Allocation concealment: A data manager independent of participant interaction assigned participants to identical-appearing capsules. This suggests reasonable concealment, but the exact mechanism (e.g., sequential envelopes vs. centralized allocation) is not described in detail. Signalling question 1.2 = Probably yes.
- Baseline imbalance: Groups were reported to not differ on gender, ethnicity, medication use, LSAS, baseline SUDS, or Social Phobia Scale scores (all p > .16). However, a statistically significant baseline difference in **age** was found (p = .019), with the DCS group being on average 11 years older. Although the authors state this was examined and did not explain the treatment effect, this baseline imbalance raises a question about the randomization process itself, particularly given the small sample size (n=30 analyzed).

Given no information on sequence generation combined with a significant baseline imbalance (age), but adequate concealment and post-hoc reassurance that age did not confound the result, this domain is judged as **Some concerns** rather than "Low" or "High," as the imbalance is plausibly a chance finding in a small trial rather than a large-effect confound, but the absence of sequence generation details prevents a "Low" judgement.

---

## Domain 2: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**Judgment: Low risk of bias**

- Blinding: Participants, experimenters, and data collectors were blinded via identical-appearing capsules. The adequacy of blinding was formally tested by having participants and experimenters guess treatment assignment; neither could discern assignment above chance (p > .15).
- No non-protocol interventions are described that would differentially affect the groups; the exposure protocol (speech preparation and delivery) was standardized/tailored using pre-specified flexible elements, applied identically regardless of group assignment.
- No deviations arising from awareness of treatment allocation are reported (and blinding success supports this).
- Analysis: The study did **not** use a strict ITT analysis (participants who dropped out before Visit 2, n=4, were excluded entirely — see Domain 3), but for the deviations-from-intervention domain specifically (i.e., issues of blinding/co-intervention), there is no evidence of problematic deviations. The authors also tested and ruled out potential confounds (concomitant medication, antidepressant status, guesses about assignment, age) as alternative explanations, and effects remained robust.

Overall, blinding was robust and verified, and no differential deviations are apparent. This domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

---

## Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

**Judgment: High risk of bias**

- Of 34 randomized participants, 4 (2 DCS, 2 placebo) did not return for Visit 2 and were **excluded entirely from all analyses** — not merely from the second time point. The authors explicitly state: "Only those participants completing Visit 2 are analyzed."
- This is a complete-case analysis excluding ~12% of randomized participants. Although the dropout was balanced numerically between groups (2 vs. 2), the very small overall sample size (n=30 analyzed out of 34 randomized) makes even this small absolute loss a meaningful proportion.
- Critically, the primary outcome (between-session habituation) **cannot by definition be assessed** in participants who did not attend Visit 2, since it requires two data points. The authors acknowledge this directly but do not report any sensitivity analysis, imputation, or exploration of whether reasons for dropout (e.g., "became unreachable," "preferred not to continue") might be related to true outcome (e.g., anxiety-driven avoidance, which is highly plausible in a socially anxious sample asked to repeat a distressing public speaking task).
- Reasons for dropout are given (loss of contact; explicit preference to discontinue), and the latter reason ("preferred not to continue") is plausibly related to the underlying anxiety outcome — i.e., missingness may plausibly depend on the true (unmeasured) value of the outcome (e.g., greater residual fear leading to avoidance of a second distressing exposure).
- No sensitivity analyses addressing the impact of these exclusions were reported.

Because the outcome data were not available for all/nearly all randomized participants, no evidence is provided that the result is robust to the missing data, and it is plausible (particularly for a socially-avoidant clinical population undergoing an anxiety-provoking repeat task) that missingness depended on the true outcome value, this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

---

## Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Judgment: Some concerns**

- The primary outcome (SUDS) is a participant-reported outcome. Participants were the outcome assessors for this measure, and by definition could not be "blinded" to their own subjective experience; however, they were blinded to intervention allocation itself (confirmed via post-hoc guessing test), which reduces the likelihood that self-report was consciously biased by expectation of drug assignment.
- The Social Phobia Scale (a secondary/generalization outcome) is also a self-report measure, subject to the same considerations.
- Measurement methods were identical across groups and time points (standardized SUDS ratings at fixed intervals, standardized scale administration), so ascertainment did not differ structurally between groups.
- Because these are subjective, participant-reported outcomes, and knowledge of allocation could in principle influence self-reported distress even if participants could not explicitly guess their assignment (e.g., subtle expectancy effects are not fully excluded by a single guessing question, which only reached significance threshold p>.15 and may have been underpowered given small N), there remains a possibility that assessment was influenced by (unconscious) awareness/expectation effects.
- No independent, non-participant, blinded corroborating measure of anxiety (e.g., blinded observer-rated behavioral avodiance) was used to cross-validate self-report, which would have strengthened confidence in this domain.

Given the reliance on self-reported outcomes with successful but not conclusively verified blinding (small-sample guessing test), and no objective/observer-rated corroboration, this domain is judged **Some concerns**.

---

## Domain 5: Bias in selection of the reported result

**Judgment: Some concerns**

- No trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is referenced in the article. There is no mention of a publicly available protocol, prospective registration (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov), or statistical analysis plan finalized prior to unblinded data availability.
- The primary outcome (categorical habituation response, defined as ≥5-point SUDS drop) appears clearly defined and analyzed as stated, and the MANCOVA outcomes (SUDS and Social Phobia Scale) are both reported with results regardless of significance (SUDS effect was non-significant, p=.14, and this was reported transparently rather than suppressed).
- However, several additional post-hoc analyses are reported (e.g., covariate analyses including medication use, antidepressant use, age, guess of assignment; and the antidepressant x group x time trend, p=.094), which are presented as exploratory/sensitivity analyses rather than confirmatory. This is transparent, but it is unclear whether other alternative outcome definitions, cut-points, or time windows were tested and not reported, since no protocol is available for comparison.
- The absence of a pre-registered protocol/SAP does not by itself confirm selective reporting occurred, but it prevents verification of whether the reported result was selected on the basis of the results, from among multiple eligible analyses (e.g., different SUDS cut-offs for "response," different covariates).

Given the absence of a pre-specified, publicly available analysis plan and the inability to confirm whether alternative measures/analyses were considered and not reported, this domain is judged **Some concerns**.

---

## 6. Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement

**Overall judgment: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Rationale:** Per the RoB 2 decision rules, an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" is warranted if the study is at high risk in at least one domain, OR if there are "some concerns" in multiple domains in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result. In this study:

- Domain 3 (missing outcome data) is judged **High risk of bias** on its own, which is sufficient to drive the overall judgement to High risk, given that: (a) the primary outcome (between-session habituation) mechanically requires Visit 2 completion; (b) participants were excluded entirely rather than analyzed via ITT/imputation; (c) reasons for dropout (particularly "preferred not to continue") are plausibly related to the underlying anxiety construct being measured, in a socially anxious population being asked to repeat a distressing exposure task; and (d) no sensitivity analyses were performed to test robustness to this missingness.
- This High risk of bias in Domain 3 is further compounded by "Some concerns" in three additional domains (randomization process, measurement of outcome, and selection of the reported result), which independently reduce confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported effect.

**Limitations/ambiguities to note (per instructions, reported rather than assumed):**
1. No details were provided on the specific method of random sequence generation.
2. No trial registration, protocol, or statistical analysis plan was referenced, precluding verification of pre-specification of outcomes/analyses.
3. The very small sample size (n=30 analyzed) limits the ability to distinguish chance baseline imbalance (age) from genuine randomization problems, and amplifies the impact of the 4 dropouts on missing data risk.
4. The dual role of participants as both randomized subjects and outcome assessors for the primary (self-report) outcome cannot be fully separated from the blinding assessment, which itself was based on a single post-hoc guessing question of unreported power.
