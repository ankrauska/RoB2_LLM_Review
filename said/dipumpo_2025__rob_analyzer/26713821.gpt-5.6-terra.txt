## RoB 2 assessment

**Result assessed:** Effect of **assignment to conventional medical care plus nutritional counselling for caloric restriction** versus **conventional medical care alone** on **neuropsychological/cognitive performance at 12 months** in obese older adults with mild cognitive impairment.  
**Effect of interest:** Intention-to-treat effect (effect of assignment to the intervention).  
**Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group trial; 80 randomized (40 per group).

**Important scope limitation:** The article’s final conclusion emphasizes the association between individual BMI reduction and cognitive improvement after pooling both randomized groups. That pooled BMI–cognition analysis is explicitly described as *a posteriori* and is an observational association within the trial cohort, not the randomized comparison. It therefore cannot itself be assessed as a randomized intervention-effect result with RoB 2. The assessment below concerns the randomized group comparison.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information.**  
  The article calls the trial “prospective, 1:1, randomized” and states that participants underwent “random allocation,” but it does not report how the allocation sequence was generated. There is no report of computer-generated random numbers, random-number tables, random permuted blocks, minimization, or another method containing a documented random component.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  No method of allocation concealment is reported. In particular, there is no description of central randomization, an independent allocation service, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No.**  
  Table 1 shows similar group sizes at randomization (40 versus 40) and no statistically significant differences in the reported baseline characteristics, including age, sex, education, IQ, MoCA score, BMI category, comorbidity, physical activity, APOE genotype, and MCI type. The observed differences do not demonstrate a problem with randomization.

**Motivation:**  
Although the groups were apparently comparable at baseline, the report does not provide information to establish either genuinely random sequence generation or concealment of allocation before enrolment. Under RoB 2, absence of information about allocation concealment, with baseline data not suggesting a problem, leads to **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.

---

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Participants assigned to the intensive intervention attended 26–28 nutritionist-led group meetings over 12 months, whereas controls received conventional medical care alone. Participants could therefore not realistically have been unaware of their assignment.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  Nutritionists delivered the group counselling intervention and geriatricians delivered conventional care; thus, the personnel delivering care necessarily knew whether nutritional counselling was being provided.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The authors note that both groups lost weight and suggest that information provided during recruitment about obesity risks “may have increased motivation to lose weight in both groups.” However, this applies to both randomized groups and is not reported as an intervention inconsistent with the protocol or as an unbalanced, trial-context deviation between groups. The report does not describe differential co-interventions, crossovers to the intensive nutritional-counselling programme, or differential departures from the assigned care strategies that would constitute trial-context deviations likely to bias the randomized comparison.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes.**  
  The between-group longitudinal comparison was conducted using generalized estimating equations including group, time, and the group-by-time interaction. The group-by-time interaction was specified as the intervention effect. The report indicates that 75 of the 80 randomized participants completed follow-up and were analysed. Excluding participants with missing outcome data is consistent with a modified intention-to-treat analysis for this RoB 2 domain; bias from missing data is considered separately in Domain 3. There is no indication that participants were analysed according to treatment received or that adherent participants only were selected for the randomized comparison.

**Motivation:**  
The trial was necessarily open to participants and intervention personnel. Nevertheless, the article does not document differential deviations from the intended interventions arising from the trial context that were likely to affect cognitive outcomes. The randomized group comparison used a longitudinal group-by-time analysis and does not appear to be an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis. Therefore, for the effect of assignment, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

**Clarification:**  
The failure to achieve a large difference in weight loss between the groups limits the ability of the trial to test the biological effect of weight loss itself, but it does not by itself constitute RoB 2 bias in estimating the effect of being assigned to nutritional counselling versus conventional care.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably no.**  
  Five of the 80 randomized participants did not complete 12-month follow-up: four dropped out and one died from pneumonia. Thus, cognitive follow-up was available for 75/80 participants (93.75%). For continuous cognitive outcomes, RoB 2 indicates that outcome availability around 95% will often be sufficient; here, the available proportion is below that level.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The article does not report a sensitivity analysis addressing plausible values of missing cognitive outcomes, nor an analysis designed to demonstrate that missing data did not affect the group comparison.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Probably yes.**  
  Four participants are reported simply as having dropped out, without reported reasons or cognitive status at withdrawal. In a trial involving participants with MCI, dropout could be related to health status or cognitive trajectory. The available report does not establish that missing cognitive outcomes were unrelated to their true values.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Probably no.**  
  The reasons for the four dropouts are not provided, and the report does not show different dropout proportions by randomized group. One participant died from pneumonia. The available information does not demonstrate that missingness was likely related to unobserved cognitive outcomes, but it also does not rule this out.

**Motivation:**  
Outcome data were missing for 5/80 randomized participants, and no analysis was reported to establish robustness to those missing data. Because the reasons for four withdrawals are unspecified, missing cognitive outcome data could be related to cognitive status. However, the report does not provide evidence that this relationship was likely, or that missingness differed by group. This supports a judgement of **some concerns**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no.**  
  Cognitive performance was evaluated with established neuropsychological instruments, including CAMcog, IQCODE, Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test, digit span, Trail Making Tests, Modified Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, and phonemic and semantic verbal fluency tasks. The report does not identify a measurement limitation showing that these instruments were unsuitable for the cognitive domains assessed.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The cognitive battery was measured at baseline and repeated after 12 months using the same specified instruments. The report does not describe different assessment schedules, thresholds, procedures, or ascertainment opportunities between intervention groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — No.**  
  The trial registry identifies the study as **single-masked**, with the **outcomes assessor** masked. This supports blinded assessment of the neuropsychological outcome. The article itself does not provide further procedural details on maintenance of assessor blinding; this limited detail is noted as a reporting limitation.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable, given masked outcome assessors.**

**Motivation:**  
The primary outcome was assessed using standardized neuropsychological tests, with no reported difference in measurement procedures between groups. The available trial-registry information states that outcome assessors were masked. There is no evidence that the measurement method was inappropriate or that outcome assessment differed by assigned group. This domain is therefore judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  A trial registration record is available, but it lists “neuropsychological test performance” broadly and does not provide a dated, detailed statistical analysis plan specifying the selected cognitive measures, time point, analytic model, covariate strategy, handling of missing data, or hierarchy among the many cognitive tests. No separate statistical analysis plan is provided.

- **5.2 Is the reported result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  Multiple cognitive domains and multiple instruments were administered. The article reports numerous neuropsychological outcomes and refers to further cognitive results in a supplemental table, but the available material does not allow confirmation that all pre-specified eligible measures, measures at all relevant time points, and all planned cognitive-domain definitions were reported according to a finalized pre-unblinding plan. There is no direct evidence that the reported randomized result was selected based on its findings; therefore, a high-risk judgement is not justified.

- **5.3 Is the reported result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The article reports several analytic approaches: generalized estimating equations for the randomized group comparison; *a posteriori* pooled analyses based on change in BMI; generalized linear models; principal-components analysis for cognitive domains; and correlation analyses with biomarkers and dietary variables. The article explicitly states that the pooled BMI analysis was decided on *a posteriori* because both randomized groups lost similar amounts of weight. It also states that no correction for multiple comparisons was undertaken because of the exploratory nature of the analyses. However, no dated analysis plan is available to determine whether the reported cognitive models and results were selected from multiple analyses based on their findings.

**Motivation:**  
The registered primary cognitive outcome is broad, while the article contains multiple cognitive tests, cognitive-domain constructions, statistical models, subgroup interactions, and exploratory post-randomization BMI analyses. In the absence of a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan, it is not possible to verify that the reported result was not selected from among multiple measurements or analyses. The evidence does not establish result-based selection; accordingly, this is **some concerns**, not high risk of bias.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**  
The study is at low risk of bias in Domains 2 and 4, but it raises **some concerns in three domains**:

1. **Randomization process:** neither sequence generation nor allocation concealment is described.  
2. **Missing outcome data:** cognitive outcomes were unavailable for 5/80 randomized participants, including four withdrawals with unreported reasons; no sensitivity analysis addressed potential impact.  
3. **Selection of the reported result:** no detailed pre-specified, date-stamped analysis plan is available despite multiple cognitive measures and analytic approaches, including explicitly *a posteriori* pooled BMI-based analyses.

Under the specified RoB 2 overall-judgement rules, the accumulation of concerns in multiple domains substantially lowers confidence in the randomized cognitive-effect result. The overall judgement is therefore **high risk of bias**.

**Interpretive limitation:** The randomized group comparison found no significant group-by-time interaction for most cognitive tests. The article’s conclusion that intentional weight loss improved cognition rests principally on pooled, post hoc associations between BMI change and cognitive change, rather than on a demonstrated randomized effect of nutritional counselling. That pooled association is not protected by randomization and should not be interpreted as the causal randomized effect of the intervention.
