## RoB 2 assessment

**Study:** Ashman et al., 2009, *A Randomized Controlled Trial of Sertraline for the Treatment of Depression in Persons With Traumatic Brain Injury* (NCT00233103).  
**Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial.  
**Experimental intervention:** Sertraline, titrated from 25 mg/day up to 200 mg/day.  
**Comparator:** Matching placebo.  
**Result assessed:** Effect of assignment to sertraline versus placebo on **clinician-rated depression severity (17-item HAM-D) at the end of the reported 10-week treatment period**, including the reported repeated-measures group comparison (HAM-D group effect: \(F_{1,222.93}=0.481\), not significant).  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect).  
**Sources assessed:** Published article and supplied ClinicalTrials.gov record/protocol information (NCT00233103).

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states that “random assignment to treatment conditions (sertraline or placebo) was balanced by using a block design (blocks of 8) with computer-generated random number sequences.” Computer-generated random-number sequences constitute a random allocation sequence.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — Yes.**  
  The article states that allocation sequences were held in “individual presealed envelopes,” and, more importantly, that eligible, medically cleared participants “were randomized into the medication or placebo arm of the study by the pharmacy.” The pharmacy implemented random assignment and dispensed identically marked medication. This indicates that allocation was administered by the pharmacy rather than by recruiting or assessing study staff.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — No information.**  
  Baseline characteristics are reported only for the 41 participants who completed the clinical trial (sertraline 22; placebo 19), whereas 52 participants were randomized (27 sertraline; 25 placebo, according to the registry record). Therefore, the baseline table cannot establish comparability among all randomized participants.  
  Among completers, there are numerical differences in some variables, including injury severity, sex distribution, ethnicity, and income. However, the report does not provide evidence that these differences were implausible under chance or that they demonstrate manipulation or failure of randomization. The article states that no statistically significant differences were found between groups on demographic, injury, and baseline clinical measures, although the abstract separately refers to a significant income difference. The available information does not demonstrate a problem with randomization.

**Motivation:**  
The random sequence was explicitly computer-generated using blocked randomization. Allocation was administered by a pharmacy, and both staff and participants were stated to be blinded. Although baseline information is restricted to completers rather than all randomized participants, no reported imbalance establishes a randomization failure. Under RoB 2, adequate allocation concealment, a random sequence, and no demonstrated baseline problem support a **low risk of bias** judgement.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  The trial is described as double-blind. The pharmacy dispensed sertraline and placebo tablets with identical markings, and the number of tablets was increased identically in both groups as dose adjustments occurred.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — No.**  
  The authors state that “both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received.” The pharmacy, rather than the treating study physicians or research staff, performed randomization and masking. Medication was delivered in identical-appearing form.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Not applicable.**  
  Since participants and study personnel were blinded, RoB 2 does not require this question for the effect of assignment to intervention.

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Yes.**  
  The investigators state that they conducted an “intent-to-treat analysis” including participants who received minimal treatment. The repeated-measures random-effects models were used because they could accommodate differing numbers of observations; the report states that some participants were missing one of the six assessments. The article also reports a last-observation-carried-forward procedure for an analysis involving participants with 8- versus 10-week observations.  
  The handling of missing data creates concern under Domain 3, but it does not indicate that participants were analysed according to treatment received rather than randomized assignment, nor that eligible participants with outcome data were excluded due to non-adherence or switching.

**Motivation:**  
The interventions were matched and administered under conditions designed to preserve blinding for participants, physicians, and research staff. No trial-context-related deviations from the protocol are reported. The reported analyses retained participants in their randomized groups; no as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis was used as the primary basis for the assessed result. Accordingly, for the assignment effect, this domain is at **low risk of bias**. Bias associated with attrition and use of last-observation-carried-forward is considered separately under missing outcome data.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — No.**  
  Fifty-two participants were randomized: 27 to sertraline and 25 to placebo. Only 41 completed the clinical trial: 22 in the sertraline group and 19 in the placebo group. Thus, outcome data at the end of treatment were unavailable for 11/52 randomized participants (21.2%): 5/27 (18.5%) in the sertraline group and 6/25 (24.0%) in the placebo group.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — No.**  
  The study reports last-observation-carried-forward for a subset of participants and random-effects analyses that can use incomplete repeated data. However, the report does not provide sensitivity analyses showing that the treatment effect was robust to plausible values of the missing end-of-treatment HAM-D scores. Last-observation-carried-forward, by itself, does not demonstrate absence of bias from missing outcome data.

- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Yes.**  
  This is plausible from the reported reasons for non-completion. Three participants were removed for clinical reasons: increased anxiety after baseline, increased depression after a family death, and an allergic reaction. The remaining eight did not complete because of protocol non-compliance, including missed follow-up appointments, failure to collect medication, or lack of response to follow-up contacts. In a depression-treatment trial, clinical worsening and disengagement may be related to depression severity.

- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Yes.**  
  The authors specifically report that participants who dropped out tended to have higher HAM-D scores at their last assessment than participants who remained in treatment (\(F=4.0\), \(P=.052\)). This finding, combined with clinical removal due to increased depression or anxiety, indicates that attrition was related to depressive symptom severity. The high proportion of missing endpoint data makes it possible that missing outcomes materially affected the estimated group effect.

**Motivation:**  
More than one fifth of randomized participants did not complete the trial, and the reported end-of-treatment table is based on only 41 completers. Missingness was not random on the available evidence: dropouts had higher depression scores at their last assessment, and some withdrawals were explicitly related to clinical deterioration. The study does not report a sensitivity analysis demonstrating that the results are robust to plausible missing-data assumptions. Under RoB 2, this constitutes **high risk of bias due to missing outcome data**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  The primary outcome was the 17-item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-D), described as a widely used clinician-rated measure of depression severity. The article reports its established reliability and validity. No information indicates that the HAM-D was unsuitable for assessing depressive symptom severity in this trial.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — No.**  
  Both groups attended the same scheduled biweekly physician and assessment visits. Depression severity was assessed on the same schedule and with the same instrument in both trial groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — No.**  
  The article states that “both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received.” HAM-D ratings were conducted independently from the study physician by a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist. The supplied registry also identifies masking of participants and investigators. There is no report that assessors were unblinded.

**Motivation:**  
The HAM-D is an appropriate clinician-rated depression scale; assessment procedures were the same in both randomized groups; and outcome assessors were reported to be blinded to treatment assignment. Therefore, there is no identified differential measurement error likely to bias the HAM-D comparison. This domain is at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  No dated statistical analysis plan is supplied. The trial registry was first posted in October 2005, after the recorded study start date of June 2003. It identifies depression outcomes at baseline and at end of treatment but does not provide a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan specifying the primary analysis model, analysis population, missing-data method, exact assessment time point, or selection between endpoint and longitudinal analyses. The registry results were first submitted in 2013 and posted in 2015, after publication of the article.

- **5.2 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — No information.**  
  Depression was assessed repeatedly at baseline and biweekly through the treatment period, and the paper additionally reports several HAM-D subscales. The article reports the total HAM-D and several subscale analyses, but no prospectively finalized document specifies which time point or which HAM-D-based result was the definitive analysis for the reported conclusion. There is no direct evidence that the reported total-HAM-D result was selected because of its magnitude or statistical significance; however, the available materials cannot exclude selection among multiple eligible time points or related measures.

- **5.3 Is the numerical result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The article reports several analysis approaches: analyses of covariance, repeated-measures analysis of variance, random-effects models, an intent-to-treat analysis, and a last-observation-carried-forward procedure. It also reports analyses of the HAM-D total score and multiple subscales. Without a prospectively finalized detailed analysis plan, it cannot be verified that the reported analysis was selected independently of the observed results. There is no explicit evidence of selective reporting sufficient to judge this domain at high risk.

**Motivation:**  
The report contains multiple measurements over time and multiple possible analytic approaches. The accessible registry entry does not provide a prospectively finalized, sufficiently detailed analysis plan, and it was registered after the recorded study start date. These facts prevent confirmation that the specific reported HAM-D group comparison was selected without knowledge of unblinded results. However, there is no documented discrepancy or direct evidence that a favourable result was selected from multiple analyses or measurements. Therefore, the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is **some concerns**.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation:**  
The study is at low risk of bias for the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and outcome measurement. It raises some concerns regarding selection of the reported result because a prospectively finalized detailed analysis plan is unavailable and multiple measurement and analytic options existed.

However, the study is at **high risk of bias due to missing outcome data**. End-of-treatment data were missing for 11 of 52 randomized participants (21%), dropout was associated with higher observed depression severity, and some non-completion was explicitly related to worsening depression or anxiety. No adequate sensitivity analysis establishes that the results were robust to the missing outcome data.  

Under the specified RoB 2 overall rules, **high risk of bias in at least one domain requires an overall judgement of High risk of bias** for the assessed HAM-D depression result at the end of treatment.
