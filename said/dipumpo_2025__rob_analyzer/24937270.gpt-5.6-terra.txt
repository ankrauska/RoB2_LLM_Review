## Scope of this RoB 2 assessment

- **Design:** Individually randomized, parallel-group, single-centre sham-controlled trial.  
- **Result assessed:** Change from pre- to post-stimulation in **theta-band interhemispheric EEG coherence**, for **random-frequency tPCS (1–5 Hz) versus sham**. The reported comparison was: mean difference 0.089 coherence points, 95% CI 0.010 to 0.1693; *p* = 0.0284.  
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to random-frequency tPCS versus sham (intention-to-treat effect).  
- **Sources available for assessment:** The article and its reported table/figure only. No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan (SAP), prospective trial-registry record, or other external trial documentation was provided.  

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — No information**  
  The article states: “*Randomization was completed using blocks of four.*” However, it does not state that the order of assignments within blocks was generated using a random method (e.g., computer-generated random numbers or random permuted blocks). Block allocation alone does not establish that a random component was used.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information**  
  No method of allocation concealment is reported. The article does not describe central randomization, an independent randomization service, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. It is therefore not possible to establish whether those enrolling participants could foresee assignments.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no**  
  The authors report no statistically significant demographic differences between groups. The baseline table does not show an obvious imbalance in sex, age, MMSE score, or baseline behavioural task performance that clearly exceeds what might occur by chance in this small trial. The unequal analysed group sizes are explicable from the reported removal of one participant in the random-frequency group because of excessive EEG artefacts, rather than providing evidence by themselves of a defective randomization process.

**Motivation**

The study is described as randomized and block allocation was used, but the reporting does not establish either genuinely random sequence generation or adequate concealment of allocation. Under RoB 2, absent information on allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement. Since the reported baseline characteristics do not themselves indicate a definite failure of randomization, the appropriate judgement is **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

---

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Probably no**  
  The study is described as “double-blinded,” and the authors specifically state that subjects were blinded to the intervention. The sham procedure involved stimulation for 30 seconds followed by automatic cessation. In the blinding assessment, participants did not identify their assigned group better than expected by chance (*p* = 0.679). These findings support successful participant blinding.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — No information**  
  The paper states that “*the investigator assessing the outcomes and the subjects were blinded*.” It does not state whether the person administering or setting up the tPCS device was blinded, nor whether the device was programmed by an independent person. Thus, blinding of personnel who delivered the intervention is not established.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — No information**  
  No information is reported on deviations from the assigned stimulation condition, implementation errors, additional interventions, or differences in care or study procedures arising from knowledge of allocation.

- **2.4 Were such deviations likely to have affected the outcome? — Not applicable**  
- **2.5 Were deviations balanced between groups? — Not applicable**

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably yes**  
  The analysis compared post-minus-pre changes between the randomized groups. The report does not indicate an as-treated analysis or a naïve per-protocol analysis. One participant allocated to random-frequency stimulation was removed because of “excessive artifacts in the EEG recording”; this is missing outcome data rather than evidence that participants were analysed according to a different intervention from that assigned. The consequence of this exclusion is addressed in Domain 3.

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact from failure to analyse participants in their randomized groups? — Not applicable**

**Motivation**

Participant blinding is supported by the design and by the empirical blinding check. However, the report does not establish whether intervention-delivery personnel were blinded, and it does not report whether deviations from intended stimulation or trial-context-related co-interventions occurred. These omissions leave uncertainty about whether unblinded delivery could have affected implementation of the experimental or sham conditions. The reported analysis otherwise appears to preserve randomized group assignment. Therefore, this domain has **some concerns**, not high risk of bias.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably yes**  
  Thirty-eight participants completed the stimulation session and were assigned across the four groups. One participant in the random-frequency group was removed from EEG analysis because of excessive EEG artefacts. Thus, theta-coherence data were analysed for 37 of 38 participants (approximately 97%). For a continuous EEG coherence outcome, this amount of missing data is sufficiently small that it is unlikely, on the information reported, to have materially changed the estimated effect.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — Not applicable**  
- **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value? — Not applicable**  
- **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value? — Not applicable**

**Motivation**

Only one participant’s EEG outcome was unavailable for analysis, owing to excessive recording artefacts. This corresponds to approximately 3% of randomized participants. The article does not report differential losses to follow-up, multiple exclusions, or outcome data missing for clinical or outcome-related reasons. Although the exclusion occurred in the random-frequency group, the very small amount of missing data means that the missing outcome is unlikely to have made an important difference to the theta-coherence comparison. The domain is therefore judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — Probably no**  
  Theta interhemispheric coherence was measured using a 64-channel EEG device, with specified recording conditions, sampling frequency, filtering, and a described coherence-estimation method (magnitude-squared coherence using Welch’s averaged modified periodogram method). The report does not provide evidence that the EEG method was unsuitable for measuring the stated EEG coherence outcome.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no**  
  EEG was recorded before and after the experimental condition in all groups, using the same equipment and procedures. The article does not report different outcome-assessment schedules, thresholds, recording methods, or opportunities for EEG outcome ascertainment across groups.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants? — No**  
  The authors state: “*Both the investigator assessing the outcomes and the subjects were blinded to the intervention.*” This directly indicates blinding of the outcome assessor.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable**  
- **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Not applicable**

**Motivation**

The targeted outcome is an instrument-derived qEEG coherence measure, obtained using the same recording approach across study groups. Most importantly, the investigator assessing outcomes was reported to be blinded. There is no report of differential measurement procedures between groups. Therefore, there is no identified material risk that knowledge of allocation biased measurement of theta coherence. This domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information**  
  No protocol, registry entry, or dated SAP is available in the supplied material. The article states that post-hoc comparisons between random-frequency stimulation and sham, 1 Hz and sham, and 100 Hz and sham were made “*according to a-priori specifications*.” However, this statement within the published article is not a prospectively finalized, externally verifiable analysis plan.

- **5.2 Is the theta-coherence result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements? — No information**  
  The study measured numerous potentially eligible EEG outcomes: theta, alpha, and beta power; alpha and theta coherence; four coherence sub-bands; two eyes conditions; and coherence at two electrode-pair locations. The article reports several group and post-hoc results, but without a prospectively available protocol or analysis plan it cannot be established whether the reported theta-coherence comparison was selected from multiple outcome measurements on the basis of its result.

- **5.3 Is the theta-coherence result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information**  
  The article used mixed-model ANOVA, interaction tests, group-effect tests, and multiple post-hoc comparisons. It also describes some analyses as “exploratory.” No prospective SAP is available to determine whether other eligible analytic choices—such as alternative handling of eye condition, electrode pairs, EEG bands/sub-bands, or post-hoc contrasts—were performed and whether reporting was influenced by the observed findings.

**Motivation**

There is no direct evidence that the theta-coherence result was selectively reported, so a high-risk judgement is not justified. However, the targeted result comes from a study with multiple stimulation arms, multiple EEG bands and sub-bands, two recording conditions, multiple electrode-pair measurements, and several post-hoc comparisons. In the absence of a protocol, registry record, or dated SAP that specifies the outcome and analysis in advance, selective choice of the reported result cannot be excluded. This domain is therefore judged as having **some concerns**.

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**

**Motivation**

The result is at:

- **Some concerns** for bias arising from the randomization process, because neither random sequence generation nor allocation concealment was adequately described;
- **Some concerns** for deviations from intended interventions, because blinding of intervention-delivery personnel and the presence or absence of relevant deviations were not reported;
- **Low risk** for missing outcome data;
- **Low risk** for measurement of the outcome; and
- **Some concerns** for selection of the reported result, because no prospectively finalized protocol or SAP was available despite multiple EEG outcomes and analyses.

Under RoB 2, some concerns in multiple domains may warrant an overall **high risk of bias** judgement when they substantially lower confidence in the result. Here, the combination of insufficiently documented randomization/concealment, incomplete information on intervention delivery and deviations, and inability to verify that the reported theta-coherence finding was selected according to a pre-specified analysis plan substantially lowers confidence in the reported random-frequency tPCS versus sham theta-coherence result.
