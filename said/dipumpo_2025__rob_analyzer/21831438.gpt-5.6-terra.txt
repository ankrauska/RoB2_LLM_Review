## RoB 2 assessment

**Result assessed:** Mean change in sitting diastolic blood pressure (DBP) from baseline to week 4, comparing **candesartan 16 mg/hydrochlorothiazide (HCT) 12.5 mg** with **candesartan 16 mg monotherapy**. This was the registered primary outcome.

**Effect of interest:** Effect of **assignment** to the randomized interventions (intention-to-treat effect).

**Study design:** Individually randomized, two-arm, parallel-group, multicentre, open-label Phase IV trial.

**Sources assessed:** Published article and ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT00621153).

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random? — Yes.**  
  The article states: “Study patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio by using the mixed block randomization (4 and 6 blocks) method.” Randomly mixed block sizes are a random allocation method.

- **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions? — No information.**  
  Neither the article nor the registry describes who generated the allocation sequence, how allocation was implemented, whether it was centrally administered, or whether sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes or another adequate concealment mechanism was used. Random block allocation alone does not establish allocation concealment.

- **1.3 Did baseline differences suggest a problem with the randomization process? — Probably no.**  
  Baseline arm sizes were close to 1:1 (117 versus 116). Baseline SBP and DBP were similar. The combination group was older on average than the monotherapy group (51.0 versus 47.8 years; *P* = 0.02). This is a single statistically significant difference among the reported baseline characteristics and does not, by itself, demonstrate an imbalance beyond that expected by chance or a failure of randomization.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The reported mixed-block method supports random sequence generation, and the observed baseline information does not demonstrate a problematic randomization process. However, concealment of the allocation sequence was not reported. Under RoB 2, absence of information on allocation concealment, despite apparently chance-compatible baseline characteristics, leads to **some concerns**.

---

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  The registry explicitly describes the study as having no masking, and the article refers to the “open study design.” Participants could therefore distinguish combination therapy from monotherapy.

- **2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial? — Yes.**  
  This was an open-label trial. Investigators and treating personnel were aware of treatment assignment.

- **2.3 Were there deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context? — Probably no.**  
  The article does not report trial-context-induced co-interventions, systematic switching between randomized regimens, or differential implementation of the assigned treatments. Both groups underwent the protocol-defined forced titration from candesartan 16 mg to 32 mg at week 4; this occurred after the primary week-4 outcome and was part of the specified treatment strategy. Median reported compliance was high in both groups (98.6% combination; 97.8% monotherapy).

- **2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention? — Probably no.**  
  The analysis was labelled “ITT,” but its definition was not a full ITT analysis: participants had to have baseline BP, at least one post-randomization BP value, and receipt of at least one study dose. Of 233 randomized participants, the primary ITT analysis included 224 (111 versus 113); thus, nine randomized participants were excluded from the primary analysis.

- **2.7 Was there potential for a substantial impact of failure to analyse participants in their randomized groups? — Probably no.**  
  The exclusions represented 9/233 randomized participants (3.9%). The primary outcome was continuous DBP, and the available report does not show that exclusions were substantial enough, or associated with prognostic factors strongly enough, to be likely to have a substantial effect on the estimated week-4 treatment difference. Nevertheless, reasons for all exclusions from the primary analysis are not fully provided by randomized group.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
Lack of blinding does not itself establish bias for the effect of assignment. No evidence was reported that open-label status led to differential, trial-context-related deviations that affected week-4 DBP. However, the so-called ITT analysis excluded nine randomized participants and therefore did not fully estimate the effect of assignment using all randomized patients. The small proportion excluded makes a substantial impact unlikely, but this analytical departure raises **some concerns**.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized? — Probably yes.**  
  Week-4 DBP was analysed for 224 of 233 randomized participants (96.1%): 111/117 in the combination group and 113/116 in the monotherapy group. For a continuous outcome, data availability of approximately 95% is generally sufficient for a low-risk judgement when the missing observations are unlikely to materially affect the treatment estimate.

- **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data? — Not applicable.**  
  The data were available for nearly all randomized participants.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary analysis included 96.1% of randomized participants, exceeding the level that RoB 2 guidance indicates will often be sufficient for continuous outcomes. Although reasons for every missing week-4 DBP measurement were not fully reported by treatment arm, the amount of missing outcome data was small. On the information available, the missing data are not sufficient to create a material risk of bias in the primary DBP result.

**Reporting limitation noted:**  
The registry reports 233 randomized and 198 completed participants, while the article reports 224 participants in the ITT analysis and 211 in the PP analysis. These figures cannot be fully reconciled from the available sources. However, the primary result itself is explicitly based on 224/233 randomized participants.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome  
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Signalling questions**

- **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate? — No.**  
  Sitting SBP and DBP were measured using a fully automated Omron device. Patients rested for at least five minutes; three readings were obtained at one-minute intervals; additional measurements were taken when DBP values differed by more than 10 mm Hg; and the mean of the remaining three measurements was used after discarding extreme values when five readings were taken.

- **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups? — Probably no.**  
  The same specified BP measurement procedure was used in both groups, at the same scheduled visits, before dosing, and at approximately the same time of day. The arm with the higher baseline DBP was selected for repeated measurements, and the same arm was used subsequently.

- **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received? — Yes.**  
  The trial was open-label, and the investigators performing the BP readings were not reported to be blinded.

- **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received? — Probably no.**  
  Although investigators were aware of assignments, the primary outcome was an automated numerical BP measurement conducted according to a prespecified, standardized procedure. It involved substantially less assessor judgement than a subjective clinical rating or patient-reported outcome.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary outcome was measured objectively with an automated device and a detailed, standardized procedure that was applied comparably to both arms. Open-label outcome assessment is documented, but knowledge of assignment was unlikely to materially influence automated sitting DBP measurements. Therefore, this domain is judged **low risk of bias**.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result  
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Signalling questions**

- **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available? — No information.**  
  The ClinicalTrials.gov record was posted before study completion and specifies the primary outcome as change in mean sitting DBP from baseline after four weeks. However, no dated statistical analysis plan was available. The registry does not fully specify the analysis population, handling of missing data, or the ANCOVA model and covariate adjustment reported in the article. Thus, it cannot be confirmed that the reported analysis was finalized before unblinded data were available.

- **5.2 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain? — Probably no.**  
  The week-4 sitting DBP change was explicitly pre-specified as the primary outcome in the registry and in the published report. The result reported in the paper corresponds to that pre-specified primary outcome.

- **5.3 Is the result likely to have been selected from multiple eligible analyses of the data? — No information.**  
  The article reports both ITT and PP analyses and uses ANCOVA adjusted for baseline BP. Without a pre-specified detailed analysis plan, it cannot be determined whether the reported primary analysis was selected from multiple possible eligible analyses on the basis of results. There is no direct evidence that such selective reporting occurred.

**Motivation for judgement:**  
The primary outcome and four-week time point were prospectively registered, which reduces concern about selection among outcome measurements. Nevertheless, no dated protocol or statistical analysis plan specifying the final analysis population and analysis model was available. The report presents both ITT and PP analyses, and the available sources do not establish that the reported numerical result was analysed strictly according to a finalized, pre-unblinded analysis plan. This produces **some concerns**, rather than high risk, because selective reporting is not demonstrated.

---

### 6. Overall risk-of-bias judgement  
**Overall judgement: Some concerns**

**Motivation:**  
The result is at low risk of bias for missing outcome data and outcome measurement. However, it raises **some concerns** in three domains:

1. **Randomization process:** allocation concealment was not reported.  
2. **Deviations from intended interventions:** the primary “ITT” analysis excluded nine randomized participants and was therefore not a full analysis of all randomized participants.  
3. **Selection of the reported result:** the primary outcome was prospectively specified, but no sufficiently detailed, time-stamped statistical analysis plan was available to verify that the reported analysis was pre-specified before unblinded outcome data were available.

No domain is judged at high risk of bias. Although there are concerns in multiple domains, the available evidence does not establish that these concerns together substantially lower confidence in the week-4 DBP treatment comparison to the extent required for a high overall RoB 2 judgement.
