## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
The article states that participants were assigned using “a randomization automated system on a one-to-one basis,” which is an adequate method for generating a random allocation sequence.

**1.2: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
The randomization list “was held securely, and released only after study completion.” In addition, gabapentin and placebo were dispensed in identical-appearing capsules. These procedures indicate that allocation was concealed until enrolment and assignment.

**1.3: N (Moderate Certainty, 87%)**  
The article reports no significant baseline differences between groups in mean age, methadone dosage, or doses of symptomatic medications. The baseline SOWS scores were also reasonably similar (gabapentin 12.6 versus placebo 14.9), with no reported statistically significant or clinically substantial imbalance suggesting a problem with randomization.

**Result of Domain 1: Low risk (High Certainty, 96%)**  
The allocation sequence was adequately generated and concealed, and reported baseline data do not suggest a problem with the randomization process.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
The study was described as double-blind, and participants received either gabapentin or identical-appearing placebo capsules. Therefore, participants were unaware of their assigned intervention.

**2.2: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
The trial was double-blind, and active treatment and placebo were provided in identical-appearing capsules. There is no indication that those delivering the interventions knew participant allocation.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
Symptomatic medications (clonidine, oxazepam, or trazodone) could be used if required. The article reports no significant differences between groups in doses of these medications, indicating that these important concomitant interventions were balanced between groups.

**2.4: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Three of 20 placebo-group participants (15%) were excluded because of non-compliance with the treatment programme and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter drugs. This exceeds 10% of participants in that group and represents failures in implementing the intended intervention.

**2.5: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
More than 10% of placebo participants (3/20; 15%) did not adhere to the assigned treatment regimen, as they were excluded for non-compliance and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter drugs.

**2.6: NI (Moderate Certainty, 78%)**  
The article reports repeated-measures analysis of variance and Mann–Whitney U-tests, but does not report an appropriate causal analysis estimating the effect of adhering to the assigned intervention, such as inverse probability weighting or instrumental-variable analysis. It also does not explicitly describe an intention-to-treat, per-protocol, or as-treated analysis for this purpose.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 95%)**  
There was greater than 10% non-adherence and implementation failure in the placebo group, and no appropriate analysis was reported to estimate the effect of adherence to the assigned intervention.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: PN (High Certainty, 98%)**  
SOWS data at the final reported assessment (day 18) were available for 37 of 40 randomized participants: 20/20 in the gabapentin group and 17/20 in the placebo group. This is 92.5% complete outcome data, which is between 90% and 95% for a continuous outcome.

**3.2: N (High Certainty, 97%)**  
No inverse probability weighting, sensitivity analysis, or other appropriate method to assess or correct potential bias from missing SOWS data was reported. The repeated-measures analysis does not itself demonstrate that missing-data bias was absent.

**3.3: Y (High Certainty, 94%)**  
The missing placebo-group data arose from non-compliance and self-medication. The authors state that this non-compliance and self-medication “may be due to the greater severity of withdrawal symptoms.” Therefore, missingness could depend on the true severity of withdrawal symptoms.

**3.4: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
All three exclusions occurred in the placebo group, creating an imbalance in missing data between groups. The stated possible reason for missingness—greater withdrawal severity—directly relates to the SOWS outcome. Thus, it is likely that missingness depended on the true outcome value.

**Result of Domain 3: High risk (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Outcome data were missing for 7.5% of randomized participants, exclusively in the placebo group, and the reported reasons indicate that missingness may have been related to withdrawal-symptom severity. No appropriate missing-data-bias analysis was reported.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 99%)**  
Withdrawal symptoms were measured using the Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale, a 16-item patient-rated scale with symptom intensity rated from 0 to 4. The article states that the SOWS has been shown to be a valid and reliable indicator of opiate withdrawal severity.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
The SOWS was administered at the same six scheduled time points for both gabapentin and placebo groups. There is no evidence that measurement methods, thresholds, or assessment timing differed between groups.

**4.3: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
This was a patient-reported outcome, and participants were blinded through the use of identical-appearing placebo capsules in a double-blind trial. Therefore, participants acting as outcome assessors were not aware of intervention assignment.

**4.4: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
The SOWS is a subjective, patient-reported measurement of withdrawal symptoms. Subjective symptom ratings could be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received if blinding were broken.

**4.5: N (Moderate Certainty, 86%)**  
There is no clear evidence that knowledge of allocation influenced SOWS assessment. The trial used double-blinding and identical-appearing placebo capsules, reducing the likelihood that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced patient-reported symptom ratings.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (High Certainty, 94%)**  
The SOWS was a valid and reliable measure, was assessed comparably in both groups, and participants were blinded to intervention assignment.
