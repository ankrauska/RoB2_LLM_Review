## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: NI (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
The article reports “sex-stratified randomization” and states that participants were assigned by “simple random selection,” but it does not describe a specific sequence-generation method such as computer-generated random numbers, a random-number table, coin tossing, or similar method.

**1.2: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
The allocation sequence was reported to be blinded through the use of sealed envelopes. Under the specified assessment instructions, reporting use of envelopes supports a judgment of adequate allocation concealment.

**1.3: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
The two groups had equal sample sizes (27 participants per group). Baseline VAS values did not significantly differ between groups (p = 0.421), and the authors reported no significant baseline differences for age, sex, or the pre-treatment dependent variables.

**Result of Domain 1: Low risk (High Certainty, 93%)**  
Allocation concealment was reported through sealed envelopes, and baseline data did not suggest a problem with randomization. Although the exact sequence-generation method was not described, this does not alter the domain judgment according to the specified algorithm.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
The study states that patients were blinded. The control group received placebo (sham) ultrasound, with the ultrasound device switched off, indicating that participants were intended to be unaware of whether they received active or placebo ultrasound.

**2.2: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
The article states that blinding of the physiotherapist who applied the treatment was not possible. Therefore, the intervention provider was aware of the assigned intervention.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
Both groups received the same treatment protocol, including microwave therapy, massage, and exercises. The only difference was active versus placebo ultrasound during the first 10 sessions. No important non-protocol interventions imbalanced between groups were reported.

**2.4: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
The article explicitly states that no patient was lost or dropped out during the study. Therefore, intervention implementation was successful for all participants.

**2.5: NI (Moderate Certainty, 70%)**  
The article does not report adherence, compliance, treatment discontinuation, crossover, or switching between the active and placebo ultrasound interventions.

**2.6: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
The study reports that analyses were conducted using intention-to-treat analysis. According to the specified criteria, intention-to-treat analysis is not an appropriate method for estimating the effect of adhering to the assigned intervention regimen.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 92%)**  
The treating physiotherapist was aware of treatment assignment, adherence information was not reported, and the analysis was intention-to-treat rather than an appropriate adherence-effect analysis. According to the specified algorithm, this results in high risk of bias.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Pain was measured using the visual analogue scale (VAS), a standard and reliable patient-reported measure of pain intensity. The VAS measurement method was clearly described.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
VAS was assessed at the same planned time points in both groups: before the first session, after the sixth session, after the eleventh session, and at discharge after the twenty-fifth session. The same measurement approach was used for both groups.

**4.3: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
VAS is patient-reported, and the study reports blinding of patients, with placebo ultrasound used in the control group. Therefore, participants reporting pain were not aware of their intervention assignment.

**4.4: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
VAS pain is a subjective patient-reported outcome. Subjective pain ratings could be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**4.5: N (Moderate Certainty, 85%)**  
Although VAS is subjective, patients were blinded through use of sham ultrasound. There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention status was likely to influence VAS assessment.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (High Certainty, 93%)**  
The VAS is an appropriate measure, assessment procedures were comparable between groups, and patients reporting VAS pain were blinded to intervention assignment.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1: NI (High Certainty, 95%)**  
No trial registration number, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or pre-specified analysis plan finalized before outcome-data analysis was reported. Therefore, it cannot be verified that the VAS analysis followed a pre-specified plan.

**5.2: Y (High Certainty, 92%)**  
The Methods section specifies VAS assessments at four time points: before the first session, after the sixth session, after the eleventh session, and at discharge after the twenty-fifth session. However, the Results tables report VAS results at baseline, after 10 sessions, and at discharge, without reporting the VAS result after the sixth session. Thus, multiple eligible VAS time-point measurements were collected, but not all were fully reported.

**5.3: N (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
The statistical methods state that Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test would be selected as appropriate according to data distribution. The reported VAS analyses used these stated methods. There is no clear evidence that multiple analyses were conducted and selectively reported for the VAS outcome.

**Result of Domain 5: High risk (High Certainty, 92%)**  
The study collected VAS data at multiple time points, but the VAS measurement after the sixth treatment session was not fully reported in the Results. This indicates selective reporting from multiple eligible outcome measurements.
