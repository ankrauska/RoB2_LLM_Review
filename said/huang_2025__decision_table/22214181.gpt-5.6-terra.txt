**Outcome Name**: List of attributions task (LAT)  
**registration number**: Not reported  

## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
A Microsoft Excel random-number generator was explicitly used to randomize participants to the fatigue-information and control conditions.

**1.2: NI (High Certainty, 98%)**  
The article does not report allocation concealment procedures, such as central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, or equivalent safeguards. Blinding of outcome coding does not establish concealment of allocation before enrolment/assignment.

**1.3: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
The FI and control groups had equal sample sizes (44 per group) and did not significantly differ at baseline in age, ethnicity, sex distribution, insomnia, fatigue, depression, dysfunctional beliefs about sleep, or LAT outcomes.

**Result of the domain: Some concerns (High Certainty, 97%)**  
Although random-sequence generation was adequate and baseline groups were comparable, allocation concealment was not reported.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Participants received either fatigue-cause information or generic sleep information. They were therefore aware of the information/intervention they received.

**2.2: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
The study investigator read the intervention information sheet alongside each participant individually and was aware of the intervention being delivered.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
No important non-protocol interventions that were prognostic for LAT outcomes, and no imbalance in such interventions between groups, were reported. Both groups completed the same study session and received comparable amounts of information.

**2.4: N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
Five participants were excluded from analysis, including two who did not complete outcome measures in a scorable manner. This represents fewer than 10% of the apparent initial sample (5/93, approximately 5.4%); therefore, there was not evidence that more than 10% experienced implementation failures.

**2.5: NI (High Certainty, 96%)**  
The article does not report adherence, compliance, refusal to engage with the information session, crossover, or discontinuation of the assigned intervention.

**2.6: NI (High Certainty, 94%)**  
No analysis intended to estimate the effect of adhering to the assigned intervention was reported. The reported mixed ANOVAs assessed groups as analysed and were not described as instrumental-variable, inverse-probability-weighted, or other appropriate adherence-effect analyses.

**Result of the domain: High risk (High Certainty, 92%)**  
Participants and the intervention provider were aware of intervention assignment. In addition, adherence was not reported and no appropriate analysis was used to address possible deviations or non-adherence.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: PN (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
The final analysed sample was 88 participants. Five participants were excluded, indicating an apparent initial sample of 93 participants. Thus, outcome data were available for approximately 94.6% of participants (88/93), which is between 90% and 95% for this continuous outcome.

**3.2: N (High Certainty, 97%)**  
The article did not report inverse-probability weighting, sensitivity analyses, or another method demonstrating that missing LAT data did not bias the results. The use of ANOVA does not itself correct for missing-data bias.

**3.3: N (Moderate Certainty, 76%)**  
The reported reasons for exclusions were age younger than 18 years, missing demographic information, and inability to calculate a total score from outcome responses. These reasons do not indicate that missingness depended on the participants’ true LAT outcome values.

**3.4: NI (Moderate Certainty, 70%)**  
The article does not report missing-data patterns by intervention group or provide sufficient detail regarding the characteristics or LAT values of participants with missing or unusable outcome data.

**Result of the domain: Low risk (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
LAT data were available for approximately 94.6% of the apparent enrolled sample, meeting the criterion for a “PN” response to Question 3.1; under the specified algorithm, this leads to low risk.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (Moderate Certainty, 78%)**  
The LAT was a predefined task developed for this study. It directly measured participants’ listed fatigue attributions, their frequency rankings, and their perceived importance. The written responses were coded using prespecified sleep-related and non-sleep-related categories. No evidence indicates that the measurement method was incapable of measuring the intended outcome.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Both groups completed the same LAT at the same pre-intervention and post-intervention time points, using the same instructions, scoring procedures, and coding approach.

**4.3: Y (High Certainty, 97%)**  
LAT outcomes were based on participants’ self-reported attributions, rankings, and likelihood ratings. Participants were aware of the information condition they received; therefore, outcome assessment through self-report occurred with knowledge of assigned intervention. Although coding personnel were blinded to hypotheses or allocation, the underlying LAT responses were participant-reported.

**4.4: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
The LAT is a subjective, participant-reported outcome. Participants listed and rated their own perceived causes of fatigue, so their responses could be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**4.5: Y (Moderate Certainty, 85%)**  
The intervention specifically provided participants with information about possible causes of fatigue, and the LAT was completed immediately afterward. The authors also acknowledged that demand characteristics or priming may have influenced FI participants’ post-intervention LAT responses. Therefore, it is likely that knowledge of intervention content influenced outcome assessment.

**Result of the domain: High risk (High Certainty, 91%)**  
The subjective self-reported LAT was measured immediately after an unblinded information intervention designed to alter precisely the attributions assessed. Participants’ knowledge of their intervention was likely to influence their outcome responses.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1: NI (High Certainty, 98%)**  
No trial registration number, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prespecified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available was reported. Therefore, it cannot be established that the reported analysis followed a prespecified plan.

**5.2: N (Moderate Certainty, 84%)**  
The Methods section specified the three LAT-derived outcomes—NumberAttributionScore, FrequencyScore, and ImportanceScore—and the Results section reported results for each of these outcomes at both pre- and post-intervention time points.

**5.3: N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
The Methods specified mixed within-between ANOVAs for the main LAT analyses, and the Results reported the corresponding group-by-time ANOVA results for all three LAT outcomes. No clear evidence indicates selection from multiple unreported eligible analyses.

**Result of the domain: Some concerns (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Although the planned LAT outcomes and main analytical approach were reported consistently between Methods and Results, no preregistration or prespecified analysis plan was available.

## Overall Bias Risk for the Article: High Risk (High Certainty, 94%)

The overall assessment is **High Risk** because Domain 2 (deviations from intended interventions) and Domain 4 (measurement of the outcome) were judged at high risk of bias.
