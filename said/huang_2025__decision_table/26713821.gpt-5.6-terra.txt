**Article ID**: Horie, 2016  
**Outcome Name**: Neuropsychological test performance  
**registration number**: NCT01286389  

## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: NI (Moderate Certainty, 88%)**  
**Reason:** The article states that participants were “random[ly] allocat[ed]” in a 1:1 randomized study, but does not describe a method for generating the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, blocks, or other specified method).

**1.2: NI (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** The article does not report allocation concealment procedures, such as central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, or identical numbered containers. Single masking of outcome assessors does not establish allocation concealment.

**1.3: N (High Certainty, 97%)**  
**Reason:** Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups. The article reports no statistically significant between-group differences in age, sex, education, cognitive screening measures, comorbidities, physical activity, APOE genotype, MCI type, or other reported baseline variables.

**Result of Domain 1: Some concerns (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** Allocation concealment was not reported (1.2 = NI), while baseline characteristics did not indicate a problem with randomization (1.3 = N).

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)

**2.1: Y (High Certainty, 97%)**  
**Reason:** Participants assigned to nutritional counselling attended 26–28 group meetings with nutritionists and received dietary counselling and caloric-restriction advice. Participants would necessarily have been aware of whether they received this intervention.

**2.2: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** Nutritionists delivering the group counselling necessarily knew which participants received the caloric-restriction counselling intervention. Geriatricians and other care providers could also potentially identify participants receiving the group intervention.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
**Reason:** Both groups received conventional medical care and physical-activity advice. The additional nutritional counselling was the protocol-defined intervention. No important nonprotocol interventions that were imbalanced between groups and prognostic for neuropsychological performance were reported.

**2.4: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** Five of 80 randomized participants did not complete follow-up (6.25%): four withdrew and one died from pneumonia. This is below the specified 10% threshold for important failures in implementing the intervention.

**2.5: NI (High Certainty, 92%)**  
**Reason:** The article notes that mobility difficulties may have decreased adherence to group meetings, but does not provide the number or proportion of participants with non-adherence, treatment cessation, crossover, or imperfect attendance. Therefore, the extent of non-adherence cannot be determined.

**2.6: NI (High Certainty, 91%)**  
**Reason:** The article used generalized estimating equations and generalized linear models, but did not report an analysis specifically designed to estimate the effect of adhering to the assigned intervention, such as instrumental-variable analysis or inverse-probability weighting for adherence-related censoring. An intention-to-treat analysis was not reported.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 93%)**  
**Reason:** Participants and intervention providers were aware of intervention allocation. In addition, adherence was insufficiently reported (2.5 = NI), and no appropriate analysis estimating the effect of adherence was reported (2.6 = NI).

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: PN (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** Neuropsychological follow-up data were available for 75 of 80 randomized participants: 75/80 = 93.75%. Neuropsychological test performance is a continuous outcome; thus, outcome data were available for 90% to less than 95% of randomized participants.

**3.2: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** The article does not report inverse-probability weighting, a sensitivity analysis for missing neuropsychological outcome data, or another appropriate method demonstrating that missing data did not bias the results. The reported analyses do not establish absence of missing-data bias.

**3.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 78%)**  
**Reason:** One participant died from pneumonia and four participants withdrew, but the reasons for the four withdrawals and their relationship to cognitive performance were not reported. Therefore, it cannot be determined whether missingness depended on the true neuropsychological outcome value.

**3.4: NI (Moderate Certainty, 75%)**  
**Reason:** The article does not report outcome values, cognitive characteristics, or withdrawal reasons for participants with missing follow-up neuropsychological data. It therefore does not allow determination of whether missingness was likely related to true cognitive performance.

**Result of Domain 3: Low risk (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** Follow-up neuropsychological outcome data were available for 93.75% of randomized participants (3.1 = PN), which meets the specified criterion for low risk in this domain.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** Neuropsychological test performance was measured using an established battery, including RAVLT, Digit Span, Trail Making Tests, Modified Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, phonemic and semantic verbal fluency, CAMcog, and IQCODE. These are established measures of cognitive domains relevant to mild cognitive impairment.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 91%)**  
**Reason:** The same neuropsychological measures were assessed at baseline and at 12 months for participants in both intervention groups. No group-specific measurement schedules, thresholds, or measurement procedures were reported.

**4.3: N (High Certainty, 97%)**  
**Reason:** The registered protocol states that the study used single masking and that the outcome assessor was masked. Neuropsychological test performance was assessed by outcome assessors rather than solely through participant self-report.

**4.4: Y (Moderate Certainty, 78%)**  
**Reason:** Neuropsychological test performance requires administration, scoring, and interpretation by an assessor and can potentially be influenced by assessor knowledge of intervention allocation. However, the registered protocol indicates that outcome assessors were masked.

**4.5: N (High Certainty, 93%)**  
**Reason:** The protocol reports masking of outcome assessors, and the article provides no evidence that outcome assessors became unmasked or that knowledge of treatment assignment influenced neuropsychological test assessment.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** Established neuropsychological instruments were used, measurements were comparable between groups, and outcome assessors were reported to be masked to intervention allocation.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1: N (High Certainty, 92%)**  
**Reason:** The trial was registered and identified neuropsychological test performance as a primary outcome. However, the article explicitly states that, because weight loss did not differ significantly between groups, the investigators “decided to a posteriori pool the longitudinal analysis between groups” and evaluate BMI change as a continuous variable. This analysis decision was made after observing the between-group weight-loss results.

**5.2: N (Moderate Certainty, 84%)**  
**Reason:** Multiple neuropsychological measures were collected, but the article reports results across the stated cognitive domains and indicates that complete cognitive-test results were provided in the supplemental material. There is no clear evidence that only favorable cognitive measures or time points were selectively reported.

**5.3: N (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
**Reason:** The article reports the planned longitudinal generalized estimating equation analyses and additionally reports generalized linear model analyses of BMI change and cognitive change. Although the latter analysis was post hoc, there is no clear evidence that one result was selected from multiple eligible analyses solely because of its statistical result.

**Result of Domain 5: Some concerns (High Certainty, 90%)**  
**Reason:** The pooled analysis examining BMI change and neuropsychological outcomes was explicitly undertaken a posteriori after the lack of a meaningful between-group difference in weight loss. This creates concern regarding selection of the analysis approach.

# Overall Bias Risk for the Article: High Risk (High Certainty, 94%)

**Reason:** Domain 2 was assessed as **High risk** because participants and intervention providers were aware of allocation, adherence was insufficiently reported, and no appropriate adherence-effect analysis was reported. Domain 5 also had **Some concerns** because a key BMI–cognition analysis was performed a posteriori.
