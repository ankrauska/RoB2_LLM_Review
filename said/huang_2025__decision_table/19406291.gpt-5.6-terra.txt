**Article ID**: Ashman, 2009  
**Outcome Name**: HDM score (Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression [HAM-D] score)  
**registration number**: NCT00233103  

## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y (99% High Certainty)**  
The study used balanced block randomization (blocks of 8) with “computer-generated random number sequences.”

**1.2: PY (85% Moderate Certainty)**  
Random assignments were placed in individual presealed envelopes, and the pharmacy implemented masking and random assignment. Study staff and participants were reported to be blinded. However, the report does not specify that envelopes were sequentially numbered and opaque, nor does it provide full procedural details of allocation concealment.

**1.3: Y (88% Moderate Certainty)**  
The abstract states that placebo recipients had a significantly lower income level than participants receiving sertraline. This statistically significant baseline difference indicates a potential problem with the randomization process, despite the Results text later reporting no significant differences in income and other baseline characteristics.

**Result of Domain 1: Some concerns (88% Moderate Certainty)**  
Allocation was generated appropriately and concealment was reported but incompletely described. A statistically significant baseline imbalance in income was reported.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions  
### Effect of assignment to intervention

**2.1: N (98% High Certainty)**  
Participants were unaware of their assigned intervention. The trial used placebo, and sertraline and placebo were packaged with identical markings.

**2.2: N (96% High Certainty)**  
Study staff, including the project physicians, were blinded to treatment allocation. The pharmacy performed random assignment and dispensed identically marked medication.

**2.3: Y (90% Moderate Certainty)**  
Important nonprotocol interventions were controlled: participants were required to abstain from initiating new psychosocial or pharmacologic treatments, and research contacts followed a standard protocol with constant duration across groups.

**2.4: Y (99% High Certainty)**  
There were failures in implementation for more than 10% of randomized participants. Eleven of 52 randomized participants did not complete the trial (21.2%), including participants removed for clinical reasons and participants who missed appointments, did not collect medication, or did not respond to follow-up.

**2.5: Y (99% High Certainty)**  
More than 10% of randomized participants had non-adherence relevant to the assigned regimen. Eight participants were noncompliant with the protocol by missing multiple follow-up visits, failing to pick up medication, or failing to respond to follow-up contacts (8/52, 15.4%).

**2.6: N (99% High Certainty)**  
The report used intention-to-treat analysis and a last-observation-carried-forward procedure. Under the specified criteria, ITT and LOCF are not appropriate methods for estimating the effect of adherence to the assigned intervention regimen.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (98% High Certainty)**  
Although participants and intervention providers were blinded and nonprotocol care was controlled, intervention implementation failures and non-adherence exceeded 10%, and the analyses used ITT and LOCF rather than an appropriate adherence-effect method.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: N (99% High Certainty)**  
HAM-D is a continuous outcome. Forty-one of 52 randomized participants completed the trial and had end-of-treatment data, corresponding to 78.8% complete observed outcome data. This is below 90%.

**3.2: N (99% High Certainty)**  
The study used last observation carried forward for participants with incomplete data. LOCF is not an appropriate method for correcting potential bias from missing outcome data. No sensitivity analysis or inverse-probability-weighting analysis was reported.

**3.3: Y (96% High Certainty)**  
Missingness could depend on the true HAM-D value. Participants who dropped out had higher HAM-D scores at their last assessment than participants who remained in treatment. In addition, some discontinuations were related to increased anxiety, increased depression, and an allergic reaction.

**3.4: Y (94% Moderate Certainty)**  
It is likely that missingness depended on the true HAM-D value because dropouts tended to have higher HAM-D scores, and clinical deterioration was reported among reasons for discontinuation. These factors indicate that missing outcome data may be related to depression severity.

**Result of Domain 3: High risk (97% High Certainty)**  
End-of-treatment HAM-D data were unavailable for more than 20% of randomized participants. The missing-data method was LOCF, and evidence indicates that missingness was likely related to depression severity.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (98% High Certainty)**  
The HAM-D was a predefined primary outcome. It is a 17-item clinician-rated measure of depression severity, and the report describes it as having high reliability and validity.

**4.2: N (95% High Certainty)**  
HAM-D was assessed at baseline, biweekly throughout treatment, and at the end of treatment in both intervention groups. The same outcome measure and assessment schedule were used across groups.

**4.3: N (95% High Certainty)**  
HAM-D is clinician rated. The study states that study staff and participants were blinded to whether sertraline or placebo was received. Depression severity was rated independently from the physician by a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist.

**4.4: Y (92% Moderate Certainty)**  
HAM-D is a clinician-rated depression scale and therefore includes subjective assessor judgment. Knowledge of intervention received could influence assessment if blinding were broken.

**4.5: N (93% Moderate Certainty)**  
There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced HAM-D assessment. Participants and study staff were reported to be blinded, placebo tablets had identical markings, and independent staff performed symptom ratings.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (95% High Certainty)**  
The HAM-D is a reliable predefined clinician-rated measure, outcome measurement was comparable between groups, and assessors were reported to be blinded.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1: Y (82% Moderate Certainty)**  
The trial was registered (NCT00233103) before study completion and publication. The registered primary outcome included HAM-D depression assessment at baseline and end of treatment. The article identified HAM-D as the primary outcome, described the intended analyses in the Methods, and reported HAM-D results using the stated approaches, including random-effects models and baseline-to-endpoint analyses.

**5.2: N (85% Moderate Certainty)**  
The article reported the HAM-D total score at baseline, over the six biweekly assessment points, and at end of treatment. It also reported analyses of HAM-D subscales. There is no clear evidence that only a favorable measurement scale, definition, or time point was selected from multiple eligible HAM-D measurements.

**5.3: N (85% Moderate Certainty)**  
The statistical methods described in the Methods—intention-to-treat analysis, LOCF analysis, random-effects repeated-measures models, and repeated-measures analyses—were reported in the Results. There is no clear evidence that only selected favorable analyses were reported.

**Result of Domain 5: Low Risk (84% Moderate Certainty)**  
The primary HAM-D outcome and its analyses were reported consistently with the study methods and registered outcome information, with no clear evidence of selective selection of outcome measurements or analyses.

# Overall Bias Risk for the Article: High Risk (97% High Certainty)

The overall risk of bias is **High Risk** because Domain 2 and Domain 3 were assessed as high risk. The principal concerns are substantial intervention non-adherence/implementation failures, use of ITT and LOCF rather than an adherence-effect analysis, incomplete HAM-D outcome data for 21.2% of randomized participants, and evidence that missing HAM-D data were related to depression severity.
