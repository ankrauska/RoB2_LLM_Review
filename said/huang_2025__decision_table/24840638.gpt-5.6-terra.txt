## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: NI (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** The article states that participants were “randomly assigned” in a 2:2:1 ratio within groups of five and that randomization was stratified by medication intent and study-entry order. However, it does not describe a specific sequence-generation method such as computer-generated random numbers, random-number tables, shuffled envelopes/cards, or other eligible random-sequence methods.

**1.2: NI (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** The article does not report any allocation-concealment method. There is no description of central allocation, sealed opaque sequentially numbered envelopes, numbered containers, or another method ensuring that allocation was concealed until enrolment and assignment.

**1.3: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** Baseline characteristics were reported as statistically similar except for education: a significantly higher proportion of percentile-schedule participants attended vocational/technical school/community college than fixed-criterion participants (χ² = 8.46, p < 0.01). This statistically significant baseline difference suggests a potential problem with the randomization process.

**Result of Domain 1: High risk (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** Allocation concealment was not reported (1.2 = NI), and there was a statistically significant baseline imbalance in education (1.3 = Y).

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** Immediately after randomization, all participants were given a description of the incentive contingency assigned to them. Percentile participants were told that their CO criterion changed to help them smoke less; fixed-criterion participants were told that incentives were earned only for not smoking; random-incentive participants were told incentives depended on attendance and not smoking. Therefore, participants were aware of their assigned intervention.

**2.2: Y (Moderate Certainty, 85%)**  
**Reason:** The intervention involved staff obtaining breath-CO samples, applying the assigned contingency, and delivering payments. The staff delivering the intervention would necessarily need to know whether participants were assigned to percentile, fixed-criterion, or random incentives in order to administer the relevant incentive schedule.

**2.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
**Reason:** Participants completed forms concerning use of smoking-cessation medication and smoking behavior. However, the article does not report whether smoking-cessation medication use or other nonprotocol interventions differed between groups. Therefore, balance of important nonprotocol interventions cannot be determined.

**2.4: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** Participants were expected to submit a breath-CO sample at each of 60 workday visits. Mean numbers of submitted samples were 42/60 in the percentile group, 40/60 in the fixed-criterion group, and 52/60 in the random-incentive group. Thus, more than 10% of scheduled intervention visits were missed in every group, indicating substantial failures in implementing the intended intervention/assessment schedule.

**2.5: Y (High Certainty, 92%)**  
**Reason:** Attendance was part of the assigned intervention regimen, because participants were expected to attend each workday for breath-CO testing and incentive delivery. Average missed visits exceeded 10% in all groups: approximately 30% in the percentile group, 33% in the fixed-criterion group, and 13% in the random-incentive group. This represents substantial non-adherence to the scheduled intervention regimen.

**2.6: NI (Moderate Certainty, 85%)**  
**Reason:** The reported analyses used Kruskal–Wallis tests, Mann–Whitney tests, chi-square tests, and sex-adjusted analyses. The article does not report instrumental-variable analysis, inverse-probability weighting, or another appropriate method specifically estimating the effect of adherence to the assigned intervention. It also does not explicitly report an intention-to-treat, per-protocol, or as-treated analysis for this outcome.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 93%)**  
**Reason:** Participants and intervention providers were aware of assigned intervention schedules; there was substantial missed attendance/non-adherence; nonprotocol interventions were not adequately reported as balanced; and no appropriate adherence-effect analysis was reported.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** The outcome was binary at each scheduled visit: breath CO <3 ppm versus ≥3 ppm/missed visit. Participants attended a mean of 42/60 visits in the percentile group, 40/60 in the fixed-criterion group, and 52/60 in the random-incentive group. Thus, a substantial proportion of scheduled outcome measurements was missing. The number of abstinent events was not much greater than the number of missing outcome assessments.

**3.2: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** No method correcting for potential bias from missing outcome data was reported. The article did not report inverse-probability weighting, an appropriate missing-data model, or sensitivity analyses addressing missing outcome data. The use of standard nonparametric and proportional tests does not correct missing-data bias.

**3.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
**Reason:** The article reports missed visits but does not provide reasons for missing breath-CO samples. Therefore, it cannot be determined whether missingness depended on the true smoking-abstinence outcome.

**3.4: NI (Moderate Certainty, 78%)**  
**Reason:** Reasons for missed visits and group-specific patterns or causes of missing outcome data were not reported. Consequently, it is not possible to determine whether missingness was likely related to true smoking-abstinence status.

**Result of Domain 3: High risk (Moderate Certainty, 88%)**  
**Reason:** There was substantial missing outcome data, no reported method to address bias from missing data, and insufficient information to exclude the possibility that missingness was related to smoking-abstinence status.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** Smoking abstinence was measured objectively using a Vitalograph CO monitor. Participants followed a standardized procedure involving a deep breath, a 20-second breath hold, and exhalation into a disposable mouthpiece. The predefined abstinence threshold was breath CO <3 ppm. Breath CO monitoring is an objective and appropriate measurement method for this outcome.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** All randomized groups were expected to submit breath-CO samples each workday for 60 visits. The same CO-monitoring method and the same abstinence threshold of <3 ppm were used across groups.

**4.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 85%)**  
**Reason:** The outcome was objectively measured by a CO monitor, but the article does not explicitly state whether persons conducting or recording the breath-CO assessments were blinded to intervention assignment.

**4.4: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** Breath CO concentration measured with a CO monitor is an objective outcome. Assessment is not meaningfully susceptible to influence from knowledge of intervention assignment.

**4.5: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention assignment was likely to influence an objectively measured breath-CO result.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** The outcome was objectively measured using a standardized and appropriate CO-monitoring procedure, with the same threshold and measurement schedule across groups. Although assessor blinding was not reported, knowledge of allocation was unlikely to influence the objective CO result.
