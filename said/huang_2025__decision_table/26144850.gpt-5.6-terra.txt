## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y — High Certainty (99%)**  
Patients were allocated using “268 three-digit random numbers from the table,” written in the patient registers in advance. Patients with even numbers received PB-MDT and those with odd numbers received ROM-6. Random-number tables are an adequate sequence-generation method.

**1.2: NI — High Certainty (98%)**  
The article does not describe allocation concealment methods such as central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or identical numbered drug containers. Although the random numbers were written in registers in advance, the article does not explicitly state whether the allocation schedule was concealed from those enrolling participants.

**1.3: N — Moderate Certainty (85%)**  
Baseline characteristics were generally comparable. Mean age (36.4 versus 40.2 years; P = 0.059) and mean disease duration (35.8 versus 32.0 months; P = 0.29) were not significantly different between groups. The distributions of lesion number and nerve thickening were also broadly similar.

**Result of the domain: Some concerns — High Certainty (96%)**  
The random sequence was adequately generated and there was no clear baseline imbalance suggesting a problem with randomization. However, allocation concealment was not reported.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: N — High Certainty (98%)**  
The article explicitly states that “blinding to treatment was not undertaken.” According to the specified assessment instruction for an open-label/non-blinded experiment, the response is N.

**2.2: Y — High Certainty (95%)**  
Carers and personnel delivering the interventions were aware of assigned treatment because monthly ROM and PB-MDT regimens involved different drug packs and different administration procedures. ROM was administered monthly under supervision, whereas PB-MDT included supervised monthly doses plus daily self-administered dapsone.

**2.3: Y — Moderate Certainty (72%)**  
No important non-protocol interventions that were prognostic for the 2-year cure outcome and imbalanced between groups were reported. Corticosteroids were given for clinical reactions according to the study procedures, rather than being reported as an important imbalanced non-protocol intervention.

**2.4: Y — High Certainty (99%)**  
There were intervention-implementation failures exceeding 10%: 27/134 (20.1%) participants in the PB-MDT group and 19/134 (14.2%) in the ROM-6 group defaulted or discontinued treatment. Overall, 46/268 (17.2%) participants did not complete treatment.

**2.5: Y — High Certainty (99%)**  
Non-adherence exceeded 10% in both groups, as indicated by treatment default/discontinuation rates of 20.1% for PB-MDT and 14.2% for ROM-6.

**2.6: N — High Certainty (92%)**  
The cure-rate result at 2 years was reported among participants who completed treatment and were followed up, rather than using an appropriate method to estimate the effect of adherence while addressing the substantial treatment non-adherence. No instrumental-variable analysis, inverse-probability weighting, or equivalent appropriate method was reported.

**Result of the domain: High risk — High Certainty (96%)**  
There were substantial failures in implementation and non-adherence (>10%), and the analysis did not use an appropriate method to estimate the effect of adhering to the assigned intervention.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: Y — High Certainty (95%)**  
Cure rate is a binary outcome. At 2 years, 100 PB-MDT participants and 103 ROM-6 participants were followed up, with approximately 97 and 102 participants cured, respectively (about 199 cure events). The number of observed cure events was much greater than the 65 participants without 2-year follow-up data (268 randomized minus 203 followed up).

**3.2: N — High Certainty (95%)**  
No analysis method that could correct for bias from missing outcome data, such as inverse-probability weighting or sensitivity analysis, was reported. Intention-to-treat analysis was not reported as a missing-data correction method.

**3.3: Y — Moderate Certainty (80%)**  
Reasons for treatment discontinuation included feeling cured, drug intolerance/weakness, swelling of feet, vomiting/diarrhea, pregnancy, and loss to treatment. Some of these reasons, particularly feeling cured and drug-related symptoms, may be related to the participants’ clinical outcome status.

**3.4: Y — Moderate Certainty (78%)**  
The reported reasons for missing data and discontinuation suggest that missingness could be related to clinical status and cure outcome. In addition, default rates differed between groups (20.1% PB-MDT versus 14.2% ROM-6).

**Result of the domain: Low risk — High Certainty (94%)**  
For this binary outcome, the observed number of cure events at 2 years was much greater than the number of participants with missing outcome data.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N — Moderate Certainty (85%)**  
Cure was predefined clinically as complete lesion resolution, flattening/hypopigmentation with reduction in lesion size, and/or recovery of sensation. Clinical lesion and sensory assessment is an established approach for assessing clinical cure in paucibacillary leprosy.

**4.2: N — High Certainty (90%)**  
Both groups were monitored using the same clinical follow-up approach. Formal assessments were conducted every 6 months after completion of treatment, and the same cure definition was applied to both groups.

**4.3: Y — Moderate Certainty (80%)**  
Blinding to treatment was not undertaken. Cure was assessed clinically by study personnel, including trained paramedical workers and medical staff, who could be aware of the treatment received.

**4.4: Y — High Certainty (90%)**  
The cure outcome involved clinical judgement regarding lesion activity, erythema, infiltration, lesion size, new lesions, nerve thickening, and recovery of sensation. These assessments include subjective clinical components that could be influenced by knowledge of treatment allocation.

**4.5: N — Moderate Certainty (75%)**  
Although outcome assessment was unblinded and included subjective clinical components, the article provides no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention assignment actually influenced the assessment of cure.

**Result of the domain: Some concerns — Moderate Certainty (83%)**  
Outcome assessment was not blinded and involved clinical judgement; however, there was no clear evidence that knowledge of treatment assignment influenced cure assessments.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1: NI — High Certainty (95%)**  
The trial recruited participants between 2001 and 2004 and follow-up ended in 2010, whereas the trial was registered only on 22 November 2013. No prospectively finalized prespecified analysis plan or protocol available before unblinded outcome data were available was reported.

**5.2: N — Moderate Certainty (82%)**  
The cure outcome was clinically defined in the Methods section, and cure status was reported at the planned follow-up time points, including the 2-year assessment. There is no clear evidence that the 2-year cure result was selected from multiple unreported eligible cure definitions or measurement methods.

**5.3: NI — Moderate Certainty (75%)**  
The article reports use of Pearson χ² tests for proportions and Cox regression for confounders, but it does not provide a prospectively specified analysis plan or sufficient information to determine whether the reported 2-year cure result was selected from multiple eligible analyses.

**Result of the domain: Some concerns — High Certainty (90%)**  
The study was registered only after completion of follow-up, and no prospectively finalized analysis plan was available. There is no clear evidence of selective selection from multiple outcome measurements, but selection from multiple analyses cannot be excluded.
