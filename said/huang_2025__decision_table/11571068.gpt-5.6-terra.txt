## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y (99% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The article explicitly states that women “were assigned randomly via random number tables” to supportive counselling, stress management, or alcohol-focused couples therapy.

**1.2: NI (98% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Although random number tables were used, the article does not report any allocation-concealment procedure, such as central allocation, sealed opaque sequentially numbered envelopes, or identical numbered treatment containers.

**1.3: N (97% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The article reports no statistically significant pre-treatment differences across treatment conditions for women’s GHQ and RSS scores. It also reports no significant differences in demographic characteristics across conditions.

**Result of Domain 1: Some concerns (97% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Random-sequence generation was adequate and baseline outcome measures did not differ between groups; however, allocation concealment was not reported.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: Y (98% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Participants received visibly different psychological interventions: supportive counselling, stress management, or alcohol-focused couples therapy. No participant blinding, placebo, or sham intervention was reported. Women were therefore aware of their assigned intervention.

**2.2: Y (99% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Therapists delivered different treatment manuals and intervention components for the three conditions. Therefore, carers and people delivering the interventions were aware of participants’ assigned intervention.

**2.3: Y (75% Moderate Certainty)**  
**Reason:** No important nonprotocol interventions that were prognostic for women’s burden and distress and imbalanced between groups were reported. The provision of stress management to women in the AFCT group when their partners declined participation was part of the stated AFCT protocol.

**2.4: Y (99% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Of 61 randomized women, 47 completed treatment. Thus, 14/61 participants (23%) did not complete treatment, exceeding the 10% threshold for failures in implementing the intervention.

**2.5: NI (92% Moderate Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The article reports that adherence to treatment suggestions was not formally assessed: “We did not assess adherence to those suggestions formally.” Therefore, non-adherence to the assigned intervention regimen cannot be fully determined.

**2.6: N (93% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The study used repeated-measures ANOVAs based on available outcome data. It did not use an instrumental-variable analysis, inverse-probability weighting, or another appropriate method to estimate the effect of adhering to the intervention in the presence of non-adherence and attrition.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (94% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Participants and therapists were aware of treatment assignment. More than 10% of randomized women discontinued treatment, adherence was not formally assessed, and the analyses did not appropriately estimate the effect of adherence to the assigned intervention.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: N (98% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Women’s burden and distress were continuous outcomes measured using the RSS and GHQ. Sixty-one women were randomized, while Table 1 reports data for 44 participants across the three groups (17 + 14 + 13), corresponding to approximately 72% complete outcome data. This is below 90%.

**3.2: N (99% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The article does not report inverse-probability weighting, sensitivity analyses for missing data, or another method that could demonstrate that missing outcome data did not bias the results. Repeated-measures ANOVA does not correct potential bias from missing data.

**3.3: Y (96% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The article reports that most women who dropped out had left their partners shortly after starting the programme and had been very unhappy in the relationship. Leaving a severely distressed relationship is plausibly related to the women’s burden, distress, and psychological symptoms.

**3.4: Y (96% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The reported reasons for missing outcome data suggest a relationship between missingness and women’s burden and distress: women who discontinued treatment commonly did so after leaving unhappy relationships, and all declined further assessment.

**Result of Domain 3: High risk (97% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Outcome data were available for fewer than 90% of randomized women. No analysis was reported to address missing-data bias, and the reasons for dropout indicate that missingness was likely related to women’s burden and distress.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (98% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Women’s burden and distress were measured using the Relative Stress Scale (RSS) and General Health Questionnaire (GHQ). The article reports good reliability for the RSS (test–retest reliability r = 0.85) and satisfactory internal reliability for the GHQ (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.93).

**4.2: N (95% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The RSS and GHQ were administered at the same planned time points—pre-treatment, post-treatment, and 6-month follow-up—for all intervention groups.

**4.3: Y (98% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** RSS and GHQ outcomes were patient-reported measures. Participants were aware of their assigned psychological treatment condition; therefore, outcome assessors, namely the participants completing the questionnaires, were aware of the intervention received.

**4.4: Y (99% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Women’s burden, stress, and psychological distress measured by RSS and GHQ are subjective patient-reported outcomes. Their assessment could be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**4.5: Y (94% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The study compared distinct psychological therapies without participant blinding and used self-reported measures of stress and distress. Knowledge of receiving a particular therapy could plausibly influence participants’ reporting of subjective burden and psychological symptoms.

**Result of Domain 4: High risk (96% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** Although valid and reliable measures were used at comparable assessment times, the outcomes were subjective self-reported measures obtained from unblinded participants. Assessment of women’s burden and distress was likely influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1: NI (95% High Certainty)**  
**Reason:** No trial registration number, protocol publication, or prespecified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available was reported.

**5.2: N (82% Moderate Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The Methods section specified the RSS and GHQ as measures of women’s burden and distress, assessed at pre-treatment, post-treatment, and 6-month follow-up. The Results section reports results for both RSS and GHQ at these planned time points. There is no clear evidence that only selected measurements or time points were reported.

**5.3: N (80% Moderate Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The article prespecified separate repeated-measures treatment-condition-by-time ANOVAs for outcomes and reports these analyses for the RSS and GHQ. No clear evidence indicates that the reported numerical results were selected from multiple eligible analyses based on the findings.

**Result of Domain 5: Some concerns (89% Moderate Certainty)**  
**Reason:** The reported RSS and GHQ outcomes and analyses were consistent with the Methods section, but no preregistered protocol or finalized prespecified analysis plan was available.
