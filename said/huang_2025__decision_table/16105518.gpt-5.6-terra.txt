## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: NI (High Certainty, 98%)**  
The article states that participants “were randomly assigned to three experimental groups,” but does not report how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated numbers, random-number table, block randomization, envelopes, coin tossing, etc.).

**1.2: NI (High Certainty, 98%)**  
No information is provided on concealment of allocation before enrolment and assignment. The article does not mention central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, or another allocation-concealment mechanism.

**1.3: NI (High Certainty, 95%)**  
The article does not report a baseline-characteristics table or baseline comparison between the three practice groups. Therefore, whether baseline differences suggested a problem with randomization cannot be assessed.

**Result of Domain 1: Some concerns (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Allocation concealment was not reported, while no baseline information was available to identify or exclude important imbalances between groups.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Participants were likely aware of their assigned practice schedule because they actively participated in visibly different training formats: whole-task functional-order practice, random-order practice of individual skills, or blocked practice of individual skills.

**2.2: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
The orthopedic faculty member who circulated during the acquisition sessions and provided feedback was likely aware of the assigned intervention. The distinct practice schedules would have made group allocation apparent to the intervention provider.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
No important non-protocol interventions that were imbalanced across groups are reported. All groups received the same instructional video, performed the pre-test and post-tests, completed 60 minutes of acquisition practice, and received faculty feedback during acquisition.

**2.4: N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
The article reports that all 28 participants completed the study assessments, with performances assessed before acquisition, immediately after acquisition, and after the rest period. No implementation failures or withdrawals affecting more than 10% of participants were reported.

**2.5: NI (High Certainty, 95%)**  
The article does not report adherence, protocol deviations, switching between practice schedules, or whether participants completed their allocated practice regimen as intended.

**2.6: NI (High Certainty, 95%)**  
No analysis specifically estimating the effect of adhering to the assigned intervention is reported. The reported mixed ANOVA estimated differences between assigned practice groups, rather than an adherence-adjusted effect.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 92%)**  
Participants and intervention providers were aware of the assigned practice schedule. In addition, adherence to the allocated practice regimen was not reported, and no appropriate adherence-adjusted analysis was conducted.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
The outcome was measured using an operation-specific checklist consisting of detailed procedures. Checklist items were selected by a panel of three experienced orthopedic surgeons to represent actions necessary to perform the task effectively. No evidence indicates that the measurement method was inappropriate.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 92%)**  
All groups completed the same whole bone-plating task at the same assessment occasions: pre-test, immediate post-test, and delayed post-test. The same operation-specific checklist was used across groups.

**4.3: NI (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Two experienced orthopedic surgeons evaluated performance, but the article does not state whether these outcome assessors were blinded to participants’ assigned practice group.

**4.4: Y (High Certainty, 93%)**  
The operation-specific checklist involves assessor judgment of detailed surgical-task performance. This is a subjective assessor-rated outcome and could be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**4.5: N (Moderate Certainty, 76%)**  
Although assessor blinding was not reported, the article provides no clear evidence that assessors’ knowledge of group assignment actually influenced checklist scoring.

**Result of Domain 4: Some concerns (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
The checklist was applied comparably across groups and was not clearly inappropriate. However, outcome-assessor blinding was not reported, and the assessor-rated checklist outcome could potentially be influenced by awareness of practice-group assignment.
