## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
**Reason:** The article explicitly states that patients “were randomized to telephone contact or no telephone contact using a computer-generated list of random numbers.”

**1.2: NI (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** The article does not report any method of allocation concealment, such as central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, or sequentially numbered identical containers. Randomization and the intervention being telephone contact do not establish that allocation was concealed before enrollment and assignment.

**1.3: N (High Certainty, 96%)**

**Result of the domain: Some concerns (High Certainty, 97%)**

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
**Reason:** Participants assigned to telephone contact received weekly calls from a pharmacist for 12 weeks, whereas control participants received no telephone contact. Participants would necessarily have been aware of whether they received these calls.

**2.2: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
**Reason:** The pharmacist delivering the telephone intervention knew which patients were assigned to receive weekly telephone contact. Personnel delivering the intervention were therefore aware of assigned intervention status.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 84%)**  
**Reason:** Both groups received the same lipid-lowering prescriptions, extensive initial medication counseling, and dietary instruction. The article does not report important nonprotocol interventions that were imbalanced between groups and prognostic for compliance.

**2.4: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** Thirty patients were enrolled and “none of whom was lost to follow-up.” The article does not report failures in intervention implementation affecting more than 10% of participants.

**2.5: Y (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** Non-adherence was the outcome under assessment and was clearly present in more than 10% of participants. At 2 years, only 7% of the no-telephone-contact group were compliant with both medications, compared with 47% in the telephone-contact group. Thus, substantial non-adherence occurred.

**2.6: N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
**Reason:** The reported analysis compared the proportion of compliant participants between randomized groups using the chi-square statistic. The article does not report instrumental-variable analysis, inverse-probability weighting, or another appropriate method to estimate the effect of adhering to the intervention regimen. The reported group-comparison analysis does not correct for non-adherence.

**Result of the domain: High risk (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** Participants and intervention providers were aware of assigned intervention status. In addition, substantial non-adherence occurred, and the analysis was not an appropriate adherence-effect analysis according to the specified criteria.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** Compliance was measured using pill and packet counts at 6 and 12 weeks and pharmacy refill records at 1 and 2 years. Noncompliance was predefined as returning more than 20% of prescribed pills or failing to fill at least 80% of prescriptions. These are established, objective methods for measuring medication possession/medication-taking behavior.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** Compliance was measured in both groups using the same methods at the same follow-up time points: pill and packet counts at 6 and 12 weeks and refill records at 1 and 2 years.

**4.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 77%)**  
**Reason:** The article does not state whether the individuals conducting pill/packet counts or obtaining pharmacy refill records were blinded to intervention assignment. The statement that pill and packet counts were “not performed within the patient’s view” does not establish blinding of outcome assessors to allocation.

**4.4: N (High Certainty, 91%)**  
**Reason:** Compliance was determined using pill/packet counts and pharmacy refill records, rather than subjective patient-reported ratings. These measurement approaches are relatively objective and are not readily influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment.

**4.5: N (Moderate Certainty, 86%)**  
**Reason:** There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention allocation influenced the pill/packet counts or pharmacy refill-record ascertainment of compliance.

**Result of the domain: Low risk (High Certainty, 91%)**  
**Reason:** The outcome measurement methods were appropriate and comparable across groups. Although assessor blinding was not reported, compliance was assessed using relatively objective pill/packet counts and pharmacy refill records, and there is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention status influenced assessment.

**5.1: NI (High Certainty, 97%)**

**5.2: N (Moderate Certainty, 86%)**  
**Reason:** The Methods section prespecified compliance measurement using pill/packet counts at 6 and 12 weeks and refill records at 1 and 2 years. The Results section reports compliance findings for these planned time points, including both short-term and long-term compliance outcomes. No clear evidence indicates selective reporting from multiple eligible compliance measurements.

**5.3: N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
**Reason:** The Methods section specified chi-square testing for proportions of compliant participants between groups. The article reports group comparisons for compliance consistent with this stated analytical approach. No clear evidence shows that results were selected from multiple eligible analyses.

**Result of the domain: Some concerns (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** There is no reported preregistered protocol or prespecified analysis plan finalized before outcome data were available; therefore, question 5.1 = NI. Although there is no clear evidence of selective choice among outcome measurements or analyses, the absence of a verifiable prespecified plan results in some concerns.
