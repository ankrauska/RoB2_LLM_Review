## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: NI (Moderate Certainty, 88%)**  
**Reason:** The article states that participants were stratified and then “randomized” to the three study conditions. However, it does not report a method used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, block randomization, or similar method).

**1.2: NI (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** No allocation-concealment method is reported. The article does not mention central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, sealed envelopes, identical containers, or any other procedure to conceal allocation before assignment.

**1.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
**Reason:** The article reports overall baseline characteristics for the full randomized sample but does not report baseline characteristics separately by intervention group. Therefore, baseline differences between groups cannot be assessed.

**Result of the domain: Some concerns (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** Allocation concealment was not reported (1.2 = NI), and there is no reported evidence of problematic baseline differences (1.3 = NI). According to the specified algorithm, this results in some concerns.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** Participants were aware of their assigned intervention because the treatment conditions involved visibly different medication-reporting schedules and settings: routine clinic care with once- or twice-weekly reporting versus 28-day MMM in either a clinic or physician-office setting. No participant placebo or sham procedure was used.

**2.2: Y (High Certainty, 97%)**  
**Reason:** Carers and personnel delivering the intervention necessarily knew the assigned intervention. Clinic staff, physicians, nurses, nurse practitioners, and counselors delivered different reporting schedules, medication supplies, counseling arrangements, and treatment settings according to group assignment.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 78%)**  
**Reason:** The protocol specified comparable monitoring across groups: two urine samples per month, one medication recall per month, and at least one monthly counseling session. Intensified stepped-care treatment was triggered by drug-positive urine specimens or medication-recall failures and was available across all study conditions. No important imbalanced nonprotocol intervention is reported.

**2.4: Y (High Certainty, 93%)**  
**Reason:** More than 10% of randomized participants failed to continue implementation of their allocated intervention. Five of 78 randomized patients withdrew immediately after assignment, and seven of the 73 participants who began the study subsequently dropped out during the 6-month follow-up. Thus, at least 12/78 (15.4%) did not continue their assigned randomized treatment.

**2.5: Y (High Certainty, 91%)**  
**Reason:** More than 10% of participants had protocol-related non-adherence or treatment changes that could affect retention. Twenty-one of 73 participants (29%) required periods of intensified care because of drug-positive urine results or medication-recall failures. Nineteen participants had at least one failed medication recall, and one office-based MMM participant discontinued MMM because he reported inability to manage the 28-day methadone supply.

**2.6: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** Treatment retention was analyzed in the stated “intent-to-treat sample (n=73)” using survival analysis. Under the specified criteria, intention-to-treat analysis is not an appropriate method for estimating the effect of adhering to the assigned intervention when non-adherence is present.

**Result of the domain: High risk (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** Participants and intervention providers were aware of assigned intervention (2.1 = Y; 2.2 = Y), important implementation failures and non-adherence occurred in more than 10% of participants (2.4 = Y; 2.5 = Y), and the analysis used was not appropriate for estimating the effect of adherence (2.6 = N).

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: N (High Certainty, 93%)**  
**Reason:** A total of 78 participants were randomized, but the retention analysis was conducted in 73 participants who began the study. Five participants withdrew upon assignment and were excluded from the reported intent-to-treat analysis. In addition, only 66 of the 73 participants who started randomized care completed 6 months. The observed retention events were not much greater than the number of participants with missing outcome data.

**3.2: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** The article does not report inverse-probability weighting, sensitivity analyses for missing outcome data, or another method demonstrating that missing outcome data did not bias the treatment-retention result. The stated intention-to-treat analysis does not itself correct bias caused by missing outcome data.

**3.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 79%)**  
**Reason:** Some withdrawals were plausibly related to the treatment-retention outcome or the ability/willingness to remain in assigned treatment. Reasons included dislike of the medication-recall frequency, inability to manage a 28-day medication supply, abrupt departure from treatment, and a decision to taper methadone.

**3.4: Y (High Certainty, 88%)**  
**Reason:** The reported reasons for missing retention outcome data indicate a likely relationship between missingness and retention. In particular, discontinuation due to dislike of the recall procedure, inability to manage assigned medication supply, and abrupt departure from treatment are directly related to continuing in treatment.

**Result of the domain: High risk (High Certainty, 91%)**  
**Reason:** Outcome data were not available for all or nearly all randomized participants (3.1 = N), no evidence was provided that missing-data bias was corrected (3.2 = N), and missingness could and was likely to depend on the true retention outcome (3.3 = Y; 3.4 = Y).

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** Treatment retention was measured as completion/continuation in randomized treatment during the 6-month follow-up and analyzed using survival analysis. Treatment status and discontinuation are appropriate and reliable measures for treatment retention.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 90%)**  
**Reason:** Retention was assessed over the same 6-month follow-up period in all three randomized groups. The article reports six-month retention for routine care, clinic-based MMM, and office-based MMM using comparable definitions.

**4.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 74%)**  
**Reason:** Treatment retention was not patient-reported; however, the article does not explicitly state whether personnel who ascertained or analyzed treatment-retention outcomes were blinded to assigned intervention.

**4.4: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** Treatment retention is an objective outcome based on whether participants remained in or discontinued randomized treatment. It is not a subjective symptom rating or assessor judgment.

**4.5: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced ascertainment of treatment retention. Retention is based on objectively recorded treatment continuation or discontinuation.

**Result of the domain: Low risk (High Certainty, 92%)**  
**Reason:** The retention outcome was measured using an appropriate method (4.1 = N), measured comparably across groups (4.2 = N), and was objective such that assessment was not likely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment (4.4 = N; 4.5 = N).
