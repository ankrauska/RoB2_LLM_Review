## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Reason: Allocation was performed by patients/caregivers drawing one of 100 sealed envelopes containing intervention or control assignments. This is a lottery-type random allocation method.

**1.2: Y (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: The article states that the envelopes were “sealed nontransparent envelopes,” which is a specifically described opaque-envelope allocation-concealment method.

**1.3: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: Baseline patient and caregiver characteristics were reported for both groups. No statistically significant baseline differences were reported, including age, sex, dementia severity, MMSE score, Barthel Index, caregiver characteristics, and caregiver health.

**Result of Domain 1: Low risk (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: The allocation method was randomized through envelope drawing, allocation was concealed using sealed nontransparent envelopes, and baseline characteristics did not suggest a problem with randomization.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Reason: Participants and caregivers would have been aware of whether they received the intensive dementia family care coordinator program, including counseling, home visits, training courses, follow-up calls, and telephone availability, versus usual care.

**2.2: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Reason: The dementia family care coordinator and other personnel delivering the intervention necessarily knew which participants were assigned to receive the coordinator-led support program.

**2.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 70%)**  
Reason: The article describes usual services in the control group and coordinator-arranged services in the intervention group, but does not provide sufficient information to determine whether important nonprotocol interventions were balanced between groups.

**2.4: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Reason: More than 10% of participants experienced events that stopped continued implementation of the community-based intervention. By 24 months, 9/53 (17%) intervention participants had died at home and 17/53 (32%) had been institutionalized.

**2.5: NI (Moderate Certainty, 72%)**  
Reason: The article reports no dropout from follow-up but does not provide adherence data regarding attendance at training courses, receipt of planned contacts, discontinuation of the support program, or crossover between groups.

**2.6: NI (Moderate Certainty, 70%)**  
Reason: The analysis used Kaplan–Meier survival estimates and time-dependent Cox regression according to randomized study group. No instrumental-variable analysis, inverse-probability weighting, or other method specifically estimating the effect of adhering to the intervention was reported.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 91%)**  
Reason: Participants and intervention providers were aware of allocation; there were substantial implementation failures due to death and institutionalization; adherence was not reported; and no appropriate analysis of the effect of adherence was reported.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: Time to institutionalization was clearly predefined as the period from enrollment to placement in long-term institutional care. Institutionalization and death were recorded using the Social Insurance Institution register, an appropriate and reliable source for this outcome.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: Institutionalization status was obtained from the same Social Insurance Institution register for both intervention and control groups, using the same outcome definition and follow-up period.

**4.3: N (High Certainty, 97%)**  
Reason: Decisions to permanently institutionalize patients were made by groups of experts who were reported to be blind to allocation and generally unaware that the patient was participating in the study.

**4.4: N (High Certainty, 93%)**  
Reason: Institutionalization is an objectively identifiable event, and the date of placement was obtained from an administrative register. In addition, the institutionalization decision-makers were blinded to allocation.

**4.5: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Reason: There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced outcome assessment. The institutionalization decision-making groups were blind to allocation, and the outcome was registry based.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: The outcome was objectively defined, measured comparably in both groups through the same register, and institutionalization decisions were made by assessors blinded to allocation.
