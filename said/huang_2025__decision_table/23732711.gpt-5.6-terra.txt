## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1 Was the allocation sequence adequately generated?**: **Y** — High Certainty (99%)  
**Reason**: Participants were “randomly assigned” by an investigator using a “computer-generated randomized list.”

**1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**: **NI** — High Certainty (96%)  
**Reason**: The article reports computer-generated randomization and states that the investigator had no knowledge of the participants, but it does not describe allocation-concealment procedures such as central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, or sequentially numbered identical containers.

**1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**: **N** — High Certainty (95%)  
**Reason**: Baseline characteristics were generally similar between sunscreen groups and between β-carotene and placebo groups. The article reports no differences in phenotype, sun exposure, or pretrial sunscreen use. Although slightly more participants were allocated to β-carotene than placebo, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.06).

**Result of Domain 1**: **Some concerns** — High Certainty (96%)  
**Reason**: The allocation sequence was adequately generated and baseline characteristics did not indicate a randomization problem; however, allocation concealment was not reported.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?**: **Y** — High Certainty (98%)  
**Reason**: Participants allocated to daily sunscreen were instructed to apply sunscreen every morning and reapply it after sweating, bathing, or prolonged outdoor exposure. The discretionary sunscreen group did not receive placebo sunscreen because placebo sunscreen was considered unethical. Therefore, participants could identify their sunscreen allocation.

**2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial?**: **NI** — Moderate Certainty (80%)  
**Reason**: The article does not report whether trial personnel who provided sunscreen or supplements, or otherwise delivered the interventions, were blinded to sunscreen allocation.

**2.3 Were important nonprotocol interventions balanced across intervention groups?**: **Y** — High Certainty (91%)  
**Reason**: During the trial, reported outdoor sun exposure was similar between daily and discretionary sunscreen groups. Other sun-protection behaviors were also similar: shade seeking was reported by 54% versus 53%, and hat use by 63% versus 67%, respectively.

**2.4 Were there failures in implementing the intervention that could have affected the outcome?**: **NI** — Moderate Certainty (76%)  
**Reason**: The article reports outcome-data losses and adherence information but does not provide sufficient information on intervention-implementation failures, such as intervention discontinuation or withdrawal from the intervention, separately from missing outcome data.

**2.5 Was there non-adherence to the assigned intervention regimen that could have affected participants’ outcomes?**: **Y** — High Certainty (97%)  
**Reason**: Non-adherence exceeded 10%. By 1996, only 77% of daily sunscreen participants reported applying sunscreen at least 3 to 4 days per week, despite instructions for daily application. In addition, supplement adherence of at least 80% of prescribed tablets was reported by only 68% of β-carotene participants and 67% of placebo participants.

**2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of adhering to the intervention?**: **N** — High Certainty (98%)  
**Reason**: The article states that data were analyzed according to treatment as randomly allocated, which is an intention-to-treat analysis. No instrumental-variable analysis, inverse-probability weighting for adherence censoring, or other appropriate method to estimate the effect of adherence was reported.

**Result of Domain 2**: **High risk** — High Certainty (95%)  
**Reason**: Participants were aware of sunscreen allocation, important non-adherence occurred in more than 10% of participants, and the analysis was according to randomized allocation rather than an appropriate analysis estimating the effect of adherence to the assigned intervention.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**: **N** — High Certainty (99%)  
**Reason**: Change in photoaging required both baseline and follow-up microtopography grades. Of 903 eligible randomized participants, 604 had good-quality replicas at both baseline and follow-up (604/903 = 66.9%), which is below 90%.

**3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**: **Y** — High Certainty (94%)  
**Reason**: The investigators conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to participants with complete photoaging grades and analyses using multiple imputation for missing photoaging grades and covariates. They reported that these analyses produced results consistent with the primary analysis.

**3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**: **N** — Moderate Certainty (83%)  
**Reason**: Missing microtopography grades in 1996 were not associated with baseline microtopography grade. Thus, the reported evidence does not indicate that missingness depended on the baseline value of the measured photoaging outcome.

**3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**: **N** — High Certainty (90%)  
**Reason**: The investigators examined characteristics associated with incomplete data, including baseline photoaging grade, and adjusted analyses for factors associated with having one missing microtopography grade. Treatment allocation was not associated with missing grades, and complete-case and multiple-imputation analyses were consistent with the main findings.

**Result of Domain 3**: **Low risk** — High Certainty (94%)  
**Reason**: Although complete outcome data were available for fewer than 90% of randomized participants, sensitivity analyses and multiple-imputation analyses gave results consistent with the main analysis, and missing follow-up grade was not associated with baseline microtopography grade.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**: **N** — High Certainty (98%)  
**Reason**: Photoaging was assessed using silicone skin-surface replicas from the back of the hand and graded with the Beagley and Gibson microtopography scale. The article reports high intra-grader and inter-grader repeatability (weighted κ statistics of 0.81 and 0.86, respectively) and states that this method is a valid measure of skin photoaging.

**4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**: **N** — High Certainty (94%)  
**Reason**: The same skin-replica procedure, anatomical site, grading scale, and baseline and follow-up time points were used across intervention groups.

**4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**: **N** — High Certainty (99%)  
**Reason**: The article explicitly states that experienced assessors grading the skin replicas were unaware of treatment allocation.

**4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**: **Y** — High Certainty (93%)  
**Reason**: Microtopography grading is an assessor-rated outcome involving judgment of skin-surface features and is therefore potentially subjective if assessors know the intervention received.

**4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**: **N** — High Certainty (98%)  
**Reason**: Assessors were blinded to treatment allocation, and there is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention status influenced the outcome assessment.

**Result of Domain 4**: **Low risk** — High Certainty (97%)  
**Reason**: The outcome measure was reliable and valid, measurement was comparable across groups, and assessors were blinded to treatment allocation. There is no evidence that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available for analysis?**: **Y** — Moderate Certainty (81%)  
**Reason**: The article identifies change in photoaging from 1992 to 1996 as the primary outcome and describes the planned microtopography measurement and ordinal logistic-regression analysis in the Methods section. The Results section reports the corresponding microtopography-grade results and treatment-effect estimates. The article also refers to an earlier study protocol and design publication.

**5.2 Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?**: **N** — High Certainty (89%)  
**Reason**: The photoaging outcome was measured using the prespecified microtopography grading of silicone replicas obtained at baseline and follow-up. The article reports the distribution of grades and the intervention effects for this measurement approach.

**5.3 Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?**: **N** — Moderate Certainty (80%)  
**Reason**: The analysis methods described in the Methods section, including generalized estimating equations and ordinal logistic regression, correspond to the analyses reported in the Results section. Both unadjusted and adjusted models were reported, and the article reported that complete-case and multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses were consistent with the main findings.

**Result of Domain 5**: **Low Risk** — Moderate Certainty (84%)  
**Reason**: The primary outcome, measurement method, and analysis approach were described in the Methods section and were reported consistently in the Results section. No clear evidence indicates selective reporting from multiple outcome measurements or multiple analyses.
