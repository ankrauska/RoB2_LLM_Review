## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: NI (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: The article states that participants were “randomly assigned” and that time blocks were randomized, but it does not describe a specific sequence-generation method (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, coin toss, or shuffled envelopes).

**1.2: NI (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Reason: The article reports that data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment/control conditions. However, it does not report a method for concealment of the allocation sequence before enrolment and assignment, such as central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, or identical numbered containers.

**1.3: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Reason: Baseline arousal imbalance differed significantly between groups: experimental group mean 107.4 min/12 hr versus control group mean 84.6 min/12 hr; *p* = .012. The article explicitly states that random assignment did not equalize the groups on arousal imbalance.

**Result of Domain 1: High risk (High Certainty, 97%)**  
Reason: Allocation concealment was not reported (1.2 = NI), and there was a statistically significant baseline difference in arousal imbalance between groups (1.3 = Y).

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Reason: The article states that subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions.

**2.2: Y (High Certainty, 93%)**  
Reason: Nursing staff implemented the individualized activity-schedule changes for participants in the BACE group. Therefore, carers and intervention providers would have been aware of the assigned intervention.

**2.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
Reason: The study measured therapeutic activity sessions and included this variable as a covariate. However, psychotropic medication use was not measured, and the article states that differences in medication use between groups could have affected the results. The balance of important nonprotocol interventions across groups cannot be determined.

**2.4: N (High Certainty, 91%)**  
Reason: Ten participants were dropped because they were not given the intervention. Among 102 participants who entered the study, this represents approximately 9.8%, which is not more than 10%.

**2.5: Y (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: Adherence to the prescribed BACE activity-schedule changes was incomplete for 28% of intervention participants: 22% received only 50%–75% of prescribed changes and 6% received less than 50%. Therefore, more than 10% of participants had non-adherence.

**2.6: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: The analysis was conducted among the final sample after excluding participants who did not receive the intervention. The reported repeated-measures ANCOVA was not an appropriate method to estimate the effect of adhering to the assigned intervention in the presence of non-adherence.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: Carers/intervention providers were aware of allocation (2.2 = Y), important nonprotocol interventions could not be assessed for balance (2.3 = NI), non-adherence affected more than 10% of intervention participants (2.5 = Y), and no appropriate adherence-effect analysis was used (2.6 = N).

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: N (High Certainty, 97%)**  
Reason: A total of 102 participants entered the study, but the final analyzed sample was 78 participants. Complete agitation outcome data were therefore available for approximately 76.5% (78/102) of participants, which is below 90% for this continuous outcome.

**3.2: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: The article does not report a missing-data method capable of correcting missing-data bias, such as inverse probability weighting, multiple imputation with appropriate assumptions, or a sensitivity analysis. The analysis of the final sample does not establish that missing outcome data did not bias the result.

**3.3: PY (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
Reason: Twelve participants died during the study. Death may reflect health status that could be associated with agitation, but the article does not establish whether death or other missingness was directly related to the true agitation outcome.

**3.4: Y (Moderate Certainty, 78%)**  
Reason: Missing data included deaths, exclusion due to erroneous or missing data, and exclusion of participants not given the intervention. Death and health deterioration may plausibly be associated with participants’ underlying condition and potentially with agitation outcomes. The article does not provide group-specific missingness or evidence that missingness was unrelated to agitation.

**Result of Domain 3: High risk (Moderate Certainty, 85%)**  
Reason: Outcome data were available for fewer than 90% of participants (3.1 = N), there was no evidence that the analysis corrected missing-data bias (3.2 = N), and missingness could plausibly depend on the true outcome value (3.3 = PY; 3.4 = Y).

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: Agitation was measured by direct observation using a 0–100 visual analog scale with predefined agitation-intensity parameters. Observers received training, and reported interrater reliability for agitation ranged from .95 to .984.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 93%)**  
Reason: Participants in both groups were observed using the same direct-observation procedure: 3-minute observations every 15 minutes from 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. during pretest and posttest measurement days.

**4.3: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: Agitation was assessed by research assistants rather than by participant self-report, and the article states that data collectors were blinded to treatment and control conditions.

**4.4: Y (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: Agitation is a subjective observer-assessed outcome. Therefore, outcome assessment could potentially be influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment if assessors had become aware of allocation.

**4.5: N (Moderate Certainty, 83%)**  
Reason: The article reports that data collectors were blinded to treatment assignment, and there is no clear evidence that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Reason: The outcome measurement method was reliable and predefined (4.1 = N), measurement was comparable between groups (4.2 = N), and outcome assessors were blinded (4.3 = N).

Therefore, it cannot be determined whether the Table 4 agitation result was analyzed according to a plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

**5.2: N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**

Although the original intended Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument was changed because adequate interrater reliability could not be achieved, the article reports the reason for this change and describes the visual analog scale measurement method. There is no clear evidence that the Table 4 result was selected from multiple eligible agitation measurement scales or time points on the basis of its results.

**5.3: NI (Moderate Certainty, 76%)**

Without a prespecified analysis plan, it cannot be determined whether the Table 4 analysis was selected from multiple eligible analyses on the basis of its results.

**Result of Domain 5: Some concerns (Moderate Certainty, 87%)**
