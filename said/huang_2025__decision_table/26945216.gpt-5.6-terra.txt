## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
The article explicitly states that participants were allocated using “computerized block randomization.”

**1.2: Y (Moderate Certainty, 85%)**  
The article states that the allocation group was “concealed in envelopes.” Although further envelope safeguards (e.g., opaque, sequentially numbered) were not described, allocation concealment using envelopes was explicitly reported.

**1.3: Y (High Certainty, 97%)**  
There was a statistically significant baseline difference in affected side across groups (right/left trapezius involvement; χ² = 5.952, P = 0.015). The guideline specifies that statistically significant baseline differences indicate a potential problem with the randomization process.

**Result of the domain: Some concerns (High Certainty, 93%)**  
Allocation was generated by computerized block randomization and concealed using envelopes. However, a statistically significant baseline imbalance in affected side was reported.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
The article states that “participants were blind to the allocation to treatment groups after randomization.”

**2.2: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
The principal investigator administered all treatments. The investigator necessarily knew whether TENS, IFT, or standard care alone was being delivered.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
No important nonprotocol interventions that were reported to be imbalanced between groups were identified. Standard care and the home exercise program were included in all groups.

**2.4: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
All 105 randomized participants completed the study and were included in the analysis. Therefore, there were no reported failures in implementing the allocated intervention affecting more than 10% of participants.

**2.5: NI (Moderate Certainty, 75%)**  
Participants were asked to maintain a home-exercise diary, which was checked at treatment sessions. However, the article does not report adherence rates, missed home exercises, treatment discontinuation, or crossover between groups.

**2.6: NI (High Certainty, 90%)**  
The article reports one-way ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc testing for outcome comparisons. It does not report an analysis specifically designed to estimate the effect of adherence to the assigned intervention regimen.

**Result of the domain: High risk (High Certainty, 92%)**  
Although participants were reportedly blinded and all participants completed the study, intervention providers were aware of allocation. In addition, adherence to the assigned regimen was not reported, and no appropriate adherence-effect analysis was reported.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Pain intensity at rest was measured using a 100-mm visual analog scale (VAS), anchored from “no pain at all” to “worst pain imaginable.” The article cites evidence supporting VAS reliability.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Pain intensity was measured using the same 100-mm VAS at the same prespecified time points in all three groups: baseline, immediately after the first treatment, before the eighth treatment, and one week after the eighth treatment.

**4.3: N (High Certainty, 92%)**  
Pain intensity was patient-reported, and the article states that participants were blind to treatment allocation.

**4.4: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
Pain intensity measured by a VAS is a subjective, patient-reported outcome. Therefore, its assessment could be influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

**4.5: N (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
The article reports that participants were blinded to treatment allocation. No clear evidence was reported that knowledge of intervention allocation influenced participants’ pain ratings.

**Result of the domain: Low risk (High Certainty, 90%)**  
Pain intensity was measured with a reliable VAS using the same method and time points across groups. Participants were reported to be blinded, and no clear evidence indicates that knowledge of treatment allocation influenced pain assessment.
