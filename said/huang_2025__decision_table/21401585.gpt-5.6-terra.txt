## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y (99% High Certainty)**  
Reason: Children were randomized within matched pairs using “a computer generated list of random numbers.”

**1.2: Y (97% High Certainty)**  
Reason: Allocation was concealed using concealed envelopes, which were opened by non-study personnel after matching and before assignment.

**1.3: N (96% High Certainty)**  
Reason: The article explicitly reports that there were no baseline differences between groups after matching and concealed random allocation. Baseline MUUL scores were reasonably comparable (CIMT 67.1; BIM 70.8), and the article reports no significant group differences in baseline measures.

**Result of the domain: Low risk (97% High Certainty)**  
Reason: The sequence was computer-generated, allocation was concealed, and baseline differences did not indicate a problem with the randomization process.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: Y (98% High Certainty)**  
Reason: Participants were aware of their assigned intervention. Children allocated to CIMT wore a tailor-made glove on the unimpaired limb, whereas children allocated to BIM received explicitly bimanual training. These interventions were distinguishable to participants.

**2.2: Y (99% High Certainty)**  
Reason: Therapists, physiotherapists, circus trainers, and other personnel delivering the camps necessarily knew whether they were providing CIMT or BIM training.

**2.3: Y (82% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: There were no substantial imbalanced non-protocol interventions during follow-up. Pre-existing therapy was reported, and there were no changes in therapy for participants except one CIMT participant who received upper-limb botulinum toxin A between the 3- and 26-week assessments. This single event was not sufficient to indicate an important imbalance affecting the MUUL result.

**2.4: N (94% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: Implementation was successful for most participants. One BIM participant dropped out on day 2 because of pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties. Compliance was reported as 100% for CIMT and 97% for BIM. Failures in intervention implementation did not exceed 10%.

**2.5: N (90% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: Reported compliance was 100% in the CIMT group and 97% in the BIM group. Although some children received 54 rather than 60 hours because of illness or public holidays, the article reported high compliance and no substantial non-adherence affecting more than 10% of participants.

**2.6: N (99% High Certainty)**  
Reason: The analysis was conducted on an intention-to-treat basis. Under the specified criteria, intention-to-treat analysis is not an appropriate method for estimating the effect of adhering to the assigned intervention regimen.

**Result of the domain: Low risk (88% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: Participants and intervention providers were aware of allocation, but there were no important imbalanced non-protocol interventions, failures of implementation were below 10%, and adherence was high. According to the specified decision rules, this results in low risk.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: Y (82% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: Sixty-four children were randomized. The baseline table includes 32 CIMT and 31 BIM participants (63 participants), and the trial reports outcome results for both groups at follow-up. Thus, outcome data were available for at least 63/64 randomized participants (98.4%), exceeding the 95% threshold for this continuous MUUL outcome.

**3.2: N (98% High Certainty)**  
Reason: The article did not report inverse probability weighting, a missing-data sensitivity analysis, or another method specifically demonstrating that missing outcome data did not bias the MUUL result. Intention-to-treat analysis alone does not establish protection from bias due to missing outcome data.

**3.3: N (78% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: The reported BIM dropout occurred because of pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties rather than deterioration in upper-limb movement quality or a reported MUUL-related outcome problem.

**3.4: N (75% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: The reported reason for dropout does not indicate that missing MUUL data depended on the true value of the MUUL outcome. There is no reported differential outcome-related missingness between intervention groups.

**Result of the domain: Low risk (82% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: Complete MUUL outcome data were available for nearly all randomized participants (approximately 98.4%). Therefore, despite no specific missing-data bias-correction analysis, the domain is assessed as low risk according to the prespecified criteria.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (98% High Certainty)**  
Reason: MUUL was a predefined primary outcome and was measured using the Melbourne Assessment of Unilateral Upper Limb Function. The study assessed its reliability in this sample using repeated scoring of video-recorded assessments. The intraclass correlation coefficient for total MUUL percentage score was 0.93 (95% CI 0.84–0.97), supporting reliable measurement.

**4.2: N (95% High Certainty)**  
Reason: MUUL assessments were conducted in both groups at the same scheduled time points: baseline, 3 weeks, and 26 weeks. The same outcome measurement method was used for both intervention groups.

**4.3: N (99% High Certainty)**  
Reason: Although the therapists who conducted assessments were aware of allocation, the primary MUUL assessments were video-recorded and scored in random order by trained occupational therapists masked to group allocation. Therefore, the outcome assessors responsible for MUUL scoring were blinded.

**4.4: Y (96% High Certainty)**  
Reason: MUUL is an assessor-rated measure of quality of upper-limb movement. Such assessment is subjective and could potentially be influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment.

**4.5: N (96% High Certainty)**  
Reason: There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention status influenced MUUL assessment because trained occupational therapists scored the video-recorded MUUL assessments in random order while masked to group allocation.

**Result of the domain: Low risk (97% High Certainty)**  
Reason: MUUL was a reliable predefined measure, was measured comparably between groups, and was scored by outcome assessors masked to treatment allocation. Although the outcome is assessor-rated, blinding makes influence from knowledge of intervention unlikely.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1: Y (84% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: The trial had a published protocol before publication of this report (the INCITE protocol, BMC Neurology 2010). MUUL was specified as a primary outcome, assessed at baseline, 3 weeks, and 26 weeks. The reported MUUL results and generalized estimating equation analysis correspond to the stated methods.

**5.2: N (91% High Certainty)**  
Reason: The MUUL was consistently reported as the predefined measure of quality of movement of the impaired upper limb. Results were presented for the intended assessment time points, including baseline, 3 weeks, and 26 weeks. There is no clear evidence that the reported MUUL result was selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements.

**5.3: N (87% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: The Methods section specified regression modelling using generalized estimating equations for repeated measurements, adjusted for matching characteristics. The Results section reports results from this planned GEE analysis. There is no clear evidence that only one favourable analysis was selectively reported from multiple eligible analyses.

**Result of the domain: Low Risk (87% Moderate Certainty)**  
Reason: MUUL was prespecified as a primary outcome, reported at the planned time points, and analysed using the stated generalized estimating equation approach. There is no evidence of selective outcome-measurement or analysis reporting.
