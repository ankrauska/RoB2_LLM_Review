## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1 Was the allocation sequence adequately generated?**: NI (Moderate Certainty, 85%)  
**Reason:** The article states that randomization was carried out confidentially by clinic administration and was arranged for active drug and placebo allocation. However, it does not report a random-sequence generation method, such as computer-generated random numbers, random-number tables, block randomization, or similar methods.

**1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**: PY (Moderate Certainty, 80%)  
**Reason:** Medication was supplied in numbered boxes, matching placebo was used, and both participants and clinicians were blinded. These features indicate allocation masking/concealment, but the article does not provide sufficient details to establish a specific robust concealment method such as centrally controlled allocation or sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed containers.

**1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**: N (High Certainty, 95%)  
**Reason:** The article reports that both groups were comparable at randomization regarding initial body weight, height, weight gain during olanzapine treatment, duration and dose of olanzapine treatment, and psychiatric diagnosis. Table 1 does not show substantial baseline imbalances likely to indicate a randomization problem.

**Result of Domain 1: Low risk (Moderate Certainty, 83%)**  
**Reason:** Allocation concealment was probably present, baseline characteristics were comparable, and there is no evidence that the randomization process caused important baseline imbalance. However, the sequence-generation method was not reported.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?**: N (High Certainty, 98%)  
**Reason:** The study was described as double-blind, and participants received either topiramate or a matching placebo. The focus of the study was revealed only after collection of final data.

**2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants' assigned intervention during the trial?**: N (High Certainty, 98%)  
**Reason:** The article explicitly states that clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment.

**2.3 Were important nonprotocol interventions balanced across intervention groups?**: Y (Moderate Certainty, 85%)  
**Reason:** Olanzapine doses and concomitant medications remained constant during the study. No important nonprotocol interventions that were imbalanced between groups and prognostic for weight were reported.

**2.4 Were there failures in implementing the intervention that could have affected the outcome?**: Y (High Certainty, 98%)  
**Reason:** Six of 49 randomized participants dropped out because they failed to attend more than one evaluation. This is 12.2% of randomized participants, exceeding the 10% threshold. All six dropouts were in the placebo group.

**2.5 Was there non-adherence to the assigned intervention regimen that could have affected participants’ outcomes?**: NI (Moderate Certainty, 80%)  
**Reason:** The article does not report medication adherence, treatment compliance, treatment discontinuation other than dropout, crossover, or switching between topiramate and placebo.

**2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of adhering to the intervention?**: N (High Certainty, 95%)  
**Reason:** Data from the six participants who dropped out were not further analyzed; only 43 completers were evaluated. This is a completer/per-protocol-type analysis and is not an appropriate method for estimating the effect of adherence in the presence of substantial dropout.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** More than 10% of randomized participants failed to complete the intervention/assessments, all dropouts occurred in the placebo group, adherence information was not reported, and the analysis excluded the dropouts.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**: N (High Certainty, 99%)  
**Reason:** Weight data were analyzed for 43 of 49 randomized women. The proportion with complete outcome data was 43/49 = 87.8%, which is below 90% for this continuous outcome.

**3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**: N (High Certainty, 95%)  
**Reason:** The article excluded the six participants with missing follow-up data from the analysis. It did not report an inverse-probability-weighted analysis, multiple-imputation approach designed to address missingness, sensitivity analysis, or other evidence demonstrating that missing data did not bias the weight result.

**3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**: NI (Moderate Certainty, 75%)  
**Reason:** The reported reason for missing data was failure to attend more than one evaluation. The article does not explain whether nonattendance was related to body weight, weight change, health status, treatment response, or another outcome-related factor.

**3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**: NI (Moderate Certainty, 70%)  
**Reason:** All six missing outcome cases occurred in the placebo group, but the article does not report the participants’ characteristics, weight trajectories, or reasons for nonattendance in sufficient detail to determine whether missingness was related to true weight outcomes.

**Result of Domain 3: High risk (High Certainty, 93%)**  
**Reason:** Complete weight data were available for only 87.8% of randomized participants, missing data were not addressed with an appropriate bias-correction or sensitivity analysis, and all missing cases were from the placebo group.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**: N (High Certainty, 98%)  
**Reason:** Weight was measured by direct weighing every two weeks. Body weight in kilograms is an appropriate and reliable objective measurement for the outcome of weight.

**4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**: N (High Certainty, 95%)  
**Reason:** Participants in both groups attended assessments every two weeks and were weighed during these visits. The same measurement schedule and method were used for both intervention groups.

**4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**: N (High Certainty, 98%)  
**Reason:** Weight was not patient-reported, and the article states that clinicians were blinded to topiramate/placebo assignment. Therefore, the personnel involved in outcome assessment were not aware of intervention allocation.

**4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**: N (High Certainty, 99%)  
**Reason:** Weight is an objective outcome measured in kilograms. Knowledge of intervention allocation would not reasonably influence the measured body weight.

**4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**: N (High Certainty, 98%)  
**Reason:** There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention status influenced weight assessment. In addition, clinicians were blinded and weight is an objective measurement.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** Weight was objectively measured, both groups were assessed using the same schedule and method, and clinicians were blinded to group assignment.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available for analysis?**: NI (High Certainty, 95%)  
**Reason:** No trial registration number, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospectively finalized prespecified analysis plan was reported. Therefore, it cannot be verified whether the reported weight analysis followed an analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

**5.2 Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?**: N (Moderate Certainty, 85%)  
**Reason:** Weight was measured every two weeks, and the article reports the course of body weight over time in Figure 1 as well as the final weight outcome. There is no clear evidence that only selectively favorable weight time points or measures were reported.

**5.3 Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?**: N (Moderate Certainty, 85%)  
**Reason:** The Methods section specifies use of the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Results section reports weight results using the stated approach. There is no clear evidence that multiple alternative analyses were conducted and only favorable analyses were reported.

**Result of Domain 5: Some concerns (High Certainty, 90%)**  
**Reason:** The study did not report registration, a protocol, or a prespecified analysis plan. Although there is no clear evidence of selective choice among multiple weight measurements or analyses, selective reporting cannot be excluded.
