**Article ID**: Togha, 2010  
**Outcome Name**: attack number  
**registration number**: NCT00668343  

## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
Reason: The article states that “a computer-based blocking method (four blocks) was used for the randomization.” This is an adequate random-sequence generation method.

**1.2: PY (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
Reason: The study reports that patients were unaware of group assignment and that all medications were given in similar packages every 3 months. This indicates masking of allocation, but the specific allocation-concealment procedure (e.g., sequentially numbered opaque envelopes or central allocation) was not reported.

**1.3: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Reason: Baseline characteristics were reported for both groups, and Table 1 states that there were no statistically significant baseline differences between groups. No baseline imbalance suggests a problem with randomization.

**Result of Domain 1: Low risk (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: Randomization was computer-based, allocation was masked through similar medication packages and participant blinding, and baseline characteristics did not indicate a problem with the randomization process.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Reason: The article explicitly states that patients were unaware of their group assignment. Similar packages were used for simvastatin and placebo.

**2.2: N (Moderate Certainty, 88%)**  
Reason: The trial registry describes quadruple masking, including care providers. The article also reports that neurologists undertaking follow-up examinations were blinded to treatment assignment.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
Reason: No important imbalanced nonprotocol interventions relevant to attack number were reported. Relapses were treated with intravenous methylprednisolone according to the study procedure in both groups.

**2.4: Y (High Certainty, 97%)**  
Reason: Fourteen of 85 randomized participants withdrew from the study (16.5%), exceeding 10%. Withdrawals included refusal to cooperate, two relapses, chronic exacerbation, and personal reasons. These failures in implementing the assigned intervention could have affected attack-number outcomes.

**2.5: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Reason: More than 10% of randomized participants withdrew and therefore discontinued the assigned study intervention before the end of the 12-month treatment period. Although the article states that participants took medication consistently until withdrawal, withdrawal resulted in cessation of the assigned regimen.

**2.6: N (High Certainty, 99%)**  
Reason: The article reports intention-to-treat analysis, per-protocol analysis, and last-observation-carried-forward analysis. Under the specified criteria, these are inappropriate methods for estimating the effect of adherence to a sustained intervention.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: More than 10% of participants withdrew or discontinued the assigned intervention, and the analyses used to address these deviations included ITT, per-protocol, and LOCF methods rather than appropriate methods for estimating the effect of adherence.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: Attack-number results were reported for 40 participants in the simvastatin group and 36 participants in the placebo group, for a total of 76/85 randomized participants (89.4%). Attack number is a continuous/count outcome, and less than 90% of randomized participants had observed outcome data.

**3.2: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Reason: The article used per-protocol analysis and LOCF to address dropouts. LOCF is not an appropriate method to correct bias caused by missing data. No inverse-probability weighting or sensitivity analysis demonstrating that missing data did not bias attack-number results was reported.

**3.3: Y (High Certainty, 97%)**  
Reason: Several participants withdrew because they had two relapses or disease exacerbation. These reasons are directly related to the outcome of attack number; therefore, missingness could depend on the true outcome value.

**3.4: Y (High Certainty, 95%)**  
Reason: Withdrawal due to attacks or chronic exacerbation was more frequent in the placebo group (four participants with two relapses and one with chronic exacerbation) than in the simvastatin group (two participants with two relapses). The reasons for missing data indicate a relationship with attack activity and differed between groups.

**Result of Domain 3: High risk (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: Less than 90% of randomized participants contributed observed attack-number data, the methods used to address missing data did not correct potential bias, and missingness was likely related to relapse activity.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: Relapse was predefined as a new or worsening neurological deficit without high body temperature, lasting more than 24 hours after at least one month of stability. This is an appropriate and clinically established method for measuring attacks in relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis.

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: Participants in both groups were evaluated every three months by neurologists using the same relapse definition and follow-up schedule.

**4.3: N (High Certainty, 97%)**  
Reason: Four neurologists conducted follow-up examinations and were blinded to treatment assignment. Therefore, outcome assessors were not aware of the intervention received.

**4.4: Y (Moderate Certainty, 79%)**  
Reason: Determination of relapse involves clinical neurological assessment of new or worsening deficits and may include some clinical judgment. Therefore, the assessment could potentially be influenced by knowledge of treatment received if assessors were unblinded.

**4.5: N (High Certainty, 93%)**  
Reason: The neurologists assessing relapses were blinded to treatment allocation. There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced assessment of attack number.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (High Certainty, 94%)**  
Reason: Attack number was measured with a predefined clinical relapse definition, using comparable assessment procedures in both groups, and outcome assessors were blinded.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1: Y (Moderate Certainty, 81%)**  
Reason: The trial was registered as NCT00668343. The registry specified attack number at one year as the primary outcome. The article prespecified number of relapses during the one-year follow-up as the main outcome and reported this outcome using the stated Mann–Whitney U-test. The reported primary attack-number result was consistent with the planned outcome.

**5.2: N (High Certainty, 91%)**  
Reason: The primary outcome was total attack number during the one-year follow-up. The article reported this outcome directly. Additional attack-number analyses, including per-protocol and LOCF analyses for three-month periods, were also reported rather than selectively omitted.

**5.3: N (High Certainty, 89%)**  
Reason: The Methods section stated that intention-to-treat analysis would be used and that per-protocol and LOCF analyses would be conducted to address dropouts. The Results section reported the primary intention-to-treat analysis as well as the per-protocol and LOCF analyses. There is no clear evidence that only a favorable analysis was selectively reported.

**Result of Domain 5: Low risk (Moderate Certainty, 84%)**  
Reason: The registered primary outcome was attack number at one year, and the article reported the prespecified attack-number outcome and the planned supplementary analyses.
