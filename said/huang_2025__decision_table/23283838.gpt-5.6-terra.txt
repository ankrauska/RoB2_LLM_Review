**Article ID**: Rodebaugh, 2013  
**Outcome Name**: between-session habituation  
**registration number**: Not reported  

## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1: NI (High Certainty, 97%)**  
**Reason:** Participants were described as “randomly assigned” to DCS or placebo, but the article did not report the method used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, block randomization, etc.).

**1.2: PY (Moderate Certainty, 85%)**  
**Reason:** DCS and placebo were administered in identical-appearing capsules, and allocation was managed by a data manager who had no interaction with participants. Participants and experimenters were unable to correctly guess assignment. However, the article did not explicitly describe a specific allocation-concealment procedure such as central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, or sequentially numbered drug containers.

**1.3: Y (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** Groups differed significantly in age at baseline (p = .019): the DCS group had a mean age of approximately 49 years and the placebo group approximately 38 years. This statistically significant baseline imbalance suggests a possible problem with the randomization process, although the authors examined age as a covariate.

**Result of Domain 1: Some concerns (High Certainty, 93%)**  
**Reason:** Allocation concealment was only probably adequate rather than clearly documented, and there was a statistically significant baseline age imbalance between treatment groups.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions

**2.1: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** Participants received identical-appearing DCS or placebo capsules. The assessment of blinding showed that participants could not discern their treatment assignment (ps > .15).

**2.2: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** Experimenters were unable to discern participants’ treatment assignment (ps > .15). The identical-appearing placebo capsules supported blinding of intervention providers/experimenters.

**2.3: Y (Moderate Certainty, 78%)**  
**Reason:** The article did not report important nonprotocol interventions that differed between groups. The exposure procedure was individualized according to protocol to achieve clinically significant fear, and no evidence indicated imbalance in additional interventions between DCS and placebo groups.

**2.4: Y (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** Four of 34 randomized participants did not return for Visit 2 and were excluded from all analyses (11.8%). This exceeds the prespecified threshold of more than 10% with failures in implementing the intervention/study procedures.

**2.5: NI (High Certainty, 92%)**  
**Reason:** The article stated that participants ingested their assigned pill before the exposure task, but it did not provide adherence data or explicitly report whether all participants fully adhered to their assigned intervention regimen.

**2.6: N (High Certainty, 99%)**  
**Reason:** The analysis included only participants who completed Visit 2. The authors explicitly stated that participants who dropped out were not included in any analyses. This is a completer-only/per-protocol analysis and is not an appropriate method to estimate the effect of adhering to the intervention when there are implementation failures or missing follow-up data.

**Result of Domain 2: High risk (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** More than 10% of randomized participants did not complete Visit 2 and were excluded from analysis. The study used a completer-only analysis rather than an appropriate method to address deviations, non-adherence, or exclusions.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1: Y (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** The primary outcome, between-session habituation, was binary (responder versus nonresponder). Four of 34 randomized participants had missing Visit-2 outcome data. There were 17 observed habituation events overall (12 DCS participants and 5 placebo participants), which was much greater than the four participants with missing outcomes.

**3.2: N (High Certainty, 98%)**  
**Reason:** The study did not use an analysis method designed to correct bias due to missing outcome data, such as inverse probability weighting or an appropriate sensitivity analysis. The completer-only analysis does not establish that results were not biased by missing outcome data.

**3.3: N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
**Reason:** Two participants became unreachable because of a changed or lost phone number, and two participants stated that they preferred not to continue. The reported reasons were not explicitly related to their true between-session habituation outcome.

**3.4: N (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
**Reason:** Missing outcome data were balanced between groups (two DCS and two placebo participants), and the reported reasons for missingness were similar across groups. The article did not provide evidence that missingness was likely dependent on the true habituation outcome.

**Result of Domain 3: Low risk (High Certainty, 91%)**  
**Reason:** For the binary primary outcome, observed events substantially exceeded the number of missing outcomes. Furthermore, missing data were balanced between groups and the stated reasons for missingness were not outcome-related.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1: N (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** Between-session habituation was measured using the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS), scored from 0 to 100. The measure was predefined, repeatedly assessed during each speech, and showed excellent internal consistency when ratings were averaged (Cronbach’s α > .85).

**4.2: N (High Certainty, 95%)**  
**Reason:** Both DCS and placebo groups completed the same speech-exposure procedure and the same SUDS assessment schedule at Visit 1 and Visit 2. The outcome was measured using the same scale and comparable time points in both groups.

**4.3: N (High Certainty, 94%)**  
**Reason:** The outcome was participant-reported SUDS distress. Participants received matching placebo or DCS capsules and were unable to discern treatment assignment according to the blinding assessment.

**4.4: Y (High Certainty, 97%)**  
**Reason:** Between-session habituation was based on subjective anxiety ratings reported by participants using the SUDS. Subjective participant-reported outcomes could potentially be influenced by knowledge or beliefs concerning intervention assignment.

**4.5: N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
**Reason:** There was no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced participants’ SUDS ratings. Participants could not accurately identify their treatment assignment, and identical-appearing placebo capsules were used.

**Result of Domain 4: Low risk (High Certainty, 92%)**  
**Reason:** The SUDS was a predefined and reliable outcome measure, measured comparably between groups. Participants were blinded to allocation, and no clear evidence indicated that knowledge of assignment likely influenced outcome assessment.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1: NI (High Certainty, 96%)**  
**Reason:** The article described the planned measurement and analysis of between-session habituation in the Methods section and reported the corresponding result. However, no trial registration number, protocol, or prespecified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available was reported.

**5.2: N (Moderate Certainty, 84%)**  
**Reason:** Between-session habituation was prespecified as a categorical outcome: a reduction of at least 5 points in average SUDS across visits. The article reported this prespecified measurement for the primary outcome. There was no clear evidence that the reported binary habituation result was selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements on the basis of results.

**5.3: N (Moderate Certainty, 83%)**  
**Reason:** The Methods prespecified chi-square analysis for categorical response to DCS, and the Results reported the categorical between-session habituation comparison using a chi-square test. There was no clear evidence that the reported numerical result was selected from multiple eligible analyses based on the results.

**Result of Domain 5: Some concerns (High Certainty, 92%)**  
**Reason:** Although the reported primary outcome measurement and analysis were consistent with the Methods section, no registered protocol or prespecified analysis plan was reported.
