## Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1 Was the allocation sequence adequately generated?**: **Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
Reason: The article states that “a random number table was produced through Microsoft Excel” and that patients were divided into groups following the random number table.

**1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**: **NI (High Certainty, 98%)**  
Reason: Although randomization using a random number table was reported, the article did not describe allocation concealment methods such as central allocation, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, or identical numbered containers.

**1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**: **N (High Certainty, 97%)**  
Reason: The article reports no statistically significant differences between groups in sex, age, active esophageal bleeding, red signs, Child–Pugh score, portal-vein width, number and degree of esophageal varices, or gastroesophageal-varix classification.

**Result of Domain 1**: **Some concerns (High Certainty, 97%)**  
Reason: Allocation sequence generation was adequate and baseline characteristics were balanced, but allocation concealment was not reported.

## Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention)

**2.1 Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?**: **Y (Moderate Certainty, 85%)**  
Reason: The two interventions differed materially: the therapy group underwent embolization plus EVL at the same time, whereas the control group underwent embolization followed by EVL two months later. Participants would likely have been aware of whether they underwent immediate EVL.

**2.2 Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial?**: **Y (High Certainty, 99%)**  
Reason: Clinicians delivering the procedures necessarily knew whether EVL was performed at the same time as embolization or scheduled two months later.

**2.3 Were important nonprotocol interventions balanced across intervention groups?**: **Y (Moderate Certainty, 80%)**  
Reason: Somatostatin was administered before and after the operation in both groups. The article does not report other important nonprotocol co-interventions before assessment of emergency hemostasis that were imbalanced between groups.

**2.4 Were there failures in implementing the intervention that could have affected the outcome?**: **Y (High Certainty, 96%)**  
Reason: In the control group, hemorrhage was not controlled in 10 of 82 patients (12.2%) after the initially assigned embolization approach, and these patients received EVL earlier than the planned two-month schedule. This exceeds 10% of randomized participants and represents a failure to implement the planned intervention regimen as intended.

**2.5 Was there non-adherence to the assigned intervention regimen that could have affected participants’ outcomes?**: **N (Moderate Certainty, 78%)**  
Reason: The article does not report participant refusal, treatment cessation, crossover initiated by participants, or other participant non-adherence. The early EVL in 10 control participants was rescue management for uncontrolled bleeding rather than reported participant non-adherence.

**2.6 Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of adhering to the intervention?**: **N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
Reason: The emergency-hemostasis analysis compared outcomes according to randomized groups, including all 90 therapy-group and 82 control-group participants. This is an intention-to-treat-type analysis and is not an appropriate method for estimating the effect of adhering to the intervention when implementation failures occurred.

**Result of Domain 2**: **High risk (High Certainty, 92%)**  
Reason: Participants and intervention providers were aware of assigned interventions. More than 10% of control participants had implementation failures requiring early rescue EVL, and the analysis did not appropriately estimate the effect of adherence to the assigned regimen.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**: **N (Moderate Certainty, 84%)**  
Reason: Emergency hemostasis was assessed by whether hemorrhaging was controlled after treatment. This is a clinically appropriate and objective outcome measure for acute variceal bleeding.

**4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**: **N (Moderate Certainty, 82%)**  
Reason: Emergency hemostasis was assessed in both groups using the same clinical criterion of whether hemorrhaging was controlled following the assigned treatment approach.

**4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**: **NI (Moderate Certainty, 75%)**  
Reason: The article did not state whether the personnel assessing whether hemorrhage was controlled were blinded to intervention assignment.

**4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**: **N (High Certainty, 93%)**  
Reason: Emergency hemostasis—whether active bleeding was controlled—is an objective clinical/endoscopic outcome and is unlikely to be materially influenced by assessor knowledge of treatment assignment.

**4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**: **N (High Certainty, 92%)**  
Reason: There is no clear evidence that knowledge of intervention assignment was likely to influence assessment of whether bleeding had stopped.

**Result of Domain 4**: **Low risk (High Certainty, 91%)**  
Reason: The outcome was measured using an appropriate and comparable objective clinical assessment. Although assessor blinding was not reported, assessment was unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention status.
