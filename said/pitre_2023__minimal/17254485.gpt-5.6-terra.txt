1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because a random-number-table method was reported, but allocation concealment was not described. Baseline characteristics do not clearly indicate a failure of randomization.  
   b. **Rationale:** The authors state that “*A random number table was produced through Microsoft Excel. The patients were divided into a control group and a therapy group following the random number table.*” This supports a random sequence-generation process. However, the report gives no information on who generated the allocation sequence, whether assignment was centrally controlled, or whether recruiters could foresee the upcoming allocation. Thus, concealment until enrolment and assignment cannot be confirmed.  
   
   The groups were similar in reported baseline characteristics, including sex, age, Child–Pugh score, portal-vein width, active oesophageal bleeding, red signs, and gastroesophageal-varix classification. Although there are numerical differences in distributions of variceal grades, the reported overall comparison was not statistically significant and the available information does not demonstrate an imbalance sufficiently extreme to indicate a problem with randomization. Nevertheless, the absence of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.  
   b. **Rationale:** The intervention strategies were clearly differentiated: the therapy group received gastric-fundus-varix embolization plus EVL during the same procedure, whereas the control group received embolization initially and planned EVL two months later. The study was necessarily open-label to participants and clinical personnel because of the differing procedures and timing. However, there is no evidence that deviations from the assigned strategies arose because of the trial context and were differentially applied between groups before assessment of emergency haemostasis.  
   
   Both groups received somatostatin: “*Somatostatin was administered before and after the operation*” in the therapy group, and “*Somatostatin was also administered before and after the operation*” in the control group. Rescue EVL was given to 10 control-group patients whose haemorrhage was not controlled. This occurred after failure of the initial assigned treatment and does not appear to have changed their classification for the emergency-haemostasis outcome: only 72/82 control participants were counted as having emergency haemostasis.  
   
   The analysis retained all randomized participants in the denominators for this outcome (90 therapy and 82 control participants). Therefore, the reported result corresponds to an intention-to-treat comparison of assignment to the two management strategies, rather than an as-treated or per-protocol analysis.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Emergency-haemostasis data appear available for every randomized participant. The report states that bleeding was controlled in “*all 90 patients in the therapy group*” and in “*72 out of 82 patients*” in the control group. Thus, the reported event counts account for all 172 randomized participants.  
   
   There is no indication of loss to follow-up, withdrawal, missing endoscopic assessment, or exclusion from the emergency-haemostasis analysis. Since this is an acute, in-hospital outcome and all randomized participants appear to have been classified as having haemostasis or not, missing outcome data could not plausibly have materially affected the estimated effect.

4. **measurement of the outcome: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** Emergency haemostasis is a clinically important and relatively objective acute outcome, apparently based on whether active variceal bleeding was controlled after endoscopic treatment. The article illustrates this outcome endoscopically: “*Active gastric fundus variceal bleeding was controlled after embolization with Histoacryl*” and “*active esophageal varicel bleeding was controlled after EVL*.”  
   
   Nevertheless, no explicit, pre-specified operational definition of “emergency hemostasis” is provided, such as a fixed assessment time, required endoscopic criteria, or a standardized clinical criterion. Also, outcome assessors were almost certainly aware of the allocated intervention because the procedures differed visibly and temporally, and no blinded outcome adjudication is reported. Knowledge of treatment assignment could potentially influence judgments about whether bleeding had been controlled, particularly where bleeding was intermittent or required a clinical decision regarding further intervention.  
   
   There is no direct evidence that outcome assessment was actually influenced by that knowledge, and the visual presence or absence of active bleeding limits the likely magnitude of bias. However, the absence of blinded assessment and standardized outcome-assessment details results in **some concerns**.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The publication reports emergency haemostasis as a study outcome and provides complete group-level numerical results (90/90 versus 72/82). However, no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is reported in the article. The available materials do not provide a trial-registration record or protocol with sufficiently detailed, date-stamped pre-specified outcome definitions, time points, and analyses to verify that the emergency-haemostasis result was analysed according to plans finalized before unblinded data were available.  
   
   The report does not provide clear evidence that the emergency-haemostasis result was selected from multiple measurements or analyses on the basis of favourability. However, emergency haemostasis could potentially be defined using different time windows or criteria, and the article does not establish that only one eligible definition or analysis was used. Therefore, in accordance with RoB 2, there are **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.
