1. Bias due to randomization: Low risk of bias
a. Judgement: The trial used adequate random sequence generation and allocation concealment, and baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups.
b. Rationale: "Once matched, children were randomized to pairs using a computer generated list of random numbers and concealed envelopes opened by non-study personnel." Table I shows balanced baseline characteristics (e.g., MUUL 67.1 vs 70.8, AHA 61.7 vs 60.8), with differences compatible with chance. The authors state: "After matching and concealed random allocation within pairs, there were no differences between groups on demographic or baseline measures." A matched-pairs design was also used to further reduce baseline imbalance risk. All signalling questions indicate low risk: random sequence (Yes), concealment (Yes), and no problematic baseline imbalance (No).

2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): Some concerns
a. Judgement: Participants, carers, and treatment providers could not be blinded to intervention (CIMT vs BIM), given the nature of the interventions (wearing a constraint glove is not concealable). However, there is little evidence that deviations from intended intervention arose specifically because of the trial context (e.g., recruitment/engagement effects) rather than from the nature of delivering two distinct rehabilitation approaches, and an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis was used.
b. Rationale: "Participants in the CIMT group wore a tailor-made glove on their unimpaired limb while attending the day camp" — clearly unblindable to participants, families, and therapists delivering intervention ("assessments...conducted...by four experienced occupational therapists and physiotherapists who were aware of group allocation" applies to assessors, but similarly camp staff/deliverers knew group assignment). This awareness could theoretically influence enthusiasm, compliance push, or ancillary encouragement differentially between groups (e.g., "Motivation to maintain continual bimanual use was more challenging in the BIM training group"), suggesting some risk that unblinded delivery affected engagement/adherence in a manner tied to trial context. However, there is no clear evidence of non-protocol interventions or systematic implementation failures reported; compliance was high in both groups (100% CIMT, 97% BIM), and dropout was minimal and unrelated to intervention preference (one BIM dropout was due to "pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties"). Analysis was intention-to-treat: "Analyses were on an intention-to-treat basis according to CONSORT guidelines," supporting appropriate handling of the assignment effect. Given lack of blinding of participants/personnel plus some indication (differential motivation) that trial context could subtly influence delivery/adherence, but no strong evidence of resulting bias affecting the outcome, this is best judged as "Some concerns" rather than high risk.

3. Missing outcome data: Low risk of bias
a. Judgement: Outcome data were available for nearly all randomized participants at both follow-up points, with only one dropout, and analysis was intention-to-treat.
b. Rationale: "Sixty-four children (32 matched pairs) were randomly assigned to treatment and 62 children received their allocated intervention. There was one dropout on day two of the camp from the BIM training group, because of pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties." The CONSORT flow diagram (Figure 1) confirms very limited attrition through 3- and 26-week follow-up. The reason for the single dropout (pre-existing emotional/behavioural difficulties, occurring at day 2 before intervention effects could be assessed) is unrelated to the true value of the outcome or to intervention received. "Analyses were on an intention-to-treat basis according to CONSORT guidelines" further supports low risk.

4. Measurement of the outcome: Low risk of bias
a. Judgement: The outcome (MUUL) was measured using an appropriate, validated instrument, in a comparable manner between groups, by assessors who were blinded to group allocation.
b. Rationale: "The primary outcome measures were videotaped and scored in random order by trained occupational therapists masked to group allocation." The MUUL was demonstrated to have good reliability for this study: "The ICC for the total percentage score of MUUL was 0.93 (95% CI 0.84–0.97)." Because outcome assessors (video-raters) were blinded and the outcome involves some judgement (movement quality rating), blinding of assessors removes the primary concern about differential/biased assessment. There is no indication that measurement or ascertainment methods differed between groups (same videotaped assessment protocol applied identically to both arms).

5. Selective reporting: Low risk of bias
a. Judgement: The outcome (MUUL) and its analysis appear consistent with the pre-specified trial protocol/registration, with no evidence of selective reporting from multiple outcome measures or multiple analyses.
b. Rationale: The trial protocol was published in advance (Boyd R, Sakzewski L, Ziviani J, et al. "INCITE: a randomised trial comparing constraint induced movement therapy and bimanual training in children with congenital hemiplegia." BMC Neurol 2010), pre-specifying MUUL as a primary activity outcome measuring "quality of movement of the impaired upper limb." The published trial report presents MUUL results fully at all three time points (baseline, 3, and 26 weeks) using the pre-specified GEE analysis approach ("Continuous data were compared between groups by fitting a regression model using generalized estimating equations (GEE)..."), consistent with a single, pre-specified analytic approach rather than a selected subset of possible analyses or measurement instruments. No evidence of selective outcome or analysis reporting was found based on the trial registration information provided.
