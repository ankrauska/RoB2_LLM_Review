1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** This was a within-participant randomized trial: each participant received both preparations, allocated to opposite cubital fossae. The allocation sequence was generated using an appropriate random method: “*randomly permuted blocks of fixed size using validated software RANCODE 3.6*.” The randomization schedule was prepared by a statistics/data-management department, rather than by those delivering or assessing treatment. Products were coded A and B, and “*blinding codes were kept sealed until closure of final database*”; unblinding occurred only after database closure. These features support both random sequence generation and concealment.

   Baseline imbalances between intervention conditions are inherently unlikely to bias this paired design because the same 35 participants received both products. No indication is given that allocation of products to left/right fossae was manipulated or that there was an implausible imbalance in allocations.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, for the effect of assignment to interventions at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial is described as “*double-blind*.” The two formulations were coded A and B, and all applications were performed by a study nurse according to a standardized schedule and dose: “*An aliquot of 0.7 ml of the coded test material was applied and gently massaged… This procedure was repeated once per application*.” Thus, participants and personnel delivering the products were unlikely to know which formulation contained emollients.

   There is no evidence of differential co-interventions, differential application procedures, or other deviations arising because of the trial context that could plausibly affect erythema or dryness differently between products. Although three participants had protocol violations and did not complete the per-protocol course, the primary reported analysis was explicitly based on the ITT population: “*Results are presented for the intention-to-treat (ITT) population*,” and all 35 randomized participants were analysed. The use of LOCF for missing observations is considered under missing outcome data rather than this domain.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not fully observed for all randomized participants. The report states that three participants were excluded during follow-up because of protocol violations—one received anti-inflammatory medication and two disinfected the treated cubital fossae—and that “*Missing values in 3 participants were replaced by the method of Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF).*”

   Three of 35 participants (8.6%) had missing data. This is not clearly negligible for this small trial, particularly because the primary outcome was a cumulative score across repeated assessments and the observed mean scores were very low. The reasons for missingness/protocol exclusion were plausibly related to skin outcomes: anti-inflammatory medication could reduce erythema or dryness, while additional disinfection could worsen irritation. Therefore, it is likely that missingness was related to the true unobserved outcome values.

   LOCF does not adequately address this concern, because it assumes that the participant’s subsequent erythema/dryness scores would remain unchanged after the last observed assessment. No sensitivity analysis was reported to demonstrate that the treatment comparison was robust to plausible alternative values for the missing observations. Consequently, the missing data could materially affect the estimated difference between formulations.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Erythema and dryness were measured using explicitly described, ordered visual scales. For erythema, the scale ranged from “*0 = none*” to “*4 = fiery red with oedema*”; for dryness, it ranged from “*0 = none*” to “*3 = severe dryness with large flakes*.” These are suitable measures for the intended clinical manifestations of irritant contact dermatitis.

   The outcome assessor was a dermatologist, and the report states: “*All evaluations were conducted blindly by the same investigator.*” The preparations were coded and the blinding code remained sealed until final database closure. Therefore, the assessor was unlikely to know which cubital fossa had received the emollient-containing formulation.

   Although visual assessments of erythema and dryness involve clinical judgement and could be influenced by knowledge of treatment allocation, the reported assessor blinding substantially protects against differential outcome measurement. Assessments were also made at the same scheduled time points for both treatment sites within each participant. There is no indication that ascertainment differed systematically between the two intervention conditions.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication clearly identifies a primary outcome and analysis: “*The sum score of dryness and erythema over all assessment times served as the primary outcome parameter*.” It also describes the planned non-inferiority analysis and the planned possibility of testing superiority after non-inferiority: “*In case of proven non-inferiority, switching to superiority as an additional end-point was considered*.” The article reports the primary ITT result, individual erythema and dryness sum scores, and per-protocol results.

   However, no dated protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial-registration record containing sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized outcome-measurement and analysis specifications was available in the supplied materials. This is relevant because erythema and dryness were assessed repeatedly at numerous time points, were reported both separately and as a combined score, and could potentially be analysed in several ways (e.g., individual time points, total scores, ITT versus per-protocol populations, or different approaches to missing data).

   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected on the basis of its statistical significance or direction. Nevertheless, without a prospectively finalized analysis plan or usable registration details, it cannot be confirmed that the reported visual-assessment result was not selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses.
