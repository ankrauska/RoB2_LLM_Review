1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants were described as randomly assigned to D-cycloserine (DCS) or matching placebo, and allocation was handled by a data manager who had no contact with participants. This provides reasonable assurance that treatment allocation was independent of recruitment and enrolment. Although the precise random-sequence generation method (e.g., computer-generated sequence) was not reported, there is no indication of a non-random or predictable allocation method.

   The article states: “Participants were randomly assigned to 250mg of DCS or placebo in identical-appearing capsules by a data manager who had no interaction with participants.”

   Baseline characteristics were generally comparable. The groups differed in age, but the authors report no differences in baseline social-anxiety severity, baseline speech SUDS, Social Phobia Scale scores, sex, ethnicity, or medication use. A single baseline difference in this small trial does not by itself indicate a problem with randomization, and analyses adjusting for age did not alter the findings.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants received either DCS or a matching placebo capsule before the exposure task. Both participants and experimenters appear to have remained blinded, supported by formal assessment of the blind: “neither participants nor experimenters were able to discern assignment (ps > .15).” The use of identical-appearing capsules and successful blinding makes important deviations arising from the trial context unlikely.

   The analysis compared the randomized DCS and placebo groups. The investigators excluded participants without Visit 2 outcome data, but this is principally an issue of missing outcome data rather than an inappropriate reassignment of participants according to treatment received. There is no report of crossovers, receipt of the wrong capsule, differential co-interventions, or exclusion based on adherence or post-randomization treatment response.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Four of 34 randomized participants (12%) did not return for Visit 2 and therefore were excluded from all analyses of between-session habituation: two in the DCS group and two in the placebo group. The balanced numbers reduce concern about differential attrition between groups, but outcome data were not available for all or nearly all randomized participants.

   The article states: “Four participants (2 DCS, 2 placebo) did not return for Visit 2 and were not included in any analyses.” Reasons included becoming unreachable (one participant in each group) and preferring not to continue (one participant in each group).

   These reasons do not clearly demonstrate that missingness depended on the unobserved second-session distress outcome, and the proportions missing were equal between groups. However, no sensitivity analysis or other analysis addressing the possible impact of the missing data was reported. Because participants who did not return may plausibly have differed in anxiety response or habituation, there are some concerns, but insufficient evidence for high risk of bias.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Between-session habituation was defined using subjective units of distress (SUDS), a participant-reported measure of anxiety during the standardized speech exposures. The same outcome method, scale, and assessment schedule were used for both groups at both visits. The authors describe repeated SUDS assessments before, during, and at the end of each speech, with average SUDS across the exposure used to define habituation.

   Although SUDS is subjective and participants themselves are the outcome assessors, participants were blinded to DCS versus placebo assignment through use of “identical-appearing capsules.” The blinding assessment found that participants could not identify their allocated intervention. Thus, knowledge of treatment assignment was unlikely to have differentially influenced their distress ratings.

   The primary outcome was explicitly defined: “Categorical response was defined as a drop of at least 5 points in average SUDS across the exposure across visits.” There is no indication that outcome ascertainment differed between the DCS and placebo groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies between-session habituation as the primary measure and reports the corresponding result: 75% of DCS participants versus 36% of placebo participants met the ≥5-point decrease criterion. It also reports continuous SUDS analyses and the Social Phobia Scale outcome. Thus, there is no direct evidence that the reported habituation result was selected from multiple outcomes or analyses because it was statistically favourable.

   However, no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied materials to confirm that the specific definition of habituation, the selected time points, the ≥5-point threshold, and the chosen analyses were specified before unblinded outcome data became available. The report includes multiple SUDS measurements within and across sessions, a categorical habituation analysis, a continuous SUDS analysis, and additional analyses with alternative covariates. This creates opportunities for selection among eligible outcome measurements and analyses.

   The trial registration is referenced as a potential source, but its content is not provided here; therefore, it cannot establish that the reported result was analyzed according to a pre-specified plan finalized before access to unblinded data.
