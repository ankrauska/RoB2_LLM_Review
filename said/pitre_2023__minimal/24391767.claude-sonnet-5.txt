# Risk of Bias Assessment: High-Field Open versus Short-Bore MRI of the Spine — Outcome: Image Quality Parameters

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:**
The allocation sequence was computer-generated (random), but there is insufficient detail regarding concealment adequacy prior to the specific image-quality outcome assessment, and baseline data raise no major concerns, but the process by which patients were "cross-referred" post-randomization introduces some ambiguity regarding whether the randomized comparison for this specific outcome (image quality) is fully preserved.

**b. Rationale:**
- The article states: "Eligible patients were randomly assigned (computer-generated sequence) in a 1:1 ratio..." — this satisfies the signalling question for random sequence generation (Yes).
- "Randomization was not stratified and allocation was concealed (using sealed envelopes)." This suggests allocation concealment was attempted; however, no further detail is given on whether envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, and sealed appropriately (minimal criteria per Table 2 of RoB2 guidance). This leaves some uncertainty about whether concealment fully met the "adequate" threshold, though the explicit statement of concealment supports "Probably yes."
- Baseline characteristics (Table 1) show no clinically meaningful or statistically significant differences between groups (e.g., age P=.1, BMI P=.3, sex P=.8, CLQ score P=.7), consistent with chance and not suggestive of a randomization failure.
- Because sequence generation was random and allocation was concealed, and baseline imbalances are compatible with chance, the default judgement would be "Low." However, the protocol lists the primary outcome as related to claustrophobia reduction, not image quality; image quality was a secondary/derived analysis using a subset of patients (93 of 174) with further post-randomization cross-referral of patients unable to tolerate their assigned scanner. This introduces some ambiguity about how the analytic subset for image quality relates to the original randomized comparison, warranting a downgrade from "Low" to "Some concerns" out of caution, since the "as analyzed" sample for image quality is a post-hoc-defined imaging subset rather than the full originally randomized cohort with a completely transparent, protocol-specified plan for this outcome.

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:**
Participants and personnel could not be blinded to the intervention (scanner type) given the nature of the intervention. Deviations from intended allocation (cross-referral to the other scanner) occurred in a non-trivial proportion of patients (25/93, ~27%) due to claustrophobia — a factor arising directly from the trial context (i.e., being assigned to a particular scanner and being unable to tolerate it). These deviations are relevant to the outcome (image quality, since the analyzed images came from the scanner actually used, not the assigned one) and were unbalanced between groups in terms of underlying reason (patients switched away specifically because of intolerance to their assigned device). Furthermore, the analysis was not a strict intention-to-treat analysis for the image-quality outcome — patients were analyzed according to the scanner on which images were actually obtained (i.e., an "as-treated" analysis), rather than as originally randomized.

**b. Rationale:**
- "Patients could not be blinded to the assigned study group due to the MR imaging setting." (2.1/2.2 = Yes — participants and personnel aware).
- "If patients did not complete imaging in their assigned MR scanner due to claustrophobia, they were cross-referred to imaging in the other scanner in order to avoid the risks of conscious sedation... Examinations performed after cross-referral, with or without sedation, were included in the primary analysis."
- This is a clear deviation from the intended (randomized) intervention that arose because of the trial context (claustrophobia triggered by being placed in the assigned scanner) — directly impacting which images (and hence image quality) were obtained and analyzed.
- Critically, for the outcome of image quality, the analysis was based on the scanner actually used, not the scanner assigned — this is effectively an "as-treated" analysis, which is explicitly identified in RoB2 guidance as inappropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention.
- "Thus, none of the patients was scanned on both scanners" — but the group comparisons for image quality were based on final scanner used after cross-referral, not original randomized assignment. This constitutes a failure to analyze participants in the group to which they were randomized.
- The subgroup analysis "excluding patients who underwent imaging after cross-referral revealed equally distributed results (P<0.0001, data not shown)" is reassuring but was not the primary analysis, and full data were not shown, limiting ability to verify lack of impact.
- Given that deviations arose because of the trial context, likely affected the outcome (image quality depends entirely on which scanner produced the images), and were not correctly handled via ITT principles for this outcome, this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:**
Outcome data (image quality ratings) were available for nearly all examinations that were performed; the numbers of sequences analyzed by group are clearly reported and appear complete relative to the imaging actually performed.

**b. Rationale:**
- Table 2 shows equal availability of sequences: e.g., T1w/T2w sagittal 48 (short-bore) vs 55 (open), matching the number of examinations performed; T2w axial 68 vs 76.
- The one instance of incomplete data was clearly documented and reasoned: "Only one patient aborted MR imaging on the first scanner due to claustrophobia after one sequence, which was thus not included in the primary analysis." This exclusion is minor (1 patient) and explained.
- "In 15 examinations optional sequences were acquired... which were not included in the analysis due to the small number" — this exclusion pertains to sequences not part of the core protocol-specified sequences and is unlikely to bias core comparisons.
- No substantial or differential missingness in image quality outcome data is apparent between groups; the flow of patients is well documented in Figure 1.
- Therefore, this domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:**
The method of measuring image quality was appropriate (validated qualitative scoring, SNR/CNR, and quantitative contour sharpness), and outcome assessors were blinded to intervention group and patient identity, minimizing risk of differential measurement error.

**b. Rationale:**
- "The qualitative analysis was performed by two examiners in consensus, and the quantitative analysis was performed by one examiner on a workstation... At the time of the analysis the examiners were blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity."
- This directly addresses the key signalling questions: outcome assessors were blinded (4.3 = No, not aware), and thus assessment of the outcome could not have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received.
- The measurement methods (qualitative 1–5 scales, SNR/CNR calculations, contour sharpness via ImageJ) are standard, validated, and pre-specified in the trial protocol, and applied identically in both groups: "The optimal viewing window-level parameters for each image were adjusted automatically by the examiners with a rectangular region of interest (ROI) positioned in a region including cerebrospinal fluid, disc tissue, and vertebral body, and standardized in size according to the anatomy," applied consistently.
- No evidence that measurement/ascertainment methods differed systematically between groups in a way that would bias results (methods were applied identically, using the same rating scales and formulas for both scanners).
- Therefore, this domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:**
The image quality outcome, its measurement methods, and analyses appear to have been pre-specified in the trial protocol/registration and were reported comprehensively and consistently with what was planned, with no evidence of selective reporting from multiple outcome measurements or analyses.

**b. Rationale:**
- The detailed trial protocol, published prior to results ("Enders J, Zimmermann E, Rief M, Martus P, Klingebiel R, et al. (2011) Reduction of claustrophobia during magnetic resonance imaging: methods and design of the 'CLAUSTRO' randomized controlled trial. BMC Med Imaging 11:4"), pre-specified the image quality assessment methodology in detail (as referenced repeatedly in the Methods: "see Protocol S1").
- All pre-specified qualitative parameters (overall image quality, contrast, contour sharpness, noise, artifacts) and quantitative parameters (SNR, CNR, contour sharpness) are reported in full for all relevant sequences and spine regions (Tables 3 and 4), with no apparent omissions.
- The trial registry (NCT00715806) lists the primary outcome as claustrophobia reduction, with image quality as a secondary/derived outcome, consistent with this paper being a pre-planned derived analysis ("The main results have been published in PLoS ONE"). This is transparent and consistent with a pre-specified secondary analysis plan rather than a post-hoc opportunistic reporting of favorable results.
- No evidence of selective omission of specific sequences, time points, or subgroups; all sequence types (T1 sagittal, T2 sagittal, T2 axial) and anatomical regions (cervicothoracic, thoracolumbar) are consistently reported for both qualitative and quantitative parameters.
- Sensitivity/subgroup analyses (cross-referred patients, sedated patients) are also reported, even though some are noted as "data not shown," which introduces minor uncertainty but does not suggest outcome-dependent selective reporting of the primary image-quality comparisons.
- Therefore, this domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias Judgement for Image Quality Outcome: **High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Although randomization, missing data handling, outcome measurement, and selective reporting domains are judged as low risk or some concerns, the domain "Deviations from the intended intervention" is judged as **High risk of bias** due to substantial post-randomization cross-referral (27% of patients) driven by the trial context (claustrophobia in the assigned scanner), combined with an effectively "as-treated" analysis for the image-quality outcome. Per RoB 2 guidance, a "High" risk of bias judgement in any single domain results in an overall "High risk of bias" judgement for this outcome.
