# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0 Tool
## Outcome: Visual Acuity (5-year visit, change from baseline)
## Effect of Interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (ITT)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The original trial (referenced 2010 publication) is not included in the provided text, so details of sequence generation and allocation concealment methods are not available in this document. The paper states "randomization" occurred but no details on sequence generation or allocation concealment methods are provided in this report. Baseline characteristics between the two ranibizumab groups are not directly presented in this 5-year paper (only completers vs. non-completers within each arm), so no direct assessment of baseline balance between randomized groups is possible from this text. Given the DRCR.net's established methodological rigor as a well-resourced clinical trials network and no evidence of problems, but with no explicit information on sequence generation/concealment in this document, a judgement of "Some concerns" (rather than "Low") is appropriate due to absence of information (No information on sequence generation and allocation concealment methods; No information to confirm absence of baseline imbalance suggestive of randomization problems).

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** Participants and treating personnel were **not blinded** to laser treatment assignment (prompt vs. deferred), as this is an open-label design regarding laser treatment strategy — participants assigned to "prompt" laser necessarily knew they received laser, while "deferred" participants knew they had not (or had only later). The paper states: "masking of the visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians" — indicating that *outcome assessors* were masked, but this does not address masking of participants or treating clinicians regarding the laser strategy itself.

Because participants/personnel were aware of intervention assignment, the question becomes whether deviations from intended intervention arose **because of the trial context** and affected the outcome differentially. The deferred group could receive laser per protocol if criteria were met (this is consistent with protocol, not a deviation). However, the paper notes appropriate ITT-consistent analysis: "A longitudinal analysis was performed...for all eyes in the two ranibizumab groups...using all available visit data," and results were also checked restricting to completers with similar findings (Figure 2), indicating a full ITT approach was used (question on appropriate analysis: **Yes**).

There is no clear evidence that non-protocol interventions or contamination occurred asymmetrically due to trial context beyond what is expected from the protocol-driven different treatment strategies (which are the interventions being tested, not deviations from them). However, "Other treatment for DME (such as intravitreous bevacizumab...)" was received by 7% of prompt-group completers (7 per protocol, 2 not per protocol) and none in the deferred group — this is a minor imbalance in non-protocol treatment but small in magnitude and mostly protocol-driven. Given the open-label nature and this small imbalance in non-protocol treatment, but appropriate ITT analysis, "Some concerns" is warranted rather than "Low" or "High."

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** Outcome data were not available for all randomized participants at 5 years. "Excluding deaths, the 5-year completion rate was 76%...and 74%" of originally randomized eyes in the prompt and deferred groups respectively — meaning roughly one-quarter of surviving randomized eyes lack 5-year data, which is a substantial proportion that could plausibly affect the result.

The authors do address this: "A comparison of eyes with a 5-year exam ('completers') to eyes without a 5-year exam ('non-completers')...Some imbalances between completers versus non-completers were noted (age, prior PRP, lens status, diabetic retinopathy severity level), and these factors were included as potential confounders in the adjusted analysis." This is evidence that missingness could depend on prognostic factors, but the authors used longitudinal models incorporating "all available visit data" from all randomized eyes (not just 5-year completers) and adjusted for these imbalances, which mitigates but does not eliminate the concern. The authors themselves state as a limitation: "it is not possible to know whether incomplete data were related to unobserved factors that could have affected the results in either direction."

Since dropout proportions and reasons appear generally comparable between groups (76% vs. 74% completion excluding deaths; sensitivity analysis in Figure 2 restricted to completers showed similar results to the primary analysis), there is no strong signal that missingness depended differentially on outcome by group, but the substantial overall missingness (~25%) without complete assurance that it is unrelated to the true outcome value supports "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The method of measuring the outcome (best-corrected visual acuity via standardized E-ETDRS protocol, implied by "E-ETDRS Visual Acuity" terminology used) is an appropriate, validated, and standard method for this outcome, and was applied identically in both groups regardless of treatment assignment — the measurement method did not differ between intervention groups since laser strategy does not affect how visual acuity is tested. Critically, the paper explicitly states: "masking of the visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians," indicating that outcome assessors were blinded to intervention group assignment. Since visual acuity assessment was conducted by masked examiners using a standardized protocol, knowledge of intervention assignment could not have influenced the outcome assessment. This supports a low risk of bias judgement.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** This is a pre-specified, registered comparative effectiveness trial conducted by an established clinical trials network (DRCR.net) with a publicly available protocol ("The protocol is available on the DRCR.net website"). Visual acuity was the pre-specified primary outcome in the original trial design (as referenced from the 2010 publication and consistent with the "Main Outcome Measure: Best-corrected visual acuity at the 5-year visit" stated in this abstract), and results are reported comprehensively: mean change in letter score, proportions with ≥10 and ≥15 letter gains/losses, and results are consistent whether using the full randomized cohort with longitudinal modeling or restricted to 5-year completers (Figure 2), with multiple pre-specified sensitivity/subgroup analyses (e.g., by baseline VA severity) reported transparently, including both favorable and less favorable (non-statistically-significant, P=0.09) results for the primary comparison. There is no indication that the visual acuity outcome or analysis method was selected post hoc based on favorable results — the same analytic approach (longitudinal model adjusting for baseline VA) was used and reported consistently with prior 1- and 3-year reports from the same trial, and multiplicity of outcome measures (10-letter, 15-letter thresholds) are all reported rather than selectively highlighted.
