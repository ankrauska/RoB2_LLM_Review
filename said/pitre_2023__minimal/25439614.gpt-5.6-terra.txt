1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the report states that participants/eyes were randomly assigned, but does not describe the random-sequence generation or allocation-concealment methods. Baseline data for the originally randomized prompt-versus-deferred groups are not presented in the supplied report, so successful implementation of randomization cannot be fully verified.

   b. **Rationale:** The article describes a “multicenter randomized clinical trial” and states that “180 eyes were assigned to ranibizumab plus prompt focal/grid laser treatment and 181 to ranibizumab plus deferred laser treatment.” This supports that random allocation was intended. However, there is no information in the supplied article on whether a computer-generated/random sequence was used or whether allocation was concealed until enrolment and assignment. The available Table 1 compares 5-year completers with non-completers within treatment groups, rather than baseline characteristics of all randomized groups. Therefore, there is insufficient information to judge this domain as low risk, but no evidence of a definite failure of randomization.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Although participants and treating personnel were necessarily likely to know whether laser was given promptly or deferred, the relevant question for the assignment effect is whether deviations arising because of the trial context were unbalanced and likely to affect visual acuity. Treatment and retreatment were governed by protocol-defined criteria: “An assessment for retreatment with ranibizumab, retreatment with laser, or initiation of laser treatment for the deferred group, occurred at each visit. The treatment decision was based on protocol specified criteria.”

   The treatment contrast itself was implemented as intended: prompt laser was given in the prompt group, whereas laser in the deferred group “had to be delayed for at least 24 weeks” and could subsequently be given only for persistent, non-improving DME. Receipt of laser by 44% of deferred-group eyes is therefore consistent with the planned intervention strategy, rather than a protocol deviation.

   The visual-acuity analysis was based on randomized treatment groups using longitudinal models with “all available visit data.” The report also states that Figure 1 includes “all randomized study eyes.” Exclusion of participants with missing outcome data is addressed under the missing-data domain. The report notes limited other DME treatment in the prompt group—9 of 124 completers, of whom 7 were treated per protocol for futility/failure and 2 were not—but there is no indication that these few non-protocol treatments arose from the trial context or materially biased the assigned-group comparison.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Five-year visual-acuity data were unavailable for approximately one quarter of the originally randomized participants/eyes. Excluding deaths, completion was “76% of the 163 original participants randomized to the ranibizumab + prompt laser group and 74% of the 150 original participants randomized to the ranibizumab + deferred laser group.” Thus, outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants.

   The principal reason was selective participation in the extension phase: “Because the fourth and fifth years were extensions of the original study, some patients chose not to consent to the longer follow-up.” This mechanism could plausibly be related to health status, treatment burden, or visual outcome. The report identified differences between completers and non-completers, including “age, prior PRP, lens status, diabetic retinopathy severity level.” The investigators adjusted longitudinal models for measured factors, but did not provide sensitivity analyses demonstrating that plausible outcomes among missing participants would not change the estimated treatment effect. They explicitly acknowledge that “it is not possible to know whether incomplete data were related to unobserved factors that could have affected the results in either direction.”

   Missingness was broadly similar between groups (24% versus 26%), and there is no firm evidence that missingness was dependent on the unobserved visual-acuity outcome. Therefore, this is judged as some concerns rather than high risk.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Visual acuity was assessed as best-corrected visual acuity/E-ETDRS letter score, an appropriate and standardized measure for this outcome. Importantly, the report identifies “masking of the visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians” as a study strength. Therefore, the outcome assessors for visual acuity were masked to treatment assignment.

   The same visual-acuity assessment procedures appear to have been used across groups and at protocol-defined follow-up visits. There is no indication that ascertainment of visual acuity differed systematically between prompt and deferred laser groups. Because visual-acuity examiners were masked and the outcome is measured using a standardized letter-score test, knowledge of treatment assignment is unlikely to have influenced outcome measurement.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report identifies best-corrected visual acuity at the 5-year visit as the “Main Outcome Measure” and reports the relevant numerical result: mean change from baseline of +7.2 letters with prompt laser versus +9.8 letters with deferred laser (difference −2.6 letters, 95% CI −5.5 to +0.4; P = 0.09). It also reports related categorical visual-acuity outcomes and both minimally adjusted and additionally adjusted longitudinal analyses.

   However, the supplied materials do not include a dated pre-specified statistical analysis plan for the 5-year extension, and the trial-registration information referenced in the request is not available in the provided text. Consequently, it cannot be confirmed that the selected 5-year visual-acuity time point, the longitudinal-model specification, truncation of visual-acuity changes to ±30 letters, and the reported analysis were all finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. Visual acuity was measured repeatedly over five years and was analysed in several ways (continuous change, categorical gain/loss thresholds, longitudinal analyses, and adjusted analyses), providing opportunities for selective selection of measurements or analyses. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because of its findings, so high risk is not warranted; nevertheless, absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan leads to some concerns.
