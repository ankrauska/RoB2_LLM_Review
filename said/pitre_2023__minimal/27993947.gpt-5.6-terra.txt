1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was adequately generated: “Randomization was performed by means of a computer-generated random sequence with stratification for age, gender, and positive family history for CHD using the Pocock and Simon method.” Baseline characteristics were well balanced, including age, sex, smoking, family history, BMI, blood pressure and lipid values (all reported *p* values >0.05), providing no indication that randomization failed.  
   
   However, the report does not describe how allocation was concealed from personnel recruiting/enrolling participants before assignment. It states that one investigator “generated the random allocation sequence and study arm assignment,” but does not state that allocation was centrally administered, inaccessible to recruiters, or protected by an equivalent concealment mechanism. Under RoB 2, absence of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns when baseline data do not suggest a problem with randomization.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.  
   b. **Rationale:** This was an unmasked trial: the registry specifies “Masking: NONE,” and participants and clinicians necessarily knew whether genetic risk information was disclosed. Nevertheless, for the assignment-effect estimand, awareness alone does not establish bias. RoB 2 requires evidence that deviations from the assigned intervention arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, affected the outcome, and were imbalanced between groups.  
   
   No such deviations were reported. Both groups received disclosure of CHD risk, a genetic-counsellor encounter, a physician encounter, and use of the Statin Choice decision aid; the key assigned difference was whether the risk estimate incorporated the genetic risk score. There is no evidence of differential non-protocol co-interventions or failures in delivery caused by the trial context.  
   
   The analysis compared the randomized groups: “Analyses were performed with randomization groups as the independent variables.” SDM results were reported for the two assigned groups, with outcome data from 206 of 207 participants. Excluding the one participant without a completed SDM survey is appropriately addressed principally under missing outcome data rather than this domain.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Of 207 randomized participants completing the second study visit, “The SDM survey was completed by 206 study participants.” Thus, SDM data were available for approximately 99.5% of randomized participants. Only one participant had missing SDM outcome data, a number too small to plausibly have made an important difference to the estimated between-group effect on this continuous score.  
   
   The article does not report the reason or allocation group for the missing questionnaire, but given the extremely small amount of missing data, this omission does not create a material risk of bias for the SDM-score result.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** The shared decision-making score was a participant-reported, subjective outcome: “SDM was evaluated using the SDM Questionnaire (SDM-Q), a validated 11-question survey that assesses patients' perception of the SDM process during clinical encounters.” Participants were the outcome assessors for this outcome.  
   
   Participants were aware of their assigned intervention, because one group was explicitly given genetics-informed CHD risk information whereas the comparator group received conventional risk information only. The registry confirms that there was no masking. Knowledge of having received genetic-risk disclosure could plausibly influence participants’ perceptions and reporting of the quality of shared decision-making—for example, through expectations that genetic information represents more individualized or advanced care.  
   
   Although the SDM-Q is validated and was administered comparably across groups, lack of blinding is particularly important for a participant-reported evaluation of the consultation itself. Since the intervention could reasonably influence how participants rated that encounter independently of any difference in the underlying quality of shared decision-making, assessment of the outcome was likely influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The trial registry prespecified shared decision-making only broadly as “Shared decision making (as assessed by survey).” It did not specify the SDM-Q instrument, scoring approach, assessment time point, handling of incomplete questionnaires, or a statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that the reported SDM-score analysis was conducted according to a sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan.  
   
   The publication reports both participant-reported SDM-Q results and an observer-based OPTION5 assessment in a random subset of 80 recorded encounters. These are distinct eligible ways of evaluating the broad shared-decision-making domain, although both were reported and neither showed a statistically significant between-group difference. There is no direct evidence that the reported SDM-Q result was selected because of its magnitude, direction, or statistical significance. However, because neither the registration record nor an available protocol/SAP specifies the intended SDM instrument, time point, scoring rules, or analysis, selective selection among possible measurements or analyses cannot be excluded.
