1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the report states that participants were randomized but does not describe either a random sequence-generation method or concealment of allocation. Baseline characteristics by randomized group are also not presented sufficiently to assess balance.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants “were stratified” on recent self-reported substance/alcohol use and duration of negative urine screens and “were then randomized into one of the three study conditions.” However, the report does not state how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table) or how allocation was concealed from recruiters/enrolling staff. The initially randomized group sizes (routine 25, clinic MMM 27, office MMM 26) are reasonably compatible with the intended three-group allocation, but this alone does not establish adequate allocation concealment. No between-group baseline table is provided for the randomized groups. Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns unless adequate concealment can reasonably be inferred.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline, because the reported retention analysis excluded five randomized participants who withdrew immediately after assignment, rather than retaining all randomized participants in their assigned groups. This exclusion was materially unbalanced across groups.

   b. **Rationale:** Although the authors describe analysis of an “intent-to-treat sample (n=73),” 78 patients were actually randomized: “routine=25, clinic-based=27, office-based=26.” They then report that “five patients withdrew from the study upon assignment to study condition; four following assignment to the routine treatment condition and one following assignment to the MMM physician office-based condition.” The survival analysis of retention was conducted in the remaining 73 participants only (routine 21, clinic 27, office 25).

   For the effect of assignment, an appropriate analysis should include all randomized participants in their assigned groups. Excluding patients after randomization because they withdrew upon learning their allocation is not consistent with this principle, especially for treatment retention, for which withdrawal immediately after allocation is itself directly relevant. The exclusions were also differential: 4/25 (16%) in routine care versus 0/27 in clinic-based MMM and 1/26 (4%) in office-based MMM. Given the small sample and the outcome being retention, this could substantially affect the estimated between-group retention comparison. The interventions were necessarily open-label, but the principal concern is the post-randomization exclusion rather than merely lack of blinding.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Among the 73 participants included in the reported randomized-care cohort, retention status at 6 months appears available for all participants: 19/21 routine-care, 24/27 clinic-based MMM, and 23/25 office-based MMM completed 6 months, with seven participants reported as dropping out and their reasons described. Thus, loss of retention is captured as the outcome rather than being unobserved outcome data.

   The five participants excluded after randomization are a serious issue, but this is best classified under deviations from intended intervention because they were excluded from the analysis after randomization, rather than as missing outcome data. There is no indication that retention status was missing for substantial numbers of the analysed 73 participants or that missingness depended on unobserved retention outcomes.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Treatment retention at 6 months is a relatively objective administrative outcome—whether a participant remained in the assigned randomized-care programme through the 6-month follow-up. The article reports the numbers completing 6 months and describes reasons for the seven discontinuations, including transfer to another clinic, tapering methadone, and abrupt departure.

   Participants and treating personnel were aware of treatment assignment because the reporting schedules and treatment settings differed. However, awareness is unlikely to bias the *measurement* of whether a participant was retained; the status can be ascertained from treatment/programme records. There is no evidence that retention was measured using different definitions or ascertainment procedures across groups. The potential influence of allocation on participants’ decisions to remain in treatment is part of the effect of assignment and the post-randomization exclusions are addressed in Domain 2, not as measurement bias.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report specifies that retention was an expected outcome and states in the Methods that the three groups “were compared on treatment retention using survival analysis.” It reports the 6-month retention percentages and the survival-analysis result: “No between-group differences were observed in retention (Wilcoxon–Gehan statistic=0.14, df=2, ns).” This is consistent with retention having been analysed and reported.

   However, the publication presents only “the first 6 months of the 1-year trial,” while the trial was described as ongoing. No prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan or sufficiently detailed, date-stamped trial-registration information is available here to verify that the reported 6-month retention analysis, time point, analysis method, and contrast were all specified before unblinded data were available. Retention could potentially be analysed at multiple follow-up times or with alternative approaches. There is no direct evidence that the reported retention result was selected because of its findings, but the absence of a verifiable prespecified analysis plan leaves some concerns.
