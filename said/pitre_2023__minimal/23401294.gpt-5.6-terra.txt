1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The study states that participants were randomly allocated, and describes a random-looking procedure: “*The randomization procedure was to pick the name of the orthosis from a bag including the names of both orthoses.*” This provides some evidence of a chance element in sequence generation. However, it does not report who prepared the draws, whether the names were identical and indistinguishable, whether draws were conducted sequentially, or whether allocation was concealed from those recruiting/enrolling participants. Therefore, allocation concealment cannot be confirmed.  
   
   Baseline characteristics appear reasonably comparable: there were no statistically significant between-group differences in age, weight, height, kyphosis angle, baseline balance score, directional control, or reaction time. However, the small sample (n = 18) and unequal allocation (10 Spinomed, 8 PTS) do not resolve the insufficient reporting of allocation concealment. Under RoB 2, lack of information about concealment leads to **some concerns** when no major baseline imbalance suggests a definite failure of randomization.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of **assignment** to Spinomed versus posture-training support (PTS), not the effect of adhering to the assigned device. Participants necessarily knew which orthosis they wore, because the devices were visibly and physically different. However, lack of participant blinding alone does not imply bias for the effect of assignment. Bias would require deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context, affected balance score, and differed between groups.  
   
   The report indicates that both orthoses were fitted by a certified orthotist and that participants in both groups underwent the same testing procedures: “*Both of the orthoses were fitted on the participants by a certified orthotist who adjusted the straps with the same tension in all participants.*” Participants were asked to wear their assigned orthosis for 1.5 hours before testing, and the order of testing with and without the orthosis was randomized. There is no evidence of differential co-interventions, crossovers between assigned orthoses, protocol violations, or other deviations attributable to the trial context that would plausibly affect balance score.  
   
   The analysis retained the randomized groups (Spinomed, n = 10; PTS, n = 8) and compared the change in balance score between groups. Thus, for the assignment effect, there is no indication that participants were analysed according to treatment received rather than assigned treatment or that eligible participants were excluded post-randomization.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** All 18 randomized participants appear to have completed the study and contributed outcome data. The article states: “*Eighteen participants … completed the study procedure.*” The balance-score analyses include all participants assigned to each group: 10 in the Spinomed group and 8 in the PTS group, totaling 18 participants.  
   
   Tables 1–3 consistently report balance-score data for all 10 Spinomed and all 8 PTS participants. There is no reported loss to follow-up, withdrawal, exclusion from the balance-score analysis, or missing balance-score measurement. Accordingly, outcome data were available for all, or effectively all, randomized participants.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Balance score was measured using the NeuroCom EquiTest sensory organization test (SOT), a standardized computerized force-platform assessment. The authors describe that “*the average three balance scores gathered from six test configurations were used to calculate the total score, which ranged between 0 and 100.*” The measurement was therefore generated by a standardized system rather than by subjective clinical judgement.  
   
   The same outcome-assessment method, equipment, procedures, and testing conditions were used in both groups. The sequence of testing with and without the orthosis was randomized, with a one-hour interval between these conditions, to reduce learning and fatigue effects. Although participants could not be blinded to whether they were wearing an orthosis, balance score is an instrument-derived performance measure with limited opportunity for assessor judgement or differential outcome reporting. There is no indication that ascertainment or scoring differed systematically between Spinomed and PTS groups.  

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** Balance score is clearly identified in the publication as the main outcome measure: “*The balance score was considered in the role of the main outcome measure.*” The paper reports balance score both within each randomized group and for the between-group comparison of change in balance score. This is reassuring.  
   
   However, the available report does not provide a dated, sufficiently detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the reported balance-score result—including the selected analysis metric (change from without-orthosis to with-orthosis), comparison, and timing—was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The trial registration may identify balance as an outcome, but registration of an outcome alone generally does not establish that the exact measurement, analysis approach, and result reported were pre-specified. Since balance could potentially have been represented by different SOT conditions, summary measures, within-group analyses, change scores, or final values, there remains some opportunity for selective selection of the reported result.  
   
   There is no direct evidence that the reported balance-score result was selectively chosen based on statistical significance; therefore, high risk of bias is not justified. Nevertheless, absence of a clearly documented pre-specified analysis plan supports a judgement of **some concerns**.
