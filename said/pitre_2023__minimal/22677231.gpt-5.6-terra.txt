1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study is described as randomized and used equal-sized groups (105 per group), but the allocation-sequence generation and allocation-concealment methods are insufficiently described. The article states: “Participants who left their email addresses at the end of the baseline survey were randomly allocated to the treatment or wait-list group in consecutive order. No stratification was applied.” This does not establish that a random component was used to generate the sequence, nor that the allocation was concealed until enrolment.

   Baseline characteristics, including BDI total score, appeared balanced: baseline BDI was 30.02 (SD 10.18) in the wait-list group and 28.81 (SD 11.11) in the Deprexis group, *p* > .4. Thus, there is no indication from observed baseline data that randomization failed. However, under RoB 2, lack of information on both sequence generation and concealment leads to some concerns rather than low risk.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to Deprexis versus assignment to the wait-list condition. Participants necessarily knew their group assignment: those assigned to immediate treatment received a Deprexis voucher, whereas wait-list participants were told that they would receive access after the eight-week reassessment. Therefore, participants were not blinded.

   However, there is no evidence that deviations from the intended interventions arose because of the trial context and were unbalanced between groups in a way likely to affect BDI scores. The mid-intervention email was sent to all participants and “did not contain any therapeutic information.” The study allowed concurrent usual treatment in both groups as part of its naturalistic design; this does not itself demonstrate trial-context deviations. Although the authors speculate that some wait-list participants may have received additional treatment, this is not reported as a differential, trial-induced deviation from protocol.

   The primary result was also analysed using an intention-to-treat approach. The authors state: “ITT analyses considered data from all subjects with available baseline data. Multiple imputation was adopted to estimate post-scores for non-completers.” Participants were analysed according to their randomized allocation, which is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention. Any concern arising from missing post-intervention outcomes is considered separately in the missing-data domain.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Post-intervention BDI data were unavailable for 40 of 210 randomized participants (19.0%). Completion differed numerically between groups: 90/105 (85.7%) in the wait-list group and 80/105 (78.1%) in the Deprexis group. Thus, outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants.

   The investigators performed multiple imputation for their ITT analysis: “100 dataset[s] were imputed, stratified for treatment.” However, the publication does not adequately describe the imputation model or provide sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-not-at-random assumptions. Consequently, there is insufficient evidence that the result was not biased by missing data.

   There is no strong direct evidence that missingness depended on the unobserved post-intervention BDI score: non-completers reportedly did not differ from completers on baseline psychopathological variables, apart from two quality-of-life subscales. Nonetheless, in an online depression intervention, dropout could plausibly relate to participants’ depressive symptoms, motivation, perceived benefit, or lack of benefit. The moderately differential completion rate adds to this concern. The available information does not establish that such dependence was likely enough to warrant a high-risk judgement; therefore, some concerns is appropriate.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The BDI total score is a participant-reported, subjective measure of depressive symptoms. Participants were aware of whether they had immediate access to Deprexis or had been allocated to a wait-list. The intervention group received an interactive online psychological programme, while the comparator group was explicitly informed that access would be delayed for eight weeks.

   The article confirms that the primary outcome “rested exclusively on self-report” and identifies the BDI as “the primary outcome.” Although the BDI is a validated and widely used instrument, validity and reliability do not remove the risk of differential measurement when unblinded participants report subjective symptoms. In this context—an online self-help intervention compared with waiting-list control—participants’ expectations, disappointment in allocation to delayed treatment, and awareness of receiving the intervention were likely to influence their self-rated depression symptoms.

   The study’s registry describes masking of the outcome assessor, but for this self-completed BDI outcome the participant is the outcome assessor. Hence, registry reporting of single masking does not provide protection against bias in this specific participant-reported outcome. Under RoB 2, knowledge of allocation was likely to influence assessment of a subjective participant-reported outcome; therefore, the risk of bias is high.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial registration identifies depressive-symptom severity assessed by the BDI as the primary outcome and specifies that participants would be assessed before intervention and eight weeks later. The publication is consistent with this broad specification: it identifies the BDI as the primary outcome and reports the post-intervention comparison, including an ITT ANCOVA result (*p* = .03; *d* = .36).

   However, the registry does not provide a sufficiently detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan for the BDI result. In particular, it does not specify the precise analysis model, handling of missing data, covariate adjustment, choice between post-treatment values versus change scores, or the planned primary analysis population. The published article reports both per-protocol and multiple-imputation ITT analyses, BDI total score and three BDI subscales, and several additional outcomes. The article states that “PP and intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses were conducted” and that the principal analyses were ANCOVAs, but there is no evidence that these choices were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   There is no clear evidence that the reported BDI total-score result was selected from multiple analyses or measurements because it was statistically favourable; the primary outcome itself was prospectively registered and both PP and ITT results were presented. Nevertheless, absence of a detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan leaves some concern about selection among eligible analyses.
