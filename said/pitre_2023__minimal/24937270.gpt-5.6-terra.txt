1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study is described as randomized and used blocking: “*Volunteers … were randomized into one of four study arms*” and “*Randomization was completed using blocks of four*.” However, the report does not state how the allocation sequence within blocks was generated (e.g., computer-generated random ordering) or how allocation was concealed until enrolment. Blocked allocation alone does not demonstrate a random sequence, and no central/randomization-envelope procedure is reported.  
   
   Baseline characteristics did not show statistically significant group differences (“*There were no significant differences in demographic characteristics between groups*”), and Table 1 does not show an obvious extreme imbalance. This reduces concern that randomization was subverted, but it cannot resolve the lack of information on sequence generation and allocation concealment. Under RoB 2, absence of information on allocation concealment generally leads to **some concerns** when baseline imbalance does not suggest a problem.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and the investigator assessing outcomes were reportedly blinded: “*Both the investigator assessing the outcomes and the subjects were blinded to the intervention.*” The sham procedure involved 30 seconds of stimulation followed by automatic cessation, which is a plausible approach to support participant blinding. The reported blinding check found that participants were unable to identify their allocation better than chance: “*Subjects did not guess correctly the stimulation group to which they were assigned beyond chance (p = 0.679).*”
   
   There is no report of important trial-context deviations, differential co-interventions, or switching between assigned stimulation conditions. The coherence analyses compared the randomized stimulation groups, using pre-to-post change as the dependent variable. One participant in the random-frequency group was excluded because of excessive EEG artefacts; this is missing outcome data rather than an analysis according to received treatment or a per-protocol exclusion due to non-adherence. Thus, the reported analysis is consistent with estimating the effect of assignment to the intervention among participants with outcome data.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Of 40 recruited individuals, 38 completed the stimulation session. The two who did not participate “*signed the consent form and decided not to participate before stimulation was given*”; they do not appear to have entered the randomized intervention comparison. The four group totals among completers sum to 38.
   
   For the connectivity outcome, one participant in the random-frequency group was excluded: “*One subject in the Random Frequency group was removed from the analysis due to excessive artifacts in the EEG recording.*” Thus, outcome data appear to have been available for 37 of 38 randomized/completing participants (approximately 97%). For this continuous EEG-coherence outcome, this amount of missing data is sufficiently small that it is unlikely, by itself, to have materially changed the estimate. The stated reason—excessive recording artefacts—is a technical measurement problem rather than an outcome-dependent clinical reason such as symptom worsening or lack of benefit. There is no indication of differential attrition across the other groups or of missingness related to the true functional-connectivity outcome.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Interhemispheric functional connectivity was assessed using EEG coherence with a defined, quantitative method: “*We used magnitude square coherence as a pair-wise connectivity measure*,” estimated using the “*Welch’s averaged modified periodogram method*.” EEG was recorded using the same 64-channel device and the same pre- and post-stimulation procedures for all groups, including eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment differed systematically by assigned group.
   
   Although manual artefact detection/rejection was used, the outcome assessor was reported to be blinded: “*Both the investigator assessing the outcomes and the subjects were blinded to the intervention.*” Coherence is also a quantitatively derived electrophysiological measure with limited scope for subjective judgement once the recording and processing approach are specified. Therefore, knowledge of treatment assignment is unlikely to have influenced measurement of this outcome.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication describes multiple potentially eligible functional-connectivity outcomes and analyses: alpha and theta bands; four sub-bands; two electrode pairs; eyes-open and eyes-closed states; an eyes-condition-by-group interaction; an overall group effect; and multiple pairwise post-hoc comparisons. The paper describes the analysis as exploratory: “*We conducted an exploratory analysis*,” and reports further unplanned-looking comparisons: “*Further exploratory analysis showed that the theta interhemispheric coherence was significantly higher for the random frequency group compared to 1 Hz … and 100 Hz stimulation*.”
   
   The authors state that the planned post-hoc comparisons of each active intervention against sham were “*according to a-priori specifications*.” However, the available material does not provide a dated, sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan demonstrating that the exact connectivity outcome, electrode-pair handling, frequency bands/sub-bands, time-point/condition treatment, model, and all reported comparisons were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. In particular, the additional exploratory active-versus-active comparisons create scope for selection among multiple analyses.
   
   No content from the cited trial-registration record is available in the supplied materials to verify whether these particular connectivity analyses and reporting choices were prospectively specified. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was statistically significant; therefore, **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias, is appropriate.
