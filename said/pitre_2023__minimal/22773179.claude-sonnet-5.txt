# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0 Tool
## Outcome: Visual Acuity (Figure 4 - Efficacy/UCVA and Figure 5 - Safety/CDVA)
## Effect of Interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias Arising from the Randomization Process

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**a. Rationale for judgement:**
The sequence generation was clearly random and appropriately described. However, there is no information provided about allocation concealment procedures, and while baseline characteristics were balanced, the paired-eye design has specific considerations that raise some uncertainty about implementation.

**b. Supporting rationale:**
- Sequence generation: "the other eye was treated with the MEL80 non-aspheric profile (TSA), assigned at random using Microsoft Excel's random number function (Microsoft Excel 2003, Microsoft Corporation, Seattle, Washington)." This clearly satisfies the criterion for a random sequence generation (1.1 = Yes).
- Allocation concealment: No information is provided about how/when the randomization result was revealed to the surgeon or whether it could be anticipated in advance of eye assignment. Given this is a paired-eye trial where both eyes of the same patient are enrolled simultaneously and the surgeon performs both procedures on the same day, the practical opportunity for allocation to influence recruitment/eligibility is low, but the lack of explicit description of a concealment mechanism (e.g., sealed envelope, third-party allocation) leads to "No information" for signalling question 1.2.
- Baseline imbalance: "The mean preoperative spherical equivalent refraction was −2.43 ± 1.53 D... in the ASA group and −2.54 ± 1.47 D... in the TSA group. There was no statistically significant difference in preoperative spherical equivalent refraction between groups (p = 0.87)." This suggests no important baseline imbalance (1.3 = No).
- Per the algorithm: Random sequence = Yes, Allocation concealment = No information, Imbalance = No → this maps to "Some concerns" (per Table 4: Y/PY, NI, N/PN/NI → Some concerns).

## 2. Deviations from Intended Intervention (Effect of Assignment to Intervention)

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**a. Rationale for judgement:**
Although blinding of the surgeon (and likely the patient) to the intervention received was not possible given the nature of laser ablation profile assignment, there is no evidence that any deviations from the intended intervention occurred due to the trial context, and an appropriate intention-to-treat-consistent analysis was used.

**b. Supporting rationale:**
- Participants/personnel awareness: The surgeon necessarily knew and selected the treatment parameters (ASA vs TSA) for each eye per the randomization schedule; this is an inherent feature of laser refractive surgery and cannot be blinded during the procedure. Thus, personnel were aware of the intervention (2.2 = Yes).
- Deviations from trial context: There is no report of any additional treatments, retreatments, protocol violations, or switching between profiles: "For each patient, one eye was treated with the MEL80 standard aspheric profile (ASA) and the other eye was treated with the MEL80 non-aspheric profile (TSA)... A 6 mm optical zone was used for all ablations." This indicates the intervention was delivered exactly as assigned in all cases, with no evidence of deviations arising from the trial context (2.3 = No).
- Since there were no deviations arising from trial context, the domain reverts to assessing the analysis approach.
- Analysis: All 30 eyes (15 patients × 2 eyes) appear to have been analysed in the groups as randomized: "Fig. 4 shows the efficacy for both groups; 100% of eyes were 20/25 or better" and "Fig. 5 shows the safety... no eyes lost two or more lines CDVA in either group" — both based on the full paired-eye sample (2.6 = Yes, appropriate ITT-consistent analysis).
- Per the algorithm (Table 6, Part 1): Either 2.1/2.2 = Yes, 2.3 = No → Low risk for Part 1; Part 2: appropriate analysis = Yes → Low. Combined: Low risk of bias.

## 3. Missing Outcome Data

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**a. Rationale for judgement:**
Outcome data (visual acuity) appear to have been available for all randomized eyes (30/30) at the 3-month follow-up, with no reported losses to follow-up or missing measurements.

**b. Supporting rationale:**
- "A prospective randomized paired-eye controlled study of 30 eyes of 15 myopic patients was conducted." All efficacy/safety figures (Figs. 4 and 5) explicitly reference "30 eyes at 3 months postop" with 100% of eyes represented in the outcome distributions.
- There is no mention in the Methods, Results, or Discussion of any patient dropout, loss to follow-up, or exclusion after randomization.
- Since data appear complete for all 30 eyes for visual acuity at 3 months (3.1 = Yes), this satisfies the low risk of bias criterion directly without need to consider further signalling questions.

## 4. Measurement of the Outcome

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**a. Rationale for judgement:**
The method of measuring visual acuity was appropriate and standardized. However, outcome assessors (and patients, as participant-facing components of acuity testing) were not blinded to intervention assignment, and there is a plausible — though not clearly evidenced — possibility that assessment of this partially subjective outcome could have been influenced by knowledge of which ablation profile (ASA vs. TSA) was used in each eye.

**b. Supporting rationale:**
- Appropriateness of measurement: "Manifest refraction, uncorrected distance visual acuity and CDVA were measured before and 3 months after surgery." This is a standard, validated method (4.1 = No).
- Comparability of ascertainment: The same measurement protocol (Snellen visual acuity, same examiners, same timepoints) was applied to both eyes/groups, so there is no indication that ascertainment methods differed between groups (4.2 = No).
- Blinding of assessors: The article provides no statement that examiners or patients were masked to which eye received which ablation profile. Given this is a same-patient, fellow-eye comparison performed by the same surgical/clinical team, and no blinding procedures are described, it is reasonable to conclude outcome assessors (and potentially participants, who contribute a subjective response during acuity testing) were aware of intervention status (4.3 = Probably yes).
- Could this affect the outcome? Visual acuity, while relying on an objective Snellen chart, does have a behavioral/participant-response component (this is not a fully "hard" outcome like all-cause mortality), so knowledge of receiving the "better" (aspheric, theoretically fewer aberrations) treatment could plausibly influence patient effort/reporting (4.4 = Probably yes).
- Likely influence: There is no specific evidence (e.g., examiner comments, differential effort) suggesting that assessment was actually influenced, and the outcome (Snellen acuity) has relatively limited subjectivity compared to, e.g., patient-reported symptom scores (4.5 = Probably no).
- Per the algorithm: Not inappropriate, not differing ascertainment, aware = Yes/PY, could be influenced = Yes/PY, unlikely influenced = No/PN → maps to "Some concerns."

## 5. Selective Reporting

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**a. Rationale for judgement:**
No pre-registered trial protocol or statistical analysis plan is referenced or available for cross-checking, and visual acuity outcomes (UCVA/CDVA) are reported comprehensively as pre-specified refractive surgery outcome metrics (per Reinstein/Waring reporting standards), but the absence of any accessible registration record prevents full verification that all pre-specified outcome measures/analyses were reported without selection.

**b. Supporting rationale:**
- The manuscript states that "Refractive outcomes including accuracy, efficacy, and safety were analyzed according to the protocol described by Reinstein and Waring," indicating use of a standardized reporting framework for visual acuity–related outcomes (UCVA for efficacy, CDVA for safety), which reduces (but does not eliminate) concern about selective outcome/analysis reporting, since this is a widely recognized, standardized set of graphs/metrics in refractive surgery (Waring GO. Standard graphs for reporting refractive surgery, J Refract Surg 2000).
- However, no trial registry entry, protocol, or statistical analysis plan was identified/available (per the accompanying trial registration document) to confirm that the specific visual acuity metrics, timepoints, and analyses reported (e.g., 20/25 threshold for efficacy, ≥2-line loss threshold for safety) were pre-specified before unblinded data were available, rather than selected post hoc based on the most favorable presentation (5.1 = No information).
- There is no evidence of multiple alternative visual acuity analyses being conducted and only a subset reported (5.2/5.3 = Probably no, based on the standard, complete set of graphs presented for both efficacy and safety in Figs. 4–5), but the absence of a registry/protocol precludes a definitive "Low risk" judgement.
- Per the algorithm: No information on prespecified plan, with no clear evidence of selection among multiple outcomes/analyses → "Some concerns."
