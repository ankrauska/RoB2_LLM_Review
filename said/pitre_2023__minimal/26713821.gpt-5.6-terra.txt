**Comparison assessed:** conventional medical care plus group nutritional counselling/caloric restriction versus conventional medical care alone, for neuropsychological test performance at 12 months. The assessment addresses the **effect of assignment at baseline**.

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the study is described as randomized with equal group sizes and broadly comparable baseline characteristics, but neither the random-sequence generation method nor allocation concealment is reported.

   b. **Rationale:** The article describes a “**prospective, 1:1, randomized study**” and reports “**Random allocation to conventional medical care alone (n = 40) or together with nutritional counselling (n = 40)**.” However, it does not state how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table) or how allocation was concealed before enrolment.

   Baseline characteristics appear broadly balanced, including age, sex, education, cognitive screening score, comorbidities, physical activity, APOE4 status, and MCI subtype. There is no compelling imbalance suggesting that randomization failed. Nevertheless, under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns even when baseline characteristics appear compatible with chance.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the interventions, because participants and personnel were necessarily aware of group assignment, and there is evidence that trial-context-related behavioural changes reduced the intended contrast between groups in a way likely to affect cognitive outcomes.

   b. **Rationale:** This was not a blinded behavioural intervention. Participants assigned to the intensive group attended “**26 to 28 1-hour meetings**” with nutritionists intended to promote caloric restriction and weight loss. Therefore, participants and the personnel delivering nutritional counselling were aware of assignment.

   The intended separation between groups was not achieved: “**both groups lost similar amounts of weight after the intervention period**” (intensive group mean BMI change −2.1 kg/m²; conventional group −1.3 kg/m²; *P* = .428). The authors explicitly acknowledge a likely trial-context explanation:

   > “**Because the process of recruiting volunteers included information on the risks of obesity, it is also possible that this may have increased motivation to lose weight in both groups.**”

   This represents a plausible deviation arising from the trial context: participants allocated to conventional care may have altered diet/weight-related behaviours because of recruitment and participation, thereby receiving elements of the behavioural change intended to be promoted by counselling. Such contamination or convergence between groups was unbalanced relative to the intended intervention contrast and was likely relevant to neuropsychological performance, since the authors report associations between BMI reduction and improvement in several cognitive domains.

   The group comparison itself was analysed using generalized estimating equations, with the group-by-time interaction interpreted as the intervention effect. Thus, analysis was broadly appropriate for the effect of assignment among participants with observed data. However, the likely trial-context-related dilution of the intervention contrast is sufficient for a high-risk judgement in this domain.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because outcome data were unavailable for 5/80 randomized participants and the reasons for four withdrawals are not reported sufficiently to exclude an association between missingness and cognitive outcome.

   b. **Rationale:** Of 80 randomized participants, 75 completed 12-month follow-up: “**Five women did not complete the follow-up: 4 dropped out and one died of pneumonia**.” The flow chart indicates that 37/40 participants in conventional care and 38/40 in the intensive group were analysed.

   Missing outcome data were therefore relatively limited (6.25%) and were similar between groups (3 versus 2 participants). The death from pneumonia is documented, but the reasons for the four dropouts, and their cognitive status or outcomes, are not described. No sensitivity analysis addressing the possible effect of missing cognitive-test outcomes is reported.

   For continuous neuropsychological outcomes in this small trial, data from 75/80 participants are close to complete but not clearly sufficient to ensure that missing outcomes could not have materially affected the group comparison. It is possible, though not clearly likely, that dropout was related to health status or cognitive performance. This warrants some concerns rather than high risk.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because the neuropsychological outcomes were measured using established instruments at the same planned follow-up time, and the trial registry specifies that outcome assessors were masked.

   b. **Rationale:** The registration record describes the study as “**SINGLE**” masked, with “**OUTCOMES_ASSESSOR**” identified as the masked party. This is particularly important for neuropsychological testing, because although many test scores are standardized, administration and scoring of some assessments can involve judgement.

   The study used a defined battery of established neuropsychological tests, including RAVLT, digit span, Trail Making Tests, Modified Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, verbal fluency, CAMcog, and IQCODE. The same measures were repeated after 12 months: “**All of these tests, except MoCA and IQ, were repeated after 12 months.**” There is no indication that different outcome-measurement methods, thresholds, or follow-up schedules were used between randomized groups.

   Although participants knew whether they attended nutritional counselling, the registry-indicated masking of outcome assessors and use of standardized testing make it unlikely that measurement of neuropsychological performance was materially biased by knowledge of assigned intervention.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the registry identifies neuropsychological test performance as a primary outcome but does not pre-specify the individual instruments, cognitive domains, time points, statistical models, or a hierarchy of analyses; furthermore, the article includes explicitly post-hoc pooled analyses based on BMI change.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial registration lists “**Neuropsychological test performance**” as a primary outcome, but gives no detail on the specific neuropsychological tests, the principal test/domain, the 12-month analysis method, covariate adjustment, handling of missing data, or whether a group-by-time comparison was the primary analysis. No protocol or statistical analysis plan with pre-specified outcome-analysis details is available in the supplied materials.

   The publication reports multiple cognitive measures and analyses. It reports the randomized longitudinal comparison using generalized estimating equations, but subsequently states:

   > “**Because both groups lost similar amounts of weight … we decided to a posteriori pool the longitudinal analysis between groups and to test for the effects of change of BMI as a continuous variable.**”

   The pooled BMI-change analyses are explicitly post hoc and do not estimate the randomized effect of assignment to nutritional counselling versus conventional care. While the paper reports that there was “**no significant time-group interaction for most of the tests after 12 months**,” the generic registered outcome and absence of a pre-specified analysis plan mean it is not possible to verify that the reported neuropsychological result was selected from among multiple eligible tests, domains, time points, and analytical approaches without reference to the findings.

   There is insufficient evidence to conclude that selective reporting definitely occurred; therefore, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk.
