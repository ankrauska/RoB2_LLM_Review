1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial reported a genuinely random, concealed allocation procedure. Participants were assigned “using a randomization automated system on a one-to-one basis,” and “the randomization list was held securely, and released only after study completion.” These procedures support both random sequence generation and allocation concealment. The groups were equal in size at baseline (20 per group), and no important baseline differences were reported: “There were no significant differences in mean age or dosage of methadone and other drugs doses… between the treatment and controls groups at baseline.” The modest baseline SOWS difference (mean 12.6 versus 14.9) is compatible with chance in this small sample and does not indicate a failure of randomization.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and personnel appear to have been blinded: gabapentin and placebo were supplied in “identical appearing capsules,” and the study was described as double-blind. There is no direct evidence that deviations from protocol arose because of the trial context, such as differential co-interventions caused by knowledge of treatment assignment.

   However, the reported analysis did not retain all randomized participants in their assigned groups. Although 20 participants were randomized to each group, three placebo participants were excluded: “one patient in the placebo group was excluded at day 14 and two others were excluded at day 17 because of non-compliance with the scheduled treatment programme and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter (OTC) drugs.” Consequently, outcome data at the final reported assessment were available for 20 gabapentin participants but only 17 placebo participants. The repeated-measures analysis also appears to have been based on the remaining participants rather than all 40 randomized participants (reported degrees of freedom: 5,33).

   Excluding randomized, eligible participants because of non-compliance or use of other medications is not an appropriate analysis for the effect of assignment to gabapentin versus placebo. The exclusions were confined to the placebo group (15% of that group) and occurred in a very small trial of a subjective withdrawal outcome. This could have a substantial effect on the estimated group difference. Therefore, the analysis is not a full intention-to-treat analysis and creates high risk of bias in this domain.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants at all time points. At baseline through day 10, data were available for all 40 participants, but at later assessments placebo-group data were missing for one participant at day 14 and three participants at day 18 (17/20 placebo participants versus 20/20 gabapentin participants).

   The missing data are both differential between groups and plausibly related to the true value of the outcome. The reasons for exclusion were “non-compliance with the scheduled treatment programme and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter (OTC) drugs.” In an opioid-detoxification trial, non-compliance and self-medication are plausibly associated with more severe withdrawal symptoms, craving, or inadequate symptom control. The article itself suggests this possibility, stating that non-compliance in the placebo group “may be due to the greater severity of withdrawal symptoms.”

   No sensitivity analysis or appropriate method for addressing missing data was reported. Since all missing outcome data occurred in the placebo group and may be related to withdrawal severity, the missingness is likely to depend on the unobserved SOWS scores. This creates a high risk of bias.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was severity of subjective opioid withdrawal symptoms measured using the Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS). The scale was described as containing 16 symptoms rated by the patient from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“extremely”), and as “a valid and reliable indicator of the severity of the opiate withdrawal syndrome.” Thus, the measurement method was appropriate for the outcome and was used at the same scheduled assessments in both groups.

   SOWS is a participant-reported, subjective outcome, so participants are the outcome assessors and knowledge of treatment assignment could potentially influence reporting. However, the trial was reported to be double-blind, with “identical appearing capsules” and the same number of capsules in each group. There is no indication that blinding was broken or that outcome collection differed systematically between groups. Therefore, knowledge of intervention received was unlikely to have influenced measurement materially.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication reports SOWS results at multiple stages and provides both a repeated-measures analysis and symptom-specific results. However, no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied information to verify that the reported SOWS outcome, time points, and analytic approach were pre-specified before unblinded data were available.

   There are also inconsistencies in the reported timing of SOWS assessments. The Methods state that SOWS was administered on days 0, 4, 7, 10, 18 and 21, whereas Table 1 reports baseline and days 3, 7, 10, 14 and 18. This discrepancy makes it difficult to determine which outcome measurements and time points were planned and whether the reported set of results was selected after examining the data.

   There is insufficient direct evidence that the reported numerical result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable to either group; indeed, the reported overall finding was non-significant. Nonetheless, in the absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan and given the inconsistent reporting of assessment time points, there are some concerns about selective reporting.
