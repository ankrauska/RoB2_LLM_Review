1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because allocation used a computer-programmed random sequence administered independently by survey-research staff, with concealment maintained until assignment. Baseline characteristics, including baseline BSI scores, were broadly balanced.  
   b. **Rationale:** The report states that randomization was conducted by Survey Research Laboratory staff using “a random allocation sequence programmed into CAPI administration software that allowed for complete allocation concealment up to the point of assignment.” It further states that “both interviewers and respondents had no way of knowing each subject’s study condition until after the assignment had occurred.”  
   
   Allocation was close to the intended 1:1 ratio (276 WRAP; 279 wait-list control). Table 1 reports no statistically significant baseline differences by condition across demographic, diagnostic, service-use, or site variables; baseline BSI Global Severity Index scores were also similar (0.76 versus 0.73). There is no indication that baseline imbalances suggest a problem in the randomization process.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to WRAP versus assignment to the wait-list/usual-care condition. Participants and intervention providers were necessarily aware of assignment, but there is no evidence of outcome-relevant deviations arising specifically because of the trial context that were unbalanced between groups. The analysis was consistent with estimating an assignment effect.  
   b. **Rationale:** This was an open behavioural intervention: WRAP participants attended peer-led classes and control participants were assigned to usual care with delayed access to WRAP. Thus, participants and facilitators were aware of allocation. However, lack of blinding alone does not imply bias in this RoB 2 domain for the effect of assignment.  
   
   The report describes implementation monitoring and high curriculum fidelity: “total course fidelity ranged from 90.3% in wave 1 to 91.7% in wave 5.” Although 43/276 (16%) intervention-assigned participants did not receive WRAP and average attendance was approximately five of eight sessions, non-receipt/adherence is not itself a source of bias for the assignment effect.  
   
   The control group could participate in peer-led support groups, but these appear to have been part of usual care rather than non-protocol interventions caused by the trial context: “control subjects could and did participate in peer-led mental health support groups.” The authors also reran models controlling for such exposure, with results not substantially changed. The primary analysis was described as “intent-to-treat mixed-effects random regression analysis,” with participants analysed by randomized condition. Exclusion related to unavailable outcome data is considered under the missing-data domain.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because BSI outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants, losses were substantial by follow-up, and missingness was plausibly related to symptom severity or health status in this population.  
   b. **Rationale:** Of 555 randomized participants, only 519 were included in the analysed sample: 251/276 (90.9%) in WRAP and 268/279 (96.1%) in control. The report states that 25 intervention participants and 11 control participants were lost to follow-up. This imbalance in complete follow-up/analysis eligibility is concerning.  
   
   Availability of BSI data declined further over time. For the BSI Global Severity Index, data were available for 224/251 analysed WRAP participants and 234/268 analysed controls at the first post-intervention assessment, and for 220/251 and 228/268, respectively, at the 6-month follow-up. Relative to all randomized participants, this corresponds to outcome data at the final follow-up for approximately 80% (220/276) of WRAP-assigned and 82% (228/279) of control-assigned participants.  
   
   Reasons given for loss included “death or ill health, moving away from the area, and formal withdrawal from the study.” In a trial involving people with severe and persistent mental illness and a self-reported psychiatric-symptom outcome, ill health, withdrawal, and inability to complete follow-up could plausibly be associated with the unobserved BSI score. The report does not provide an analysis demonstrating robustness to plausible missing-not-at-random assumptions. Although follow-up completion percentages among the analysed sample did not statistically differ between groups, absence of a statistically significant difference does not establish that missingness was unrelated to psychiatric symptom severity.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because psychiatric symptom severity was measured by an unblinded participant-reported instrument, and participants’ knowledge of having received the peer-led WRAP intervention versus being wait-listed was likely to influence symptom reporting.  
   b. **Rationale:** The BSI was a self-report outcome: “The primary outcome was reduction of psychiatric symptom severity, measured by the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), a patient self-report research instrument.” Participants rated how much they had been bothered by symptoms during the preceding week. Therefore, the participant—not the telephone interviewer—was the relevant outcome assessor.  
   
   Although the telephone interviewers were blinded, this does not blind participants to their own allocation. The report confirms interviewer blinding—“interviewers were blinded to respondents’ study condition”—but WRAP participants necessarily knew they had attended a structured, peer-delivered recovery programme, while controls knew they were in a waiting-list condition. For this subjective participant-reported outcome, expectations, perceived benefit, disappointment with delayed treatment, or social desirability could reasonably affect BSI responses. The authors explicitly recognize this limitation: “the study relied on participant self-report data that were uncorroborated by clinicians or objective observers.”  
   
   The same BSI instrument and scheduled time points were used in both groups, so there is no indication of different measurement procedures between groups. Nevertheless, the likely influence of knowledge of allocation on a subjective self-reported psychiatric-symptom outcome warrants a high-risk judgement.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because no prospectively finalized, sufficiently detailed statistical analysis plan is available to verify that the reported BSI result and analysis were selected before unblinded outcome data were available. The registry was submitted after the trial had started and after primary completion, and it does not fully resolve the multiplicity of BSI measures and analyses.  
   b. **Rationale:** The publication states that “all outcomes, hypotheses, and statistical analyses presented here were prespecified at the time the proposal was submitted to the federal government for funding consideration.” However, the funding proposal or a dated statistical analysis plan was not provided. This statement therefore cannot establish that the specific reported analysis was finalized before outcome data became available.  
   
   The ClinicalTrials.gov record was first submitted and posted in December 2009, whereas the trial started in December 2007 and primary completion was in September 2009. Thus, the registry cannot serve as prospective evidence of the final analysis intentions. It specifies BSI Positive Symptom Score as a primary outcome at baseline, 8 weeks, and 6 months, whereas the publication reports both BSI Positive Symptom Total and BSI Global Severity Index, using longitudinal mixed-effects condition-by-time analyses.  
   
   Both BSI outcomes are reported in the article, and there is no direct evidence that the reported BSI result was selected because it was statistically significant. However, in the absence of a prospectively dated detailed analysis plan specifying the BSI scale/index, analytic model, and handling of repeated measurements and missing data, selective reporting cannot be confidently excluded.
