**Assessment scope:** individually randomized, three-arm parallel trial. This assessment considers the comparison of the **percentile incentive schedule versus fixed-criterion incentive schedule** for smoking abstinence, defined objectively as a breath CO sample **<3 ppm**, and the effect of assignment at baseline.

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that hard-to-treat participants were “**randomly assigned to one of three groups: percentile, fixed criterion, or random incentives**,” and describes allocation in blocks/cohorts of five: “**randomly assigning 2 participants to the percentile group, 2 to the fixed criterion group and 1 to the random incentives group from each group of 5 participants completing intake.**” This is compatible with a random allocation sequence, although the precise method used to randomize the order of assignments within each group of five is not reported.

   Allocation concealment is not described. In particular, it is unclear who generated the allocation sequence, whether the investigator enrolling participants could predict the next allocation, or whether an independent/central allocation method or sequentially numbered opaque envelopes were used. Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns unless adequate concealment can reasonably be inferred.

   Baseline characteristics were generally similar, although there was one statistically significant imbalance in education: “**A higher proportion of percentile incentive participants achieved [Vocational/Technical School/Community College] relative to fixed criterion participants**” (\(\chi^2=8.46\), *p*<0.01). A single imbalance among numerous baseline variables can occur by chance and does not, by itself, demonstrate a failure of randomization. Thus, the main concern is incomplete reporting of sequence generation and, especially, allocation concealment.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew their assigned incentive contingency, and personnel delivering payments were also likely aware of allocation. For example, immediately after randomization, participants were told the contingency for their assigned group: percentile participants were told that the CO criterion would change to help them smoke less, whereas fixed-criterion participants were told that incentives could be earned “**only for not smoking**.”

   However, awareness is not sufficient for bias in the effect of assignment to intervention. The different incentive contingencies were the intended randomized interventions, rather than non-protocol co-interventions or implementation failures. The report provides no evidence that knowledge of group assignment caused additional deviations from protocol that were inconsistent with the assigned intervention and likely to affect CO-confirmed abstinence.

   For the result concerning whether participants produced at least one CO-confirmmed abstinent sample during treatment, the analysis includes all randomized participants in the relevant groups: 23/36 percentile and 25/40 fixed-criterion participants. This is consistent with estimating an effect of assignment.  

   **Qualification:** Some reported analyses were restricted to participants who had already achieved at least one abstinent CO sample—for example, analyses of the total number of abstinent samples and maintenance of abstinence. Such post-randomization restriction would not be an appropriate analysis for the effect of assignment at baseline and would raise serious concerns if that conditional result, rather than the all-randomized-participant abstinence result, were the target result.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** CO data were not available at every planned assessment. Participants were expected to provide samples on each workday for 60 visits, but the mean numbers of submitted samples were only:

   - Percentile: “**42 (SD = 22)**” of 60;
   - Fixed criterion: “**40 (24)**” of 60;
   - Random incentives: “**52 (19)**” of 60.

   Thus, approximately 30%–33% of planned CO measurements were missing on average in the two principal comparison groups, with substantial variability. The event-record figure explicitly distinguishes “**missed visits**” from samples with CO ≥3 ppm or <3 ppm.

   The study does not report a method that adequately addresses missing CO outcomes, such as an analysis treating missing smoking-status assessments conservatively, multiple imputation justified by observed predictors, inverse-probability weighting, or sensitivity analyses under plausible assumptions about smoking status at missed visits. It also does not report reasons for missing visits by randomized group.

   In smoking-cessation studies, failure to attend for biochemical verification can plausibly be related to continued smoking or failure to maintain abstinence. Given the substantial quantity of missing CO assessments and the absence of analyses demonstrating robustness to plausible smoking outcomes among those missing assessments, it is likely that missingness could depend on the true abstinence outcome. This creates a high risk of bias for an outcome based on repeated CO-confirmed abstinence or abstinence maintenance.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Smoking abstinence was measured using an objective biochemical outcome: “**We used an abstinence breath CO criterion of < 3 ppm**.” Breath samples were obtained using “**Vitalograph CO monitors**,” with a standardized collection procedure: participants took a deep breath, held it for 20 seconds, then expired into the monitor over 20 seconds; “**The peak breath CO reading was taken as the participant’s breath CO level.**”

   The same CO threshold and device-based procedure were used across groups. Although participants and study staff were aware of the incentive schedule, the outcome is an instrument-generated CO reading rather than a subjective participant- or clinician-rated outcome. There is no indication that measurement procedures, thresholds, or ascertainment opportunities systematically differed between the percentile and fixed-criterion groups. Consequently, knowledge of allocation is unlikely to have materially influenced whether a sample was classified as <3 ppm.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication reports several related abstinence outcomes and analyses, including: proportion with at least one abstinent sample, time to first abstinent sample, total number of abstinent samples among those who achieved abstinence, consecutive abstinent samples, maintenance following the first abstinent sample, and incentive earnings. It also presents results both across all three groups and specifically for the percentile versus fixed-criterion comparison.

   The article does not provide a protocol or a statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the exact CO <3 ppm result assessed here—including its time window, outcome metric, treatment of missed visits, and analysis population—was specified before unblinded outcome data were available. The available text also describes a number of analyses conditional on post-randomization abstinence initiation, which increases the number of potentially eligible analytic choices.

   There is no clear evidence that the reported CO-confirmed abstinence result was selected because it was statistically significant or favorable to a particular intervention. Therefore, high risk of bias cannot be concluded. Nevertheless, in the absence of a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan or registry information confirming the exact measurement and analysis, RoB 2 indicates **some concerns** about selection of the reported result.
