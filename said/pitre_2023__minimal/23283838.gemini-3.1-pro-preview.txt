**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Low risk of bias.
b. Rationale: Participants were randomly assigned to treatment groups by a data manager who had no interaction with them, ensuring the sequence was concealed. Although there was a baseline difference in age between the groups, this is compatible with chance in a small sample size (N=34) and the authors verified it did not explain the group effects. The study used identical-appearing capsules to maintain blinding.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**
a. Judgement: Some concerns.
b. Rationale: Participants and personnel were successfully blinded to the intervention ("identical-appearing capsules", "neither participants nor experimenters were able to discern assignment"). Therefore, deviations arising from the trial context were unlikely. However, the authors explicitly state they did not conduct an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis ("Only those participants completing Visit 2 are analyzed... It is also unclear what the meaning of an intent to treat analysis would be"). While the number of excluded participants was small (4 out of 34) and evenly distributed, the failure to analyze all randomized participants raises some concerns for the effect of assignment. 

**3. missing outcome data: some concerns**
a. Judgement: Some concerns.
b. Rationale: Data were not available for all participants, as 4 out of 34 randomized participants (11.8%) did not complete Visit 2 and were excluded from the analysis (2 from the DCS group, 2 from the placebo group). While the proportions and reasons for missingness (unreachable, preferred not to continue) were balanced between the groups, missingness could potentially depend on the true value of the outcome (e.g., highly anxious participants choosing not to return for the second speech). No sensitivity analyses were reported to prove the missing data did not bias the results.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Low risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The outcome was between-session habituation based on the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS), which is a participant-reported measure. Because the trial successfully blinded participants to their assigned intervention, the outcome assessors (the participants themselves) were unaware of the intervention received. Therefore, the assessment of the outcome could not have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
a. Judgement: Some concerns.
b. Rationale: While the reported outcome (between-session habituation using SUDS) aligns with the stated objectives of assessing exposure facilitation, there is insufficient information provided in the text (without access to a date-stamped, pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial registry) to definitively confirm that the data were analyzed strictly in accordance with a plan finalized before unblinded data were available. Generating categorical thresholds (e.g., "a drop of at least 5 points") can sometimes be subject to post-hoc selection, warranting some concerns.
