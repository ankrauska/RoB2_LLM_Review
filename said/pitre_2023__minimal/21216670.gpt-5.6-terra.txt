1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial used an adequately random sequence and allocation concealment. Randomisation was stratified/balanced by centre using computer-generated random numbers and a fixed block size, with assignment performed by the coordinating-centre pharmacist. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered opaque envelopes. Baseline differences—such as slightly older age, more previous strokes, and a higher baseline FMMS score in the fluoxetine group—were modest and plausible by chance in this relatively small trial; there is no evidence of a serious failure or manipulation of randomisation. The primary analysis also adjusted for centre, age, history of stroke, and baseline FMMS score.

   Relevant reporting includes: “Randomisation was balanced by centre, with an allocation sequence based on a block size of four, generated with a computer random-number generator by the pharmacist at the coordinating centre.” Further, “Allocation was concealed by use of sequentially numbered opaque envelopes.” The report states that the groups were “well balanced in terms of baseline and demographic characteristics and stroke severity,” while noting modest imbalances in age, previous stroke, and baseline FMMS.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to fluoxetine versus placebo at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants, carers, site staff, and investigators were masked to treatment assignment, and placebo capsules were identical in appearance and packaging to fluoxetine capsules. Thus, there is little reason to expect deviations arising from the trial context, such as differential co-intervention or differential implementation of rehabilitation, to have biased the assignment effect. All participants received physiotherapy and standard stroke-unit care; although rehabilitation procedures varied between centres, this was part of usual local care and centre was included in the adjusted analysis.

   The primary analysis was a full-analysis-set analysis including all randomized participants with outcome data, analysed according to assigned group (57/59 fluoxetine and 56/59 placebo). For the assignment effect, exclusion solely because outcome data were missing is treated as a modified ITT approach and is appropriate for this domain; implications of the five missing day-90 outcomes are considered under missing outcome data.

   The article reports: “Patients, carers, all site study staff, and investigators were masked to treatment assignment.” It also reports that “Fluoxetine and placebo capsules were both prepared by the pharmacist,” and that “The placebo was identical to the active drug in appearance and packaging.” Treatment adherence was similar: mean relative dose intensity was 94.8% in the fluoxetine group and 94.0% in the placebo group. The protocol-specified approach to depression, including possible open-label fluoxetine while maintaining the blind, does not by itself indicate an unplanned, trial-context deviation.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data for the primary FMMS progression outcome were available for 113 of 118 randomized participants (95.8%): 57/59 in the fluoxetine group and 56/59 in the placebo group. For a continuous outcome such as FMMS change, this amount of missing data is sufficiently small that it is unlikely that the missing values could materially alter the estimated intervention effect. Missingness was also similar between groups: two participants in the fluoxetine group and three in the placebo group did not contribute day-90 primary-outcome data.

   The trial profile shows that, in the fluoxetine group, one participant died and one withdrew; in the placebo group, one participant died and two withdrew. Reasons included respiratory distress after food inhalation, severe hypoxia, septic shock, kidney tumour, and pulmonary embolism. These serious clinical events could be related to prognosis, but their very small and broadly balanced number means that a material effect on the continuous FMMS comparison is unlikely. No sensitivity analysis for missing FMMS outcomes was reported, but it is not essential given the near-complete follow-up.

   The report states: “118 patients were randomly assigned… and 113 were included in the analysis.” It further states: “Two patients died before day 90 and three withdrew from the study.”

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** FMMS is an established motor-function measure with reported good validity and intra-rater/inter-rater reliability. It was measured at the same scheduled time points in both groups (baseline, day 30, and day 90) by physiotherapists. Most importantly, physicians assessing outcomes, participants, carers, site staff, and investigators were masked to allocation. Therefore, differential outcome assessment related to knowledge of intervention assignment is unlikely.

   The report describes FMMS as “widely used for assessment of motor recovery after stroke” with “excellent intra-rater and inter-rater reliability and validity.” It also states that “All motor assessments were made by a physiotherapist at day 0 (baseline) and then 30 days and 90 days after enrolment.” Masking was explicit: “Participants, carers, and physicians assessing the outcome were masked to group assignment,” and, in the methods, “Patients, carers, all site study staff, and investigators were masked to treatment assignment.”

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The registered primary outcome was consistent with the published primary outcome: the registration specifies “Progression in the Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale” at 3 months, while the publication specifies the change in FMMS between baseline and day 90. This agreement reduces concern that an entirely different primary outcome or time point was selected after seeing results.

   However, the available registry record does not provide a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan for the exact reported result. In particular, it does not pre-specify the detailed analytic model, covariate adjustment, handling of missing data, or whether the reported adjusted analysis of FMMS change was the sole planned eligible analysis. The trial began in March 2005, whereas it was first posted on ClinicalTrials.gov in April 2008, so the registration was not prospective relative to trial initiation. There is also no publicly available dated SAP in the supplied materials demonstrating that the analysis approach was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The publication reports both unadjusted day-90 FMMS summaries and an adjusted analysis of change from baseline, with adjustment for “centre, age, history of stroke, and FMMS score at inclusion.” Although there is no direct evidence that the reported favourable analysis was selected based on its result, the absence of a sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan leaves some concern about selection among eligible analyses.
