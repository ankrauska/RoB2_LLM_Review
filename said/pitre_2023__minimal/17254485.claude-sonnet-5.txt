# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Rate of Emergency Hemostasis

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to inadequate reporting of allocation concealment despite adequate sequence generation.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation (adequate):** "A random number table was produced through Microsoft Excel. The patients were divided into a control group and a therapy group following the random number table." This indicates a random component (Signalling question 1.1 = Yes).
- **Allocation concealment (no information):** There is no description of how the allocation sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants. No mention of sealed envelopes, central randomization, or any concealment mechanism. This is a critical gap given that this is an open-label, unblinded procedural trial where knowledge of upcoming allocation could influence enrollment decisions (e.g., selectively enrolling patients with more severe active bleeding into one arm). (Signalling question 1.2 = No information)
- **Baseline imbalance:** The baseline table shows groups were well balanced (all P>0.05 for sex, age, active bleeding status, red sign, Child-Pugh score, portal vein width, number and degree of varices, GOV classification), suggesting no gross imbalance indicative of randomization failure (Signalling question 1.3 = No).
- Per the RoB2 algorithm: No information on concealment + no evidence of baseline imbalance → **Some concerns**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias due to lack of blinding combined with a real possibility that non-protocol interventions (crossover treatment) arose because of the trial context and clearly affected the emergency hemostasis outcome, unbalanced between groups.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Awareness of intervention assignment:** This was an open-label procedural trial — participants, endoscopists, and clinical staff were necessarily aware of which combined intervention (simultaneous vs. staged embolization/EVL) was being used, as this determined the physical actions taken during the endoscopic procedure. (Signalling questions 2.1/2.2 = Yes)
- **Deviations arising from trial context affecting the outcome:** In the control group, patients with active esophageal bleeding not controlled by embolization alone crossed over to receive EVL rescue during the same admission: "Hemorrhaging in 10 patients among the 20 patients with active esophageal variceal bleeding was not controlled and EVL was performed for the patients whose hemorrhage was not controllable. Hemorrhaging was controlled after EVL." This rescue EVL is a deviation from the assigned (control/staged) protocol and directly determines whether "emergency hemostasis" was achieved—the very outcome being assessed.
- **Effect on outcome and balance:** This deviation clearly affected the outcome (hemostasis was only achieved after the rescue intervention was given) and was unbalanced between groups by design — the therapy group did not need such rescue because EVL was already part of the intervention. This is not a "non-adherence" issue outside investigator control; it reflects the fundamental design/context of managing an emergent bleeding condition, and it inflates apparent hemostasis rates in the control group actually achieved by administering the experimental co-intervention.
- Because the analysis (as reported) appears to count these rescued patients as part of the "controlled" hemostasis outcome for the control group, and this practice was inherent to trial conduct rather than randomization assignment, there is a high risk that the assignment effect is contaminated.
- Per algorithm: awareness of assignment (Yes) + deviations arising from trial context (Yes) + affecting outcome (Yes) + unbalanced between groups (Yes) → **High risk of bias**.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias; outcome data appear complete for all randomized participants.

**b. Rationale:**
- All 90 patients in the therapy group and all 82 patients in the control group (172 total) appear to have complete emergency hemostasis outcome data reported: "Hemorrhaging was controlled in all 90 patients in the therapy group, the rate of emergency hemostasis was 100.0%. In the control group, hemorrhaging was controlled in 72 out of 82 patients and the rate of emergency hemostasis was 87.8%."
- There is no mention of loss to follow-up, withdrawal, or exclusion for this immediate procedural outcome, which is assessed at the time of intervention (an outcome not vulnerable to attrition over time).
- (Signalling question 3.1 = Yes) → **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias because outcome ascertainment methods differed between groups in a manner intertwined with the intervention itself, and assessors were not (and could not be) blinded.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Method of measurement:** "Emergency hemostasis" is an observer-reported outcome requiring some clinical judgment (endoscopic assessment of bleeding cessation), assessed by the treating endoscopist who was not blinded (open-label design, as procedural interventions cannot be masked). (Signalling question 4.1 = No; but 4.3 = Yes, assessors aware)
- **Differential ascertainment between groups:** Critically, in the control group, the definition/ascertainment of "hemostasis" success incorporated a rescue procedure (EVL) that was not part of the original assigned control intervention: "EVL was performed for the patients whose hemorrhage was not controllable. Hemorrhaging was controlled after EVL." This means measurement of the outcome variable itself differed systematically between groups, since the control group's hemostasis outcome partially reflects an added procedure absent from the original protocol comparison for that arm. This constitutes ascertainment that "could have differed between intervention groups" (Signalling question 4.2 = Yes).
- Additionally, unblinded assessors delivering the intervention have a plausible motivation (vested interest, given they are also study investigators) to judge/report hemostasis success favorably, particularly influencing borderline judgment calls at the time of endoscopy.
- Per algorithm: measurement differed between groups (Yes) → **High risk of bias**, regardless of answers to subsequent blinding questions.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, as no trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan was identified/available for this study to confirm that the reported result for emergency hemostasis was not selected from multiple analyses or definitions.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registration information (doc_id: 4dab5cab-2b44-42f8-8559-2dd9073b18b5) was referenced in task instructions but no corresponding registry record content was provided/found matching this specific trial (conducted 2000-2006, published 2007), which is unsurprising as prospective trial registration was not widely mandated for trials of this era, especially in this context/region.
- The outcome "rate of emergency hemostasis" is reported using a single, clear, non-ambiguous binary definition (bleeding controlled vs. not) with a single time point (during the index hospitalization/procedure), analyzed via chi-square test — there is no evidence of multiple alternative definitions, cut-points, or analytic approaches being tried and selectively reported for this particular outcome.
- However, in the absence of a pre-specified/registered protocol or statistical analysis plan against which to verify this, we cannot fully rule out selective reporting of definitions or analyses (Signalling questions 5.1 = No information; 5.2 = No information; 5.3 = No information).
- Per algorithm: no information on pre-specification and no information on selection from multiple outcomes/analyses → **Some concerns**.
