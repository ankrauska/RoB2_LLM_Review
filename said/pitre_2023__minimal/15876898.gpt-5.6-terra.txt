1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The report describes the study as randomized and states that “*the randomization was carried out confidentially by the clinic administration*.” However, it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers) or how allocation was concealed until enrolment and assignment. Thus, adequate random sequence generation and allocation concealment cannot be confirmed.  
   
   The baseline characteristics presented in Table 1 appear broadly comparable between groups, including age, height, initial weight, olanzapine-associated weight gain, duration of olanzapine therapy, olanzapine dose, and diagnostic category. There is no clear baseline imbalance suggesting that randomization failed. Nevertheless, missing information on the sequence-generation and concealment methods leads to some concerns rather than low risk of bias.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to topiramate versus placebo at baseline. The trial was described as double blind: “*Subjects received blinded medication daily*” and “*Both subjects and clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment*.” Therefore, participants and personnel were unlikely to have knowingly altered treatment, co-interventions, or care because of treatment assignment.  
   
   Olanzapine and concomitant medication doses were reportedly held constant: “*During the study, the doses of olanzapine and the concomitant medications … remained constant.*” The report does not identify important deviations from the intended protocol that arose because of the trial context or that were different between groups.  
   
   Although six placebo-group participants were excluded because they missed more than one assessment, this concerns missing outcome data rather than a deviation from the intended intervention. The analysed participants remained classified according to their randomized groups; there is no indication of an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis based on treatment adherence.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not available for all randomized participants. The study planned to allocate 25 women to topiramate and 24 to placebo, but the final analysis included 25 and 18 participants, respectively. The report states: “*Six subjects who failed to appear for more than one evaluation (all from the placebo group) dropped out of the study, and their data were not further analyzed.*” Thus, all missing data occurred in the placebo group: 6/24 placebo participants (25%), compared with 0/25 topiramate participants.  
   
   This differential attrition is substantial for a small trial and could materially affect the estimated difference in weight. Moreover, failure to attend repeated weight assessments may plausibly be related to participants’ weight trajectory, dissatisfaction with assigned treatment, perceived lack of benefit, or other health-related factors associated with the outcome. No appropriate imputation, sensitivity analysis, or other analysis addressing the potential impact of missing weight data was reported.  
   
   Since missingness was differential between groups and may have depended on the unobserved weight outcome, the result is at high risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Body weight is an objective outcome, and participants were weighed at scheduled visits: “*Every 2 weeks, subjects were seen, weighed, and tested*.” The same method and schedule appear to have been used in both intervention groups.  
   
   In addition, participants and clinicians were blinded to allocation: “*Both subjects and clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment.*” Therefore, outcome measurement is unlikely to have differed systematically between groups or to have been influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment. There is no indication that weighing procedures or equipment were inappropriate for measuring body weight.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies “*weight checks*” among the primary outcome measures, but does not specify a single prespecified weight endpoint, analysis metric, or time point that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. Weight was measured repeatedly every two weeks over 10 weeks, creating multiple potentially eligible time points. The report emphasizes the week-10 result and presents a figure showing weight over time, but does not provide a prespecified statistical analysis plan or a protocol detailing the intended primary weight analysis.  
   
   The supplied material does not provide sufficient information from the trial registration to verify that the analysed week-10 weight result, its analytic approach, and the handling of missing data were prespecified before outcome data were unblinded. There is also no clearly documented prespecified approach for selecting among possible analyses of repeated weight measurements (e.g., final weight, change from baseline, repeated-measures analysis, or differing methods for missing data).  
   
   There is no direct evidence that the reported weight result was selected because it was statistically significant; therefore, high risk of bias is not justified. However, the absence of a verifiable prospective analysis plan and the availability of multiple measurement time points result in some concerns.
