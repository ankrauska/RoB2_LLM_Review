1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants were described as “randomly allocated to one of four study conditions,” but the report does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated or how allocation was concealed until enrolment. A statement that participants were randomized, without details of a random component, is insufficient to establish low risk of bias under RoB 2.

   Baseline comparability for important prognostic factors was also not adequately established. In particular, participants were not pretested for relevant prior knowledge: the authors acknowledge that they “did not screen our participants for prior knowledge and did not pretest them” and that they “cannot conclusively rule out an imbalance between the conditions or professional groups.” Although professional-group distributions were reported and appear broadly similar, this does not rule out imbalance in prior diagnostic or basic-science knowledge, which is particularly relevant to delayed diagnostic performance. There is no evidence of a clear, severe baseline imbalance, but inadequate reporting of sequence generation, concealment, and key baseline prognostic characteristics precludes a low-risk judgement.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew the instructional condition to which they had been assigned because the learning materials visibly differed (science-based causal explanation, basic-science-first, clinical-features-first, or clinical-feature list). However, there is no evidence that awareness caused deviations from the assigned educational intervention arising specifically from the trial context. Thus, awareness alone does not create high risk for the assignment effect.

   The major concern is the analysis. The authors excluded two participants after randomization because they spent less than five minutes on the delayed diagnostic test and had very low delayed-test scores:

   > “One participant from the BC and CB group each spent less than five minutes on the delayed diagnostic test. Upon inspection, these participants’ performance was three standard deviations below the mean performance for their respective groups, with 1 participant scoring 0% and the other scoring 8% …”

   These participants apparently completed the delayed outcome assessment, so their exclusion was not simply due to missing outcome data. It was a post-randomization exclusion based on test-taking behaviour and extremely low outcome performance. This is not an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis of the effect of assignment. The exclusions occurred in the BC and CB groups, which could materially affect the estimated between-condition difference in a small study with approximately 21–25 participants per group. Therefore, there was potential for a substantial impact on the delayed diagnostic-performance result.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Delayed diagnostic-performance data were not available for all randomized participants. Five enrolled participants did not complete the delayed study session:

   > “Five enrolled participants failed to complete the delayed session of the study … and were excluded from the analysis.”

   In addition, two participants who completed delayed testing were excluded from the analysis because of very short completion time and extremely low scores. Consequently, the delayed-outcome analysis was not based on all randomized participants.

   The amount and distribution of missing/excluded data are concerning in a small trial. Attrition was reported as occurring in the BC and CB groups, and the two further outcome-based exclusions were also one each from BC and CB. Thus, missingness/exclusion was not balanced across all conditions. Moreover, exclusion of participants with very low delayed diagnostic scores directly indicates that the absence of their outcomes from the analysis depended on, or was strongly associated with, the true outcome value. No sensitivity analysis was reported to demonstrate that the condition-by-time result was robust to the missing/excluded data.

   There are also internal inconsistencies in the reported final sample size: the text states that the final sample was 94, whereas the stated group sizes (“25 in the FL, 21 in the CB, 24 in BC, and 25 in the SC groups”) sum to 95. This further limits confidence that outcome availability and the analysis population were fully and transparently reported.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Delayed diagnostic performance was measured using a standardized computer-administered diagnostic test comprising clinical vignettes with four diagnostic options. The outcome was the number/percentage of correct answers, which is an objectively scored performance outcome with little scope for assessor judgement. The authors state:

   > “The diagnostic test consisted of 12 clinical vignettes for which students had to identify the correct diagnosis from a list of four possible disorders.”

   The test cases were created by an expert clinician, independently reviewed by another clinician, and had reportedly been used in prior related studies. Alternate test versions were counterbalanced between immediate and delayed testing, and the same testing procedures were applied across randomized groups.

   Participants were aware of their instructional condition, but this does not plausibly bias the *measurement* of whether a multiple-choice answer was correct. Awareness may affect actual test performance, which is part of the intervention effect, rather than differential outcome measurement. There is no indication that scoring procedures or ascertainment differed between groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies delayed diagnostic performance as part of its primary diagnostic-test analysis and reports the condition-by-time interaction, group-level immediate and delayed scores, and post hoc comparisons. It also describes the intended primary analysis:

   > “The primary analysis was performance on the diagnostic test using a mixed, repeated-measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with group … as the between-subjects factor and test … as the repeated factor.”

   Nevertheless, there is insufficient information to confirm that the specific reported delayed diagnostic-performance result and analytic approach were prespecified in an analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. No protocol or statistical analysis plan is provided in the article, and the supplied material does not contain the referenced trial-registration record.

   The outcome could reasonably have been analysed in several eligible ways—for example, delayed score alone, change from immediate to delayed testing, the condition-by-time interaction, adjusted or unadjusted analyses, and differing handling of the participants with missing or excluded delayed outcomes. The report also describes several outcomes and analyses, including memory-test results, diagnostic-test results, time-on-task variables, covariate selection based on observed correlations, and post hoc least-significant-difference comparisons. While this does not demonstrate that the reported result was selected because of its findings, the absence of a verifiable prospective analysis plan leaves some concern about selection among outcome measurements and analyses.
