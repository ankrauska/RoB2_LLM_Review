1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The report states that patients “were randomly selected for insertion of the 0.014 or 0.016 inch wires,” but does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated. It also provides no information on allocation concealment before assignment. Thus, it cannot be confirmed that a truly random sequence was used or that recruiters could not foresee allocations. No baseline characteristics by treatment group are reported that would allow assessment for important baseline imbalance. The unequal group sizes (56 versus 53) are compatible with chance and do not themselves indicate a problem, but the absence of information on sequence generation and concealment leads to some concerns rather than low risk.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the initially allocated wire size.  
   b. **Rationale:** The trial compared assignment to a 0.014- versus 0.016-inch initial NiTi archwire. Although blinding is not reported and is unlikely for the clinicians delivering the wires, there is no evidence that deviations from the intended wire assignment arose because of the trial context or differed systematically between groups. The article states that, after allocation, “the archwires… were ligated to all teeth,” and that no “extra-oral appliances, palatal arches or quad-helix appliances were used during the experimental period.”  

   Patients were permitted to use analgesics (“The patients were allowed to take medication when they felt it necessary”), but this appears to be an allowed aspect of care rather than a trial-context-induced deviation from assigned intervention. Moreover, the outcome assessed here is *initial pain*—the time at which pain was first perceived following wire insertion—so subsequent analgesic use is unlikely to materially affect that outcome. The comparison appears to have retained the randomized groups: the initial-pain data account for all randomized patients, including those reporting no pain (56 in the 0.014-inch group and 53 in the 0.016-inch group).

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data on initial pain appear available for all randomized participants. The report explicitly states: “The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned at the following appointment.” The randomized groups comprised 56 and 53 participants, respectively. For initial pain, Table 1 reports timing of pain onset among those who experienced pain, while the text reports that six participants in each group reported no pain. Thus, the reported timing categories plus no-pain responses account for all participants in each group. There is no indication of loss to follow-up, incomplete questionnaires, or exclusions from the initial-pain assessment.

4. **measurement of the outcome: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** Initial pain was a participant-reported, subjective outcome. Participants completed a questionnaire after archwire insertion; question 1 asked “the time at which pain was first perceived after archwire insertion.” This is a reasonable and comparable method for measuring initial pain in both groups, and there is no indication that the timing question or data-collection procedures differed by allocated wire group.  

   However, the participants were the outcome assessors, and the report does not state that participants were blinded to the wire size received. Nor does it provide evidence that they could not identify or infer their allocated intervention. Knowledge or beliefs about the size of the initial wire could potentially influence reporting of a subjective pain outcome. Nevertheless, the interventions were two closely related active archwire sizes, and there is no clear evidence of strong expectations or of differential outcome assessment that would make such influence likely. Therefore, this raises some concerns rather than high risk of bias.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** No prospectively finalized protocol, statistical analysis plan, or usable trial-registration information specifying the prespecified outcome definition, time-point handling, and analysis of initial pain is available in the supplied material. Consequently, it cannot be verified that the reported analysis was specified before unblinded outcome data became available.  

   The publication describes initial pain as an intended study aim—“the time at which pain starts”—and reports the distribution of time to initial pain for both groups. There is no direct evidence that the reported initial-pain result was selected from multiple pain-onset measures or multiple analyses because of its results. However, without a prespecified analysis plan or adequately detailed registration record, selective choice among analytic approaches cannot be excluded. Under RoB 2, this warrants some concerns rather than low risk.
