1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The study reports a genuinely random method of sequence generation: “*Two hundred and forty-one patients were prospectively randomized using computer-generated random numbers into three groups*.” This supports a low risk for generation of the allocation sequence.

   However, allocation concealment is insufficiently described. The report states that women “*were allocated randomly … with sealed envelopes by specialist nurse*,” but does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, opened sequentially, or opened only after irrevocable participant enrolment. Under RoB 2, merely reporting “sealed envelopes” is not enough to establish adequate concealment.

   Baseline characteristics appear balanced across the three groups: age, duration of infertility, BMI, and BMI category were similar (all reported as non-significant). There is no indication that baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization. Nevertheless, because concealment cannot be confirmed, the domain is judged as **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline.

   The trial does not report blinding of participants, carers, clinicians administering treatment, or those delivering IUI. Given that the interventions were different gonadotrophin preparations (Follitropin alfa, urinary FSH, and hMG), participants and treating personnel were likely aware, or could have been aware, of assigned treatment.

   There is no reported evidence that differential co-interventions, treatment changes, or failures of implementation arose specifically because of the trial context and differed between groups. The stimulation and IUI procedures appear protocolized: monitoring was performed with estradiol and ultrasound, doses were adjusted individually, and IUI was performed 36 hours after hCG. However, the report does not provide sufficient information to determine whether deviations from intended treatment occurred because clinicians or participants knew the allocated gonadotrophin.

   For clinical pregnancy, the reported denominators correspond to all randomized participants: 21/81, 11/80, and 10/80. Thus, the analysis appears consistent with an intention-to-treat approach, at least with respect to retaining randomized participants in their assigned groups. Nonetheless, because of lack of information about blinding and trial-context-related deviations, this domain is judged as **some concerns** rather than low risk.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Clinical pregnancy data appear to be available for all randomized participants. The trial randomized 241 women—81 to rFSH, 80 to uFSH, and 80 to hMG—and Table II reports clinical-pregnancy results using precisely these denominators: 21/81 (25.9%), 11/80 (13.8%), and 10/80 (12.5%).

   The article also states that “*only one cycle per patient was performed*” and describes clinical pregnancy ascertainment by transvaginal ultrasound 6 weeks after IUI. Although the flow diagram as reproduced does not clearly provide arm-specific follow-up details, the reported outcome denominators indicate that no randomized participants were omitted from the clinical-pregnancy analysis.

   There is therefore no indication of missing clinical-pregnancy outcome data, differential loss to follow-up, or an analysis restricted to a post-randomization subset. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data is **low**.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Clinical pregnancy was defined using an appropriate and objective method: “*Clinical pregnancy was documented by TV-US, 6 weeks after the IUI.*” Transvaginal ultrasound confirmation of a clinical pregnancy is an appropriate measurement method for this outcome.

   The protocol indicates that the same outcome definition and timing were used across all intervention groups. There is no indication that women assigned to one gonadotrophin preparation had additional or different ultrasound surveillance that could cause differential outcome ascertainment.

   Blinding of ultrasound assessors is not reported. However, clinical pregnancy documented by ultrasound has limited scope for subjective judgement relative to participant-reported outcomes. Knowledge of treatment assignment is unlikely to alter whether an ultrasound-confirmed clinical pregnancy is recorded. Therefore, the risk of bias in measurement of clinical pregnancy is **low**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The publication explicitly identifies clinical pregnancy rate as the primary outcome: “*The primary outcome measure was clinical pregnancy rate.*” It also reports the clinical-pregnancy results for each of the three randomized groups, including event counts and percentages.

   However, no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the provided material. The supplied trial-registration reference contains no accessible registration content in the information provided here; consequently, it is not possible to verify whether the clinical-pregnancy outcome definition, assessment time point, planned comparisons, and analysis methods were specified before unblinded data became available.

   The article defines clinical pregnancy as ultrasound-confirmed pregnancy at 6 weeks after IUI, and there is no direct evidence that this result was selected from multiple eligible clinical-pregnancy definitions, time points, or analyses on the basis of the findings. Nevertheless, absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan prevents a low-risk judgement. Therefore, selective reporting is judged as **some concerns**.
