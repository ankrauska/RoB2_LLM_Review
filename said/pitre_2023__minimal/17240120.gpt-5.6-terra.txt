1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The double-blind phase used a computer-generated allocation sequence and allocation concealment procedures that were unlikely to permit investigators to know the next assignment. The report states that eligible responders were assigned “according to a **computer-generated randomisation list**” and that “the details of the randomisation series were **unknown to any of the investigators** and were contained in a set of **sealed opaque envelopes**.” Patients were enrolled sequentially and assigned the lowest available randomization number.

   The groups were similar at randomization. The authors report that there were “**no differences in demographics and clinical parameters between the two treatment groups in the randomisation period**.” Table 1 shows no material imbalance in age, sex, race, age at onset, or duration of illness. The modest group-size difference (163 escitalopram versus 157 placebo) is compatible with chance.

   Although the envelope procedures are not described in sufficient detail to confirm all safeguards (e.g. sequential numbering and tamper-proof seals), the computer-generated list, opaque envelopes, and investigators’ lack of access to the sequence support a judgement of low risk.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants, investigators, and other study personnel were blinded during the randomized treatment period: “**All study personnel and participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire duration of the study**.” Study tablets were also described as of “**identical appearance, taste or smell**.” Thus, differential co-interventions or deviations arising specifically from knowledge of assigned treatment and the trial context were unlikely.

   The placebo group underwent a planned one-week taper for patients previously receiving escitalopram 20 mg/day. This was part of the intended randomized strategy rather than an unplanned deviation. The investigators also assessed potential discontinuation effects and reported analyses censoring relapses in the first 7 or 14 days after randomization; escitalopram remained superior.

   The primary time-to-relapse analysis was described as using the ITT population and compared randomized groups using log-rank methods and Cox regression. The report defines the ITT population as all randomized patients who took at least one dose and had at least one post-randomization efficacy assessment. Two randomized patients who did not meet the pre-randomization response criterion were excluded; this appears to be exclusion of patients who were ineligible for randomization rather than exclusion due to treatment received, adherence, or post-randomization outcome. There is no evidence of substantial switching, non-protocol treatment, or analysis according to treatment received that would bias the assignment effect.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was analysed as time to relapse, so participants who discontinued without relapse were censored and may have missing subsequent relapse information. Non-relapse withdrawal during the double-blind period was reported for 13/163 (7.9%) escitalopram participants and 14/157 (8.9%) placebo participants. These proportions are reasonably similar, which reduces concern about strongly differential censoring.

   However, the report does not provide adequate outcome-specific detail on the reasons for all censoring/withdrawals, nor does it provide a sensitivity analysis directly addressing whether censoring was informative for relapse. In a relapse-prevention trial, withdrawal may plausibly be related to worsening OCD symptoms, adverse effects, or perceived lack of benefit—all of which could be related to the subsequent probability of relapse. The report’s statement that withdrawals other than relapse were comparable between groups is reassuring but does not establish non-informative censoring.

   Therefore, there is no strong evidence that missing outcome data biased the result, but the available information does not fully exclude this possibility.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Relapse was prospectively defined as either “**an increase in the Y-BOCS total score of ≥5 points from the time of randomisation, or an unsatisfactory treatment effect (lack of efficacy) as judged by the investigator**.” The main component was the Y-BOCS criterion: “**116 out of 119 patients, 97%**” of relapses met this criterion. Thus, the result was overwhelmingly based on a standardized clinician-rated OCD scale rather than the more subjective investigator judgement of lack of efficacy.

   Outcome assessors/investigators were blinded to assigned treatment: “**All study personnel and participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire duration of the study**.” Tablets were indistinguishable, and trained raters were required: “Only those investigators who had actively participated in rater training sessions … were allowed to rate patients.” The same investigator rated each patient whenever possible, further supporting consistency.

   The same relapse definition, measurement schedule, and assessment procedures were used in both randomized groups. Consequently, differential outcome ascertainment or outcome assessment influenced by knowledge of treatment assignment is unlikely.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication explicitly identifies time to relapse as the prospectively defined primary efficacy analysis: “**The prospectively defined primary analysis of efficacy was the time to relapse from the start date of double-blind treatment**.” It reports the corresponding log-rank comparison, Kaplan–Meier curves, and Cox-model hazard ratio. This consistency is reassuring, and the available trial-registration information can be used to corroborate that relapse/time-to-relapse was a planned outcome.

   However, a sufficiently detailed, date-stamped statistical analysis plan finalized before access to unblinded outcome data is not available in the supplied materials. Several potentially eligible analytical choices existed, including the primary composite relapse definition, a post-hoc analysis restricted to Y-BOCS-defined relapse, analyses censoring events in the first 7 or 14 days, ITT and per-protocol populations, and Cox versus log-rank analyses. The main result appears to be the planned ITT time-to-relapse analysis, but the absence of a detailed pre-specified analysis plan means it cannot be confirmed that the reported numerical result was not selected from among multiple eligible outcome definitions or analyses.

   Accordingly, there are some concerns rather than evidence of definite selective reporting.
