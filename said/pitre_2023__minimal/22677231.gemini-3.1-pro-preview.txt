**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Low risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The study used a computerized random allocation sequence ("randomly allocated to the treatment or wait-list group in consecutive order" after leaving their email address). Allocation concealment was maintained as it was an automated online system, preventing investigators from predicting or influencing the assignment. Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the groups ("Randomization was successful: significant differences emerged neither for any of the background nor psychopathological variables").

**2. deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**
a. Judgement: Some concerns.
b. Rationale: Participants were aware of their assigned intervention, as blinding is not feasible for this type of internet-based psychological intervention versus a wait-list control. While the study conducted an appropriate intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis using multiple imputation for missing data, the lack of blinding raises some concerns that deviations from the intended intervention could have arisen due to the trial context (e.g., wait-list participants seeking other treatments, or treatment participants having altered engagement), which could have affected the outcome.

**3. missing outcome data: some concerns**
a. Judgement: Some concerns.
b. Rationale: The overall completion rate was 81.9%, with 85.7% completing in the wait-list group and 78.1% in the Deprexis group. While the researchers used appropriate multiple imputation methods for their intention-to-treat analyses, there remains some concern that missingness in the outcome (depression severity) could depend on its true value (e.g., participants who did not improve or experienced worsening symptoms may have been less likely to complete the follow-up assessment). 

**4. measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**
a. Judgement: High risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The primary outcome (Beck Depression Inventory) is a participant-reported measure. Because participants were aware of their intervention assignment (active online therapy vs. wait-list), their knowledge of the received intervention could have influenced their assessment of their own depressive symptoms. In psychological intervention trials with subjective self-reported outcomes and unblinded participants, there is a high likelihood that the assessment of the outcome was influenced by expectations and knowledge of the intervention received.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
a. Judgement: Some concerns.
b. Rationale: The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01401296); however, the registration date (July 2011) was shortly after the study start date (May 2011). While the primary outcome (BDI) reported in the paper matches the registry, the lack of a prospective, date-stamped statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available raises some concerns regarding the potential for selective analysis reporting.
