1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence appears genuinely random: “*a Microsoft Excel random number generator was used to randomize participants into the two conditions*.” However, the report does not describe allocation concealment (e.g., central allocation, sealed opaque envelopes, or an independent allocator). Thus, it is unclear whether the investigator enrolling participants could have known or predicted the forthcoming assignment.  
   
   Baseline comparisons did not indicate an evident problem with randomization: the authors report no significant pre-intervention differences in insomnia, fatigue, depression, dysfunctional sleep beliefs, or LAT outcomes, and equal group sizes were analysed (44 per group). Nevertheless, absence of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns under RoB 2.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to the fatigue-information versus sleep-control information condition. Participants and the study investigator were necessarily aware of the information condition. However, there is no evidence of non-protocol co-interventions, failures to deliver the allocated information, or differential deviations from the intervention caused by the trial context. The study procedures appear to have been delivered as intended: “*The study investigator read the information sheet alongside each participant individually*.”  
   
   There are concerns regarding analysis of all randomized participants. Five participants’ data were excluded: two because they were younger than 18 years, one because demographic information was incomplete, and two because the outcome measures could not be scored. The final analyses included 88 participants. Exclusion of those aged under 18 may be appropriate if this was a pre-specified eligibility requirement, but this is not clearly stated. Excluding a participant solely for incomplete demographic information, and excluding participants with incomplete LAT data, means that a full intention-to-treat analysis was not conducted. The number excluded was modest, but the reporting does not allow complete confirmation that exclusions could not have materially affected the result.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data for the LAT appear to have been available for nearly all participants. Of the five exclusions, only two were explicitly because they “*did not complete the outcome measures in a way that would allow us to obtain a total score*.” Thus, outcome data were unavailable for only approximately 2% of the apparent recruited/randomized sample (2 of 93). This is sufficiently small for this continuous, immediately assessed outcome that the missing values are unlikely to have made an important difference to the estimated group-by-time effect.  
   
   The reasons for the two unavailable LAT outcomes are not reported by intervention group and there is no indication that missingness was related to participants’ true fatigue-attribution outcomes. Therefore, there is no strong basis to conclude that missing outcome data biased the result.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** The LAT is a participant-reported, study-developed outcome. Participants themselves were the outcome assessors, and they were aware of the information they had just received. Importantly, the intervention explicitly provided the fatigue-information group with alternative, non-sleep-related causes of fatigue, while the control group received generic sleep information. Immediately afterwards, participants completed the same task asking them to list and rate causes of their fatigue. Thus, knowledge of the assigned intervention was very likely to influence how participants completed the outcome measure.  
   
   The authors themselves acknowledge this concern: “*it is also possible that demand characteristics or priming played a role in FI participants’ post-intervention responses on the LAT*,” and state that the information intervention “*may have influenced participants to respond in a certain manner*.” Since the outcome directly measures the cognitions that the intervention explicitly taught participants to report, and assessment occurred during the same one-hour session, bias in measurement of this subjective outcome is likely.  
   
   Although coding of the written responses was undertaken by a volunteer blind to the hypotheses, and the investigator reviewed coding while blind to random assignment, this does not address the principal issue: participants’ generation, ranking, and likelihood ratings of their own attributions could have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article reports all three described LAT outcomes—NumberAttributionScore, FrequencyScore, and ImportanceScore—and gives results for each. This reduces concern that only one favourable LAT result was selected from the three stated outcome scores. The report also describes the intended group-by-time interaction analyses before presenting the results.  
   
   However, there is no accessible prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan or sufficiently detailed trial-registration information in the material provided to verify that the precise LAT measures, time points, scoring decisions, coding rules, handling of exclusions, and analysis models were specified before outcome data were available. The LAT was developed specifically for this study and allowed several possible analytic choices, including classification of responses as sleep-related or non-sleep-related, rank-score transformation, and aggregation of ratings.  
   
   The authors describe a post hoc coding decision for disputed responses—for example, “*uncomfortable bed*” and “*loud roommate during the night*” were classified as sleep-related—and do not provide a pre-specified analysis plan supporting these decisions. There is no direct evidence that the reported results were selected based on their statistical significance, but the lack of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan gives some concerns.
