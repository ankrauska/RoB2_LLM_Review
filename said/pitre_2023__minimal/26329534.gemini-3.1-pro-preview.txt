**1. bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** High risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The study claims participants were randomized, but the text explicitly states that the sample sources differed by intervention group: "A random sample of 7200 pharmacists... provided by the Medical Marketing Service (for the postal mail and hybrid groups) and Statlistics (for the email group), was used." Because the email group was drawn from a completely different database than the postal and hybrid groups, true randomization between all delivery methods did not occur. This structural flaw led to substantial baseline imbalances between the groups, which is evident in Table 1 (e.g., the proportion of Black pharmacists was 3.2% in the postal group versus 26.4% in the email group). These massive imbalances strongly indicate a critical failure in the randomization process.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** Blinding of participants and personnel to the survey delivery method (postal mail, email, hybrid) is inherently impossible. However, there is no evidence that deviations from the intended interventions arose due to the trial context. The outcome is the response rate itself, and the analysis appropriately compared the groups based on the initial assignment (adjusted for undeliverable surveys, which is a standard and appropriate practice for calculating survey response rates). 

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The primary outcome being assessed is the "response rate". By definition, the outcome is a binary measure of whether a participant responded completely or not. The researchers had the full denominator of eligible participants (those who successfully received the survey) and observed the exact numerator (those who returned a fully completed survey). Therefore, there is no missing outcome data for the outcome of "response rate."

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The method of measuring the outcome (counting the number of returned, completed surveys divided by the number of successfully delivered surveys) is objective and appropriate. Although the outcome assessors (the researchers calculating the rates) were aware of the intervention assignments, the outcome is a simple, objective count that cannot plausibly be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
* **Judgement:** Some concerns.
* **Rationale:** There is no mention of a pre-published protocol, trial registration, or pre-specified statistical analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded data were available. While the reported outcome (response rate) is standard for this type of methodological research and does not appear to have been selectively chosen from multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses, the absence of a pre-specified plan means we cannot definitively rule out selective reporting.
