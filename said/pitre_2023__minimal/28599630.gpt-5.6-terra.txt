**Outcome assessed:** prevalence of depressive symptoms, defined as EPDS score ≥11, at 4–6 weeks postpartum (T3), comparing omega-3 fish-oil supplementation with soybean-oil placebo. The effect of interest is the effect of **assignment** to fish oil versus placebo at baseline.

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study is described as randomized and allocation was performed by a researcher who was not involved in data collection: “*The randomization was performed by a researcher not involved in the data collection using the participant identification (subject ID) after stratification for EPDS score and previous history of depression.*” Stratification on prognostic variables is compatible with appropriate randomization. However, the report does not specify how the random allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, or random permuted blocks), nor does it clearly state how allocation was concealed before enrolment/assignment.

   Baseline characteristics were broadly similar, with the only borderline difference being a higher proportion of non-white women in the placebo group (82.1% versus 59.4%; *P* = 0.055). This isolated difference is compatible with chance and does not itself suggest a failure of randomization. Nevertheless, because concealment of allocation is insufficiently described, the randomization-process domain has some concerns.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** This judgement concerns the effect of assignment to intervention, rather than the effect of adherence. Participants, research assistants, and technicians were reportedly blinded: “*Participants and all research assistants and technicians responsible for running both the cohort study and the RCT were blinded to the study allocation.*” The fish-oil and placebo capsules were “*identical regarding color, shape, size and packing*” and were deodorized, supporting successful blinding.

   There is no evidence that deviations from the assigned intervention arose because of the trial context and differed between groups in a way likely to affect postpartum depressive symptoms. Adherence was imperfect (mean consumption approximately 74% in both groups), and some women did not receive or discontinued the intervention. However, non-adherence or discontinuation alone does not bias an effect-of-assignment estimate unless it arose because of the trial context in a differential manner. Adherence was similar between groups: 74.4% in the fish-oil group and 73.5% in the control group (*P* = 0.903).

   The authors report an intention-to-treat analysis: “*The ITT population included all women randomized, regardless of whether or not they dropped out or fully adhered to the intervention.*” The reported ITT analyses compare women according to randomized group. Any concerns arising from unavailable postpartum EPDS data are addressed in the missing-outcome-data domain.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Substantial loss or exclusion occurred after randomization. Sixty women were randomized (32 fish oil; 28 placebo), but only 32 women completed the trial according to the participant-flow description and per-protocol analyses (15 fish oil; 17 control). The report describes several post-randomization events: women did not receive the intervention, dropped out of the intervention, missed study visits, or had pregnancy losses. In particular, “*At the postpartum visit, 2 women in the fish oil group missed the appointment*”; postpartum EPDS data necessarily cannot be obtained from women with stillbirth or miscarriage.

   Although the authors describe the ITT population as including all randomized women, they do not explain how postpartum EPDS outcomes were obtained or handled for participants without a postpartum assessment. The percentages in the ITT table continue to use the randomized denominators, but there is no transparent accounting of available T3 EPDS data, no description of imputation, and no sensitivity analysis assessing plausible outcomes among women with missing postpartum EPDS measurements.

   Missingness could plausibly be related to depressive symptoms or to pregnancy outcomes, and the amount of attrition/exclusion is sufficiently large that it could materially affect the estimated prevalence of EPDS ≥11. Therefore, the result is at high risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was measured using the EPDS, a standard and validated self-report screening instrument. The authors state that the Portuguese EPDS version had been validated in Brazilian mothers and that the threshold of ≥11 had reported sensitivity of 83.8% and specificity of 74.7% for moderate/severe postpartum depression screening.

   For this participant-reported outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor. Participants were reportedly blinded to their assigned supplementation, and the fish-oil and placebo capsules were designed to be indistinguishable. Consequently, participants’ reporting of EPDS symptoms was unlikely to be differentially influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment. EPDS administration occurred at the same scheduled trial time points in both groups. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment methods differed between intervention groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies “*prevalence of EPDS ≥11*” as the primary outcome and presents results for that outcome at several time points. However, the available ClinicalTrials.gov record is for the parent observational cohort, not a clearly specified randomized supplementation trial. It lists the study as observational, prospectively enrolled 299 women, and identifies “major depressive episode” as the primary outcome; it does not pre-specify the randomized fish-oil versus placebo comparison, EPDS ≥11 at 4–6 weeks postpartum, the EPDS threshold, or the planned longitudinal logistic-regression analysis.

   Moreover, the registration was retrospective: the article states that enrolment occurred from November 2009 to October 2011 and follow-up ended in July 2012, whereas the registration was completed on 24 May 2012. Thus, the registry does not provide a pre-specified, finalized analysis plan established before unblinded outcome data could have become available.

   The article reports several EPDS-related outcomes and analyses, including prevalence at multiple time points, EPDS continuous scores, change scores, longitudinal models, ITT and per-protocol analyses, adjusted and unadjusted models, and a subgroup defined by history of depression. There is no direct evidence that the reported T3 prevalence result was selected because of its magnitude or statistical significance. However, because no sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan is available to rule out selection among multiple measurements and analyses, there are some concerns about selective reporting.
