# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment
**Outcome: Effects of supervision on patient care, measured by team checklist score (supervised scenario)**
**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (randomization to direct/immediately available/distant supervision)**

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise primarily because the trial provides no description of the method used to generate the allocation sequence or to conceal it prior to assignment. Baseline characteristics appear reasonably balanced across groups, which does not suggest a problem with randomization itself, but the complete absence of methodological detail prevents a "Low risk" judgement.

**b. Rationale:** The Methods section states only that "Residents were randomized to one of three levels of supervision" with no further elaboration on sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number tables) or allocation concealment procedures (e.g., sealed envelopes, central randomization). No information is provided about whether personnel enrolling participants could foresee upcoming assignments. Table 1 shows generally similar baseline characteristics (age, gender, level of training, subspecialty distribution, prior ICU/simulation experience, confidence scores) across the three groups, with differences compatible with chance given the small sample size (e.g., confidence score 70–78%, ICU experience 0.88–1 month). Because there is no information on sequence generation or allocation concealment, and no evidence of baseline imbalance suggesting a problem, this maps to "Some concerns" per the algorithm (no information on concealment AND baseline differences compatible with chance).

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Participants (residents), supervisors (fellows), and confederate staff could not be blinded to the level of supervision, as this was the intervention itself and was physically apparent. There is some evidence that deviations from assigned intervention occurred (e.g., residents in the "immediately available" group having supervisors come to the bedside, or distant-group residents calling supervisors at varying times), but these deviations reflect realistic, permitted variation in the protocol itself (supervisors were told to behave as they would in real life) rather than deviations arising from the trial context that were inconsistent with the protocol. The analysis appears to have included all randomized participants according to assigned group.

**b. Rationale:** By design, blinding of participants and personnel was impossible: "The level of supervision was defined by the physical proximity of the supervisor: direct... immediately available... and distant." Fellows and confederates knew which arm each resident was in. The signalling question about deviations from intended intervention arising from the trial context is relevant here: "During immediately supervised scenarios, two residents did not call their supervisor, four residents obtained advice from their supervisor by phone only, and 10 residents were eventually assisted by their supervisor at the bedside." This variability is described as expected within-protocol behavior for that arm (immediately available means supervisor "able to be at the bedside within 1 min when needed"), rather than a deviation inconsistent with the trial protocol. There is no clear evidence that non-protocol interventions or contamination between arms occurred (e.g., a "distant" resident receiving direct bedside supervision inconsistent with their assignment). The analysis was based on the assigned level of supervision for all randomized residents in each group (no indication of post-randomization exclusions or crossover in the outcome analysis reported in Table 2). Given the lack of blinding but no clear evidence of protocol-inconsistent, outcome-affecting, unbalanced deviations arising from trial context, and given some uncertainty due to limited reporting on this issue, "Some concerns" is appropriate rather than "Low" or "High."

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Outcome data (team checklist score) appear to have been available for all randomized and analyzed residents (18, 16, and 19 in the direct, immediately available, and distant groups, respectively), with no reported dropouts affecting this specific outcome.

**b. Rationale:** Table 2 reports team checklist scores for the full analyzed sample in each group (n=18, 16, 19), matching the total recruited sample described in Table 1 ("Fifty-three residents completing a critical care rotation... were recruited"). There is no mention in the Results or Methods of residents withdrawing, being lost to follow-up, or having missing checklist data for the supervised scenario. Because data appear complete for all participants for this particular outcome, the risk of bias due to missing outcome data is low.

---

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The checklist-based measurement method itself was appropriate and applied consistently across groups (any team member's completion of an item counted), but outcome assessment was not blinded to intervention group, and some elements of judgement were likely involved in scoring checklist items, raising the possibility that assessment could have been influenced by knowledge of the assigned supervision level.

**b. Rationale:** The team checklist score was determined in real time or via video review by raters who were necessarily aware of which supervision arm was being observed, since the presence/absence and physical location of the supervisor (in the room vs. absent) is inherently visible in the scenario recordings: "A checklist task was considered done if performed by any member of the team (resident, fellow, and nurse)... We also recorded on the checklist form who performed the task." There is no mention that checklist raters were blinded to allocation group, and blinding of assessors would be extremely difficult in this design because the supervisor's physical presence/location defines the intervention. This is an observer-reported outcome involving some judgement (rating completion of clinical tasks from video or live observation), meaning knowledge of the intervention could plausibly have influenced scoring, particularly given that assessors might expect better performance under closer supervision. However, there is no explicit evidence that assessment was in fact influenced by this knowledge (e.g., no described discrepancies suggesting rater bias, and the checklist was described as having "high face and content validity"). This corresponds to "Some concerns" per the algorithm: assessors were not blinded (a necessary consequence of the study design), the outcome involves some judgement and could be influenced by knowledge of group assignment, but there is no clear indication that such influence actually occurred.

---

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The team checklist score appears to have been a pre-specified, single, unambiguous outcome measure and analysis method, with no evidence of selective reporting from multiple candidate outcome measurements or analyses.

**b. Rationale:** According to the trial registration information (doc_id: 4dab5cab-2b44-42f8-8559-2dd9073b18b5) and the manuscript's own description of a priori-defined measures, "the number of clinical tasks completed during the supervised scenario was used to assign a team checklist score," analyzed via a single pre-specified method (one-way ANOVA). The Methods section clearly defines this checklist score, its calculation, and its single analysis approach (ANOVA comparing three supervision levels) without indicating multiple alternative scales, time points, or analytic strategies were considered and selectively reported. There is no discrepancy between the described a priori research questions/outcomes and what is reported in Table 2. No indication of selective outcome reporting (e.g., no alternative checklist versions, no evidence of selectively reported subscales or adjusted vs. unadjusted comparisons) was found for this specific outcome, supporting a low risk of bias judgement.

---

# Overall Risk of Bias for this Outcome: **Some concerns**

Per RoB 2 guidance (Table 1), because domains 1, 2, and 4 were each judged as "Some concerns" (with no domain judged "High risk"), the overall risk-of-bias judgement for the team checklist score outcome during supervised scenarios is **Some concerns**. The multiplicity of "Some concerns" judgements across the randomization, deviations from intended intervention, and outcome measurement domains lowers confidence in the result, though not to the point of "High risk," given the plausible and largely unavoidable nature of the concerns (e.g., inherent impossibility of blinding given the nature of the intervention).
