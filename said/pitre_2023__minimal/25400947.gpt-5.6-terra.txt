1. **bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Although the study is described as randomized and states that participants were divided “by simple random sampling,” the method used to generate the allocation sequence is not adequately described and allocation concealment is not reported. More importantly, there is a substantial baseline imbalance in cervical effacement, a clinically important prognostic indicator of preterm delivery and treatment response. This imbalance is unlikely to be compatible with chance and suggests a problem in the randomization process.  
   
   The authors state: “all patients were divided in two groups by simple random sampling,” but provide no information on sequence generation (e.g., random-number table or computer-generated sequence) or concealment of assignments before enrolment. Table 3 reports significantly greater baseline effacement in the celecoxib group than in the MgSO4 group (mean 42.0% versus 35.93%; *P* ≤ 0.0001). The authors also report a statistically significant difference in baseline gestational age (*P* < 0.0001), although the corresponding data are not clearly presented. These pronounced imbalances in important predictors of delivery timing raise serious concern that randomization did not produce comparable groups.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** This assessment addresses the effect of assignment to celecoxib versus MgSO4 at baseline. Blinding is inadequately described as “single blinded,” without stating whether participants, treating clinicians, or outcome assessors were blinded. Given the clearly different routes and modes of administration—intravenous MgSO4 infusion with intensive toxicity monitoring versus oral celecoxib—it is likely that at least carers and treatment providers were aware of assigned intervention, and participants may also have been aware.  
   
   The report states: “It was a single blinded randomized clinical trial study,” but does not identify who was blinded or describe how blinding could have been maintained. The treatment procedures differed substantially: the MgSO4 group received “4 gr of MgSO4 intravenously as loading dose and 1 gr per hour as maintenance dose,” whereas the celecoxib group received “100 mg celecoxib … orally every 12 hours.” Differential monitoring and co-interventions are therefore possible.  
   
   The article does not report whether deviations from protocol treatment, switching, or differential non-protocol interventions occurred because of the trial context. However, the analysis appears to compare randomized groups of 300 participants each, rather than explicitly using an as-treated or per-protocol analysis. Therefore, there is insufficient evidence for high risk, but incomplete information about blinding and trial-context deviations leads to some concerns.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** The study reports 600 randomized women, apparently allocated equally between the two groups, and presents the delivery-within/after-48-hours outcome as percentages based on 300 participants per group. There is no reported loss to follow-up, withdrawal, or post-randomization exclusion for this outcome.  
   
   The authors state that “600 pregnant women with preterm labor criteria were randomly divided in two equal groups.” In Table 1, MgSO4 data total 300 participants (240 and 60). The celecoxib percentages, 75.7% and 24.3%, also total 100% and correspond to a denominator of 300.  
   
   There is an apparent typographical or transcription error in Table 1: the reported celecoxib frequencies, 227 and 30, total 257 rather than 300; 24.3% of 300 would correspond to approximately 73 rather than 30 participants. Nevertheless, the percentages strongly indicate that outcome data were intended to be reported for all 300 women in the celecoxib group. The report provides no indication that the discrepancy reflects missing outcome data or differential attrition. This issue should be noted when extracting the numerical result, but it does not by itself demonstrate bias due to missing data.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Delivery after 48 hours is an objective clinical outcome that would ordinarily be determined from delivery timing and hospital records. It is unlikely that knowledge of the allocated intervention could materially influence whether or when a delivery occurred within the 48-hour threshold.  
   
   Both groups were monitored in hospital after presentation with preterm labour. The report describes regular assessment of contractions and fetal status, including that “fetal heart rate was checked regularly” and uterine contractions were checked every two hours. Although the report does not explicitly state that outcome assessors were blinded, the outcome does not require a subjective judgement comparable with patient-reported symptoms or clinician-rated improvement. There is also no indication that the definition or timing of ascertainment of delivery differed between groups.  
   
   The internal inconsistency in the labelling and frequencies in Table 1 affects clarity of reporting, but it does not indicate that the underlying method used to measure delivery timing was inappropriate or different between groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The available report identifies delivery at 48 hours as an outcome and reports a comparison between groups. However, there is insufficient evidence that the specific analysis of delivery after 48 hours was conducted according to a fully specified analysis plan finalized before access to unblinded outcome data. The trial registration can support that the trial and its broad outcomes were planned, but does not provide a detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan that specifies the exact outcome definition, analysis population, handling of discrepancies or missing data, and analysis method.  
   
   In addition, reporting of this outcome is internally inconsistent. The text states that “80% of MgSO4 group and 75% of celecoxib group showed positive response … and uterine contraction stopped at least for 48 hours,” whereas Table 1 labels these same approximate percentages as “Delivery before 48 hours.” The celecoxib frequencies in the table also do not match the stated percentages. These inconsistencies make it difficult to determine the exact numerical result intended for the outcome “frequency of delivery after 48 h.”  
   
   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected from multiple outcome measurements or multiple analyses on the basis of its statistical significance; therefore, high risk of bias is not justified. Nevertheless, the absence of a sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan and the inconsistent reporting support a judgement of some concerns.
