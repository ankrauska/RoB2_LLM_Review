1. **bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because although the report states that allocation used random numbers, allocation concealment is not described and the randomized groups appear importantly imbalanced in baseline work-intensity roles and CK values. These imbalances are potentially prognostic for the muscle-damage outcome.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The authors state that, in period B, “crew members were divided into two groups according to random numbers,” which supports a random component in sequence generation. However, there is no information on concealment of the sequence before enrolment/allocation (e.g., central allocation, independent pharmacy, or opaque sequential envelopes).  
   
   More importantly, the groups were markedly different in the distribution of physically demanding crew roles: the allopurinol group contained 8 high-intensity-work sailors and the placebo group only 2 (with 4 low-intensity sailors). This is consistent with the substantially higher pre-race CK level in the allopurinol group (445.8 U/L) than in the placebo group (275.8 U/L). Since high-intensity work was strongly associated with higher CK in period A, these differences suggest a potentially problematic randomization result rather than a trivial imbalance. Under RoB 2, absent information on concealment together with baseline differences suggesting a problem with randomization leads to a high-risk judgement.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to allopurinol versus placebo at baseline.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The intervention was allocation to 300 mg/day allopurinol or placebo before racing. The report states that “Sailors were blind regarding the group assignment,” which indicates that participants were blinded. Use of a placebo also makes it likely that participants’ behaviour during racing was not materially altered by knowledge of allocation.  
   
   There is no report of differential co-interventions, crossover, differential implementation of the assigned study treatment, or departures from the protocol that arose because of the trial context and could have affected CK or AST. Four sailors declined participation in period B before the reported intervention-phase groups were formed; the remaining sample is reported as 13 allopurinol and 10 placebo participants, matching the reported analysed groups.  
   
   The analysis is reported by randomized treatment group, with measurements before and after the race. Although the analysis is limited—particularly because the paper emphasizes within-group changes rather than a formal between-group comparison—it does not appear that participants were analysed according to treatment received rather than randomized assignment, or excluded because of post-randomization deviations. Therefore, there is no evidence of bias due to deviations from intended intervention for the assignment effect.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The intervention-phase sample comprised 23 sailors after four crew members declined participation: “a first group of 13 participants … was given” allopurinol and “the other ten-member group received a placebo.” Figure 4 reports CK and AST results for all 13 allopurinol and all 10 placebo participants before and after the racing period.  
   
   Thus, for the reported muscle-damage markers, outcome data appear available for all randomized participants in period B. The article does not report post-randomization losses, missing blood samples, or imputation procedures. The four sailors who declined to take part appear not to have entered the randomized intervention comparison and therefore do not constitute missing outcome data from the analysed randomized sample.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** Muscle damage was assessed using serum CK and AST activity, objective laboratory biomarkers. The authors report that these were “spectrophotometrically measured using commercially available reagents on an automatic random access analyser.” They also report intra-assay coefficients of variation of 4.5% for CK and 4.2% for AST. These are appropriate and objective measurement methods for the reported enzyme markers of muscle damage.  
   
   Blood sampling was undertaken at the same stated time points in both groups (“just before and after the second round of the Louis Vuitton Cup”), and there is no indication that outcome ascertainment or laboratory methods differed by intervention group. Even if laboratory personnel were aware of allocation, automated biochemical measurements provide little scope for knowledge of intervention to influence outcome assessment. The investigators acknowledge that CK timing may not perfectly represent the full extent of muscle damage, but this concerns the interpretation and sensitivity of the biomarker rather than differential measurement bias between randomized groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** No protocol, statistical analysis plan, or usable trial-registration details are available in the supplied material to establish that the muscle-damage analyses were pre-specified and finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. Consequently, it is not possible to verify prespecification of the outcome definition, timing, analysis population, or statistical approach.  
   
   The article reports several biomarkers (CK, AST, and MDA), multiple racing periods, several time points, subgroup analyses by work intensity, and several comparisons. This creates opportunities for selecting among measurements and analyses. In particular, the intervention-phase conclusion emphasizes that CK significantly decreased within the allopurinol group, whereas AST did not change and MDA decreased without statistical significance. The report does not present a clear formal between-group comparison of change in CK, despite this being the randomized comparison of interest.  
   
   There is not sufficient evidence to conclude that the reported CK result was definitely selected because it was favourable; therefore, high risk is not assigned. However, the absence of a pre-specified, date-stamped analysis plan and the availability of multiple outcomes and analyses justify some concerns.
