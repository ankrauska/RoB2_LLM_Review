**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
**a. Judgement:** The trial used random selection for the allocation sequence, but there is no information provided on whether the allocation sequence was concealed. Baseline characteristics (gender and sample size) appear balanced between groups. The lack of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns.
**b. Rationale:** The authors state, "Insertion of either a 0.014 or 0.016 inch wire was by random selection" and "The patients were randomly selected for insertion of the 0.014 or 0.016 inch wires." However, there is no mention of the method used to conceal the sequence (e.g., opaque envelopes, central randomization). The groups were well-balanced in size (56 vs. 53) and gender distribution.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** Although the orthodontists delivering the interventions were aware of the wire sizes, there is no evidence that this led to deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context. Furthermore, all randomized patients completed the questionnaire, indicating an appropriate analysis of the effect of assignment to intervention.
**b. Rationale:** The trial was not blinded for the operators. However, "The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned at the following appointment," showing that all participants were analyzed in the groups to which they were randomized, fulfilling intention-to-treat principles.

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** Outcome data were available for all randomized participants, so there is no risk of bias due to missing outcome data.
**b. Rationale:** The authors state, "The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned at the following appointment," which indicates a 100% response rate with no dropouts or missing data for the initial pain outcome.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** The outcome of initial pain was self-reported using a questionnaire. While the operators were unblinded, the patients (the outcome assessors for this self-reported outcome) were likely unaware of the minute difference in wire size (0.014 vs 0.016 inch). Even if they were aware, it is highly unlikely that knowledge of such a specific wire dimension would systematically influence their reporting of when the initial pain started.
**b. Rationale:** The outcome was measured via a self-reported questionnaire: "They described the time of initial pain in the first question...". A patient cannot physically differentiate between a 0.014 and 0.016 inch wire, effectively blinding the outcome assessor and preventing the assessment from being influenced by knowledge of the intervention.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
**a. Judgement:** There is no mention of a pre-specified clinical trial protocol or statistical analysis plan available to confirm whether the data were analyzed in accordance with original intentions. 
**b. Rationale:** Without a date-stamped trial registration or published protocol, it is impossible to verify if the reported outcome measurements and analyses (such as the specific time points and pain metrics reported) were chosen from among multiple possible analyses based on the statistical significance of the results. This lack of information automatically raises some concerns for this domain.
