1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants were randomly selected from eligible clinic registrants and, after consent and baseline assessment, were centrally allocated to the three study groups using a computerized, age-stratified randomization procedure. Allocation occurred through the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, separate from local recruitment, which provides reasonable assurance of allocation concealment. Baseline characteristics were reported to be similar across groups.

   Supporting information includes:
   - “A stratified randomization method blocking by age (50–59; 60–70) was used to allocate women to each group using a computerized random generator program.”
   - “A complete data base with 500 eligible women interviewed at baseline was sent to the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center in the US … to proceed with a random allocation.”
   - “No significant differences were observed between groups in the demographic characteristics of the participants.”

   Thus, the allocation sequence was random, concealment was likely adequate because allocation was performed centrally after recruitment/baseline data collection, and there is no indication of an important baseline imbalance suggesting a failure of randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and the lay health educators could not be blinded, as acknowledged by the authors. However, the trial appears to have implemented the assigned intervention strategies as planned, and the principal result was based on an intention-to-treat analysis using all randomized women in their assigned groups.

   The conditional telephone/home-visit component in the high-intensity arm was part of the planned intervention: women were contacted only if they had not arranged or completed mammography following the mailing. Therefore, the fact that only 50% of women in this group received telephone and/or home contact does not represent a protocol deviation for the assignment-effect estimand.

   Important co-intervention exposure—opportunistic advice from a primary-care provider—was similar between groups:
   - “Brief advice about having a mammogram was delivered by a primary care provider to 15% (73/500) of participants. This intervention had a very similar distribution across groups.”
   - “100% of participants in the low and high intensity groups received the mail contact.”
   - “Fifty percent (83/167) of the women [in the high-intensity group] were contacted by telephone and/or received a home visit.”
   - “An intention to treat analysis was conducted using electronic clinical records… They were reviewed for all women and compliance was estimated using the baseline denominators of each group.”

   Although lack of participant blinding could theoretically have influenced health-seeking behaviour, there is no evidence that deviations arising specifically from the trial context occurred differentially and affected the outcome. The analysis preserved randomized assignment and included the full randomized denominators.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The primary analysed outcome was mammography completion according to electronic clinical records, and these records were reviewed for all 500 randomized women. The investigators explicitly used baseline group denominators in the intention-to-treat analysis. Therefore, loss to follow-up from the final self-report survey did not lead to missing data for the primary electronic-record outcome.

   Relevant statements are:
   - “An intention to treat analysis was conducted using electronic clinical records.”
   - “They were reviewed for all women and compliance was estimated using the baseline denominators of each group.”
   - “After 6 months, 92.4% (462/500) of the women completed the final survey.”

   The approximately 7.6% non-completion of the final questionnaire was relatively balanced across arms—6.6% in control, 7.8% in low intensity, and 8.4% in high intensity—but, importantly, electronic outcome data were available for the whole randomized sample. Consequently, missing self-report data are unlikely to have biased the electronic-record result assessed here.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Mammography completion was ascertained using objective electronic sources: the radiology-unit database and the electronic medical chart. The method was appropriate for determining whether mammography had been performed, was applied to all groups, and staff at the clinic and radiology unit were blinded to allocation.

   Supporting information includes:
   - “Mammography screening was also measured using electronic records.”
   - “The first source was the electronic database containing the reports of all tests conducted during the study at the radiology unit affiliated with the university clinic. The second source was the electronic chart where the results of all tests are registered.”
   - “Staff at the clinic and at the radiology unit where participants got their mammograms were blinded to the allocation group.”
   - “The analysis of the data was conducted by investigators of the coordination center… They were blinded to the group assignment of participants.”

   Completion of mammography in electronic records is not a subjective participant-reported endpoint. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment differed systematically across trial arms; mammography was free and clinic access was equivalent in all groups. Therefore, knowledge of allocation was unlikely to influence the recorded outcome.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial report and registration identify mammography-screening compliance as the principal outcome, assessed at six months, and the publication reports electronic-record results for all randomized participants, consistent with the stated intention-to-treat approach. It also reports self-reported screening results. Thus, there is no clear evidence that the reported electronic-record result was selected from multiple outcome measures or analyses because of its findings.

   However, low risk of bias requires evidence that the specific analysis was conducted in accordance with a sufficiently detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. Neither the article nor the available registration information provides a dated, detailed statistical analysis plan specifying, for example, the precise preferred outcome source if self-report and electronic records differed, the exact effect measure/model, or handling of all analytic choices.

   The report states:
   - “The main outcome of the study was compliance with mammogram screening.”
   - “We measured mammogram screening by self report at baseline and six months after randomization.”
   - “Mammography screening was also measured using electronic records.”
   - “An intention to treat analysis was conducted using electronic clinical records.”

   The registration supports that mammography-screening compliance was a planned outcome, reducing concern about post hoc outcome introduction. Nevertheless, the absence of a clearly pre-specified, detailed analysis plan means that the possibility of selection among eligible measurement and analytic approaches cannot be fully excluded.
