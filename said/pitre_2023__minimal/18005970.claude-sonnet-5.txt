# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment

**Outcome assessed:** Number of mistakes made in diagnosing problems (diagnostic hypotheses) identified by students in Phases 1 and 2
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The sequence generation method is described only minimally: "the students enrolled in the research project were consequently randomised with a randomisation table into two different groups." No further detail is given about how this table was generated (e.g., simple randomization, restricted/blocked randomization), so it is not possible to be fully confident that a robust random process was used, though a random component is stated ("Probably yes" to Signalling Question 1.1).

There is no description whatsoever of allocation concealment procedures. It is not stated whether the randomisation table was held by an independent party, whether allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants, or how enrolment order interacted with the sequence. This leads to "No information" for allocation concealment (Signalling Question 1.2), which is a critical gap for a trial in an educational setting where enrolling personnel/tutors were aware of the study.

Regarding baseline balance, the footnotes to Table 2 state "n.s. = no significant difference between Trial and Control groups in each group," suggesting no major imbalance in the *within-campus* intervention vs. control comparisons on measured characteristics such as sex, education, and prior work experience. However, the *between-campus* comparisons (Trento vs. Udine) shown in Table 2 reveal statistically significant differences in previous work experience duration (p<0.05), voluntary work experience (p<0.01), and clinical training programmed (p<0.01) — although these reflect baseline contextual/cohort differences rather than a failure of randomization per se, since Trento and Udine were not randomized to each other.

Given: (i) an adequate description of random component but (ii) no information on allocation concealment, and (iii) no clear evidence of problematic imbalance within each randomized comparison — this maps to "Some concerns" per the RoB2 algorithm (Table 4: NI on concealment + Y/PY on random sequence + no imbalance evidence → Some concerns).

---

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** Blinding of participants and personnel (tutors) was not possible and was not attempted, as is inherent to this type of pedagogical intervention — students and tutors necessarily knew which tutoring strategy (laboratory, intensive tutoring, weekly tutorials, routine tutoring) was being delivered. This satisfies "Yes" to Signalling Questions 2.1 and 2.2 (participants and personnel aware of assigned intervention).

The critical follow-up question is whether deviations from intended intervention arose *because of the trial context* and affected the outcome. The authors describe substantial efforts at standardization: "All the tutors involved in the study met to ensure that the methods of tutoring and the list of laboratory questions were the same for each group" (Table 1), suggesting fidelity to protocol was actively monitored. However, the authors themselves note an important contextual deviation: during the study, "the Trento Faculty of Nursing experienced 'cut-backs' with reduced numbers of tutors allocated to supervising students... Student exposure to intensive tutorials was reduced and routine tutorials by ward staff... were introduced." This is a systemic deviation affecting delivery of the intended intervention (though it appears to have affected the *control* condition/context in Trento specifically, and reflects a naturally occurring resource change rather than participant/personnel-driven contamination based on trial awareness).

There is no clear evidence of cross-contamination between arms (e.g., students switching tutorial strategies) or of participants seeking non-protocol interventions because of trial-related recruitment/consent processes. The analysis appears to include all randomized participants at each phase (Ns are consistent with those randomized: e.g., Table 3 Phase 2 shows 41+41 Trento and 23+39 Udine, matching total enrolled), consistent with an appropriate (ITT-like) analysis (Signalling Question 2.6 = Yes).

Because awareness of intervention assignment was unavoidable, and there is some indication of contextual deviation (the Trento tutor cutbacks) whose impact on the specific outcome (paper-based diagnostic accuracy scored against a standard) is unclear, but not established as substantial or unbalanced by design, the judgement is "Some concerns" rather than "High risk," per Table 6 (Y/PY on awareness, Y/PY/NI on deviations from trial context, uncertain balance/effect on outcome).

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Of 179 students initially enrolled, 144 (80.4%) were determined eligible ("A total of 144 students... were involved"); this exclusion occurred at the eligibility-determination stage *prior to randomization* based on pre-specified criteria (language ability, prior work experience, attendance), not as a post-randomization dropout: "Each student who was not accepted was offered the best learning strategies available." This does not constitute missing outcome data in the RoB2 sense.

Following randomization, outcome data appear complete across all phases and all four groups. Table 3 reports consistent denominators across Phase 0, 1, and 2 (e.g., Trento n=82 total across phases, split into 41 Intensive/41 Routine in Phase 2; Udine n=62 total, split into 23 Routine/39 Weekly in Phase 2), matching the numbers described in the Participants section and the flow diagram (Figure 1), with no reported losses to follow-up. There is no mention of any student failing to complete a phase or having missing diagnostic-hypothesis data. Given data appear available for essentially all randomized participants at every phase, risk of bias due to missing outcome data is low.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The method of outcome measurement was appropriate and standardized: "Two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students and these were compared to the 'standard.' A panel of 15 professional nurses independently defined the standard problems of the case history used. A definition of the standard was agreed upon by professionals, using the K of Cohen and taking a cut-off as K>0.80." This indicates a validated, objective scoring rubric with a documented measure of inter-rater agreement.

Critically, the outcome assessors (the two researchers scoring diagnostic hypotheses) were blinded to intervention group: the text states "The data were processed blind; only after this had been done were the identities of the Schools (Trento and Udine) revealed." Because this scoring is an observer-based, judgment-involving-but-blinded assessment against a fixed standard, and the same case ("Valentina's case," modified for later phases) was used for all groups equally, there is no indication that measurement of the outcome differed between intervention groups, nor evidence that outcome assessors' judgement could have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received, since they were blinded during scoring. This satisfies the "Low risk" pathway (Signalling Questions 4.1=No, 4.2=No, 4.3=No).

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The article does report the primary outcome (number of diagnostic hypothesis errors in Phases 1 and 2) with pre-specified hypotheses stated clearly in the Methods/Rationale section ("the hypotheses confirmed by the studies were that the 'intervention group' of students in Trento... had less probability of making reasoning errors... than the... 'control group'..."), and the results for this primary outcome are reported comprehensively for both campuses, both in intra-trial and inter-trial analyses (Tables 3 and 4), which supports a reasonably transparent reporting of this specific outcome.

However, no trial registry entry, published trial protocol, or statistical analysis plan is referenced or cited anywhere in the article, and the review of the associated trial registration document did not surface a pre-registered protocol allowing verification that the analysis presented (e.g., choice of median-based cut-offs to dichotomize error counts for odds ratio calculations in Table 4, choice of covariates for multivariate adjustment) was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The use of median-split cut-offs for calculating odds ratios (e.g., "≥Median (3)" in Phase 1, "≥Median (2)" in Phase 2) is a data-dependent analytic choice that could reflect selection of a categorization scheme after seeing the data distribution, though this is explicitly described as a methodological decision rather than hidden. Additionally, several secondary outcomes (student perception, self-confidence, perceived difficulty) are also reported, reducing (but not eliminating) concern about outcome-selective reporting, but analysis-selective reporting (e.g., choice of cut-points, choice of univariate vs. multivariate models with only "significant" ORs carried into multivariate analysis, as noted in Table 4's footnote: "Only variables that have a significant odds ratio (OR) are included in the multivariate analysis") raises the possibility that the analytic pathway was influenced by the pattern of results.

Given the absence of a documented, date-stamped pre-specified analysis plan and evidence of at least one data-driven analytic choice (selection of variables for multivariate modeling based on univariate significance, and post hoc median cut-offs), but with the core outcome itself (numerical mistake counts) reported in full and consistent with study aims, the judgement is "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."
