**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** The trial used an adequate method for sequence generation and allocation concealment, and baseline differences do not suggest a problem with the randomization process.
* **Rationale:** The publication states that "random assignment to treatment conditions (sertraline or placebo) was balanced by using a block design (blocks of 8) with computer-generated random number sequences in individual presealed envelopes." Furthermore, "The pharmacy implemented masking and random assignment of participants... and was responsible for dispensing medications with identical markings", ensuring allocation concealment. While there was a baseline difference in income level, this is consistent with chance in a small sample and does not suggest subversion of the randomization process.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Participants, carers, and personnel were successfully blinded to the intervention assignments, making deviations due to the trial context unlikely. Appropriate analyses were conducted.
* **Rationale:** The trial was a "double-blind, randomized controlled trial." The authors state that "medications were packaged in 25-mg quantities and had the same markings so that increases in the number of pills taken in both drug conditions were identical." Additionally, "both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received." An intent-to-treat analysis using random-effects modeling was applied.

**3. missing outcome data: some concerns**
* **Judgement:** A notable proportion of participants did not complete the study, and those who dropped out had higher baseline depression scores. It is plausible that missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome, raising some concerns.
* **Rationale:** Out of 52 randomized participants (27 in sertraline, 25 in placebo according to the trial registry), 11 dropped out (5 from the sertraline group and 6 from the placebo group). The authors note that "dropouts tending to have higher HAM-D scores." Although random-effects models for repeated measures were used to handle the missing data (which tolerate different numbers of observations), the attrition rate of over 20% and the relationship between baseline symptom severity and dropout introduce some concern that missingness could depend on the unobserved outcomes. 

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** The outcome was measured using an appropriate and validated scale by independent assessors who were blinded to the intervention assignment.
* **Rationale:** The Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-D) is a standard, widely-used clinician-rated instrument. The publication specifies that "a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist rated the clinical severity of the participant’s depressive (HAM-D)... independently from the physician at each biweekly visit." Since the trial was double-blinded and assessors were independent, the assessment was not influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**5. selective reporting: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** The reported results align with the pre-specified analysis intentions and primary outcomes outlined in the trial registry.
* **Rationale:** According to the trial registry (NCT00233103), the primary outcome measure was "Depression at End of Treatment" assessed via the HAM-D score. The published article reports on this exact pre-specified primary outcome and presents the analyses at the planned endpoint, indicating no evidence of selective reporting based on the nature or direction of the results.
