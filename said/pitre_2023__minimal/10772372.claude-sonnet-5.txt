# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Compliance Outcome

**Study:** Impact of Pharmacy Counseling on Compliance and Effectiveness of Combination Lipid-Lowering Therapy (Pharmacotherapy 2000;20(4):410–416)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to lack of allocation concealment information, despite adequate sequence generation.

**b. Rationale:** The trial states: "Patients were randomized to telephone contact or no telephone contact using a computer-generated list of random numbers." This satisfies the signalling question for random sequence generation (Yes/Probably yes). However, there is no information provided about allocation concealment — no description of how the sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants (e.g., no mention of central randomization, sealed envelopes, or similar). Given the small, single-site study (n=30) with no mention of concealment procedures, this is judged "No information" on concealment, which per the guidance (Table 3/4) leads to "Some concerns" unless baseline imbalance also suggests a problem. Baseline characteristics were reported as similar between groups ("Baseline characteristics were similar for both groups (Table 1)"), so there is no evidence of imbalance-related problems. Per Table 4, when sequence is random (Y/PY) but concealment is "No information," and there is no indication of imbalance, the default judgement is "Some concerns."

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as neither participants nor personnel’s awareness of assignment is expected to have influenced deviations from intended intervention that would bias the compliance outcome via the trial context, and an appropriate (ITT-consistent) analysis was used.

**b. Rationale:** This trial is unblinded — patients in the intervention group knew they were receiving weekly telephone counseling, and the pharmacist/personnel obviously knew group assignment ("Patients were randomized to telephone contact or no telephone contact"). Thus, question 2.1/2.2 = Yes. However, compliance was measured objectively via pill/packet counts and pharmacy refill records for both groups regardless of intervention ("Compliance was determined by pill and packet counts... at the 6- and 12-week clinic visits. To assess long-term compliance, pharmacies... were contacted at 1 and 2 years"). Awareness of group assignment is intrinsic to the intervention itself (telephone contact) rather than a "deviation from intended intervention" — i.e., the telephone calls themselves ARE the experimental intervention, not a deviation. There is no evidence of non-protocol interventions or failure to implement intended intervention arising from trial context that would additionally bias assignment effect estimates for compliance beyond the intervention itself. All 30 randomized patients were included in the analysis ("Thirty patients were enrolled in the study, none of whom was lost to follow-up"), consistent with an intention-to-treat approach. Therefore, Part 1 = Low, Part 2 (appropriate analysis) = Low, yielding an overall Low risk judgement for this domain.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as complete compliance data were available for all randomized participants at all time points.

**b. Rationale:** The report explicitly states "Thirty patients were enrolled in the study, none of whom was lost to follow-up." Compliance data (pill/packet counts and refill records) appear to have been collected for all 30 participants at 6 weeks, 12 weeks, 1 year, and 2 years (Tables 2 and 4 report data for all patients in each group, e.g., "Only 1 patient (7%) in the nonintervention group was compliant... compared with 7 patients (47%) in the intervention group" — implying full accounting of all 15 patients per group). With no missing outcome data, this satisfies the "Low risk" criterion outright (data available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants).

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, primarily due to potential differences in outcome ascertainment between groups and lack of blinding of outcome assessment given the nature of the intervention.

**b. Rationale:** Compliance was measured via pill/packet counts and pharmacy refill records — an observer-reported outcome not directly self-reported by blinded assessors, and described as "not performed within the patient's view" for the pill/packet counts, suggesting some effort to standardize measurement. However, the study explicitly raises this concern itself in the Discussion: "It is also possible that compliance rates were skewed toward the favorable side because of the relatively short time interval... Compliance rates may have been misleading because patients were highly aware of the existence and severity of their medical condition" and raises the "Hawthorne effect" as an explanation for early results. Critically, the intervention itself (weekly telephone contact) inherently involves interaction with patients about their medication use, which could differentially influence patients' actual pill-taking behavior or their reporting/handling of pills/refills compared to the control group — i.e., measurement/ascertainment of the outcome (compliance) may not have been entirely comparable between groups, given that the phone calls themselves prompted patients to think about and discuss their refill/adherence behavior (question 4.2 relevant: "Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?"). Since compliance assessors (pharmacy staff providing refill records, or those conducting pill counts) were not described as blinded to group assignment, and the same pharmacist conducting counseling calls may have influenced or been involved in interpreting compliance information, there is potential — though not definitive evidence — of differential ascertainment. This does not rise to "likely" influence (no explicit evidence of assessor bias in recording pill counts/refill data, which are relatively objective), so the judgement is "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the compliance outcome, as pre-specified outcome measures (pill/packet counts and refill records at 6, 12 weeks, 1 and 2 years) appear to have been fully reported without evidence of selective outcome or analysis reporting.

**b. Rationale:** No trial registration record was retrievable/available for this 2000-published trial (trial registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov were not yet in common use in this era), so no independent pre-specified protocol could be directly compared against the published report. However, the Methods section pre-specifies compliance assessment methodology in detail ("Compliance was determined by pill and packet counts... at the 6- and 12-week clinic visits. To assess long-term compliance, pharmacies at which patients filled their prescriptions were contacted at 1 and 2 years") and the Results section reports compliance data fully and consistently across all four pre-specified time points (6 weeks, 12 weeks, 1 year, 2 years) for both lovastatin and colestipol, with no apparent omission of pre-specified compliance measures or alternative analyses. There is no indication of selection from multiple compliance definitions or analytic approaches — the same compliance thresholds (>20% pills returned = noncompliant at 6/12 weeks; <80% refilled = noncompliant at 1/2 years) were applied and reported consistently and transparently, including for both drugs. Thus, there is no clear evidence of selective reporting for this outcome, supporting a "Low risk" judgement, albeit with some residual uncertainty due to absence of a formal pre-registered protocol/SAP for verification.
