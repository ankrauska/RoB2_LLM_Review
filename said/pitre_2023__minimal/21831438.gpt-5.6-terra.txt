**Outcome assessed:** change in mean sitting diastolic blood pressure (DBP) from baseline to week 4, comparing candesartan 16 mg/hydrochlorothiazide (HCT) 12.5 mg combination therapy with candesartan 16 mg monotherapy. The effect of interest is the effect of **assignment** to intervention at baseline.

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the trial reported use of mixed-block randomization but did not report how allocation concealment was maintained before enrolment and assignment.

   b. **Rationale:** The article states that participants “were randomized in a 1:1 ratio by using the mixed block randomization (4 and 6 blocks) method.” This provides reasonable support that a random allocation sequence was used, since variable block sizes were specified. However, there is no information on allocation concealment—for example, central randomization, an independent pharmacy, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes.

   Baseline data do not strongly suggest failure of randomization. There was a statistically significant age difference (mean 51.0 versus 47.8 years; *P* = 0.02), but this isolated difference is compatible with chance among several baseline comparisons. Baseline DBP was virtually identical (104.7 versus 104.5 mm Hg; *P* = 0.89), as was baseline SBP (161.9 versus 160.0 mm Hg; *P* = 0.26). Thus, the principal concern is the unreported allocation-concealment procedure rather than evidence of actual manipulation or major baseline imbalance.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the study was open label and the primary analysis did not include all randomized participants, although the number excluded was small and there is no evidence of important trial-context deviations affecting week-4 DBP.

   b. **Rationale:** The registry explicitly describes an “Open-label, Randomised” study with masking “NONE.” Therefore, participants, carers, and personnel delivering treatment were aware of assigned treatment. Nevertheless, the report gives no evidence that non-protocol co-interventions, differential implementation, or other deviations arising from the trial context occurred and affected the objective blood-pressure outcome during the first 4 weeks. Treatment adherence was actively assessed through questioning and pill counts, and median compliance was high (98.6% in the combination group and 97.8% in the monotherapy group).

   The reported efficacy analysis was labelled ITT, but its definition was not a full ITT analysis: it included only patients with baseline BP, at least one post-randomization BP measurement, and at least one dose of study medication. Of 233 randomized patients (117 combination; 116 monotherapy), the week-4 efficacy analysis included 224 (111 and 113, respectively). Thus, nine randomized participants were excluded from the primary analysis. For the effect of assignment, excluding randomized eligible participants—particularly those who did not receive treatment or lacked post-baseline data—is not fully consistent with ITT principles. However, the exclusion represents approximately 4% of randomized participants, and the available information does not indicate that this small exclusion had a substantial effect on the estimate. Therefore, this raises some concerns rather than high risk of bias.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because week-4 DBP data were available for nearly all randomized participants.

   b. **Rationale:** The primary week-4 analysis included 111/117 (94.9%) randomized participants in the combination group and 113/116 (97.4%) in the monotherapy group—224/233 (96.1%) overall. Thus, only six participants in the combination group and three in the monotherapy group were unavailable for this result.

   For a continuous outcome such as DBP, outcome data for approximately 95% or more of participants will often be sufficient for a low-risk judgement. Although the amount missing was slightly greater in the combination group, the absolute number of missing observations was small (nine total), and no evidence indicates that missingness was related to the unobserved week-4 DBP values or differed systematically for outcome-related reasons between groups. The report does not present a sensitivity analysis, but the small amount of missing data is unlikely to have materially altered the estimated between-group difference.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because sitting DBP was measured using a standardized, automated method applied comparably in both groups; knowledge of treatment assignment is unlikely to have materially influenced this objective outcome.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was measured using “a fully automated device produced by Omron.” The protocolized measurement procedures were detailed and applied similarly to both groups: measurements were taken at the same time of day before dosing, after at least five minutes’ rest, with three readings at one-minute intervals; when necessary, five readings were obtained, the highest and lowest discarded, and the remaining three averaged. The arm with the higher screening mean DBP was used subsequently, and “every attempt was made to have the same individual perform all BP readings in the same patient at each visit.”

   Although this was an open-label trial and assessors may have known treatment assignment, DBP was obtained with an automated device under standardized procedures. There is little scope for assessor judgement to differentially influence the recorded DBP values, and there is no indication that measurement methods or measurement opportunities differed between intervention groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the primary outcome and time point were prospectively registered and reported, but no sufficiently detailed, finalized pre-unblinding statistical analysis plan was available to confirm that the precise reported analysis was pre-specified.

   b. **Rationale:** The ClinicalTrials.gov record was first posted on 22 February 2008, before completion of the study, and prospectively specified the primary outcome as “Changes in Mean Sitting DBP From Baseline After 4 Weeks of Therapy.” The publication reports this outcome at the specified time point and reports results for both treatment groups. This is reassuring and provides no direct evidence that the reported week-4 DBP outcome was selected from multiple unreported outcome measurements.

   However, the registration entry does not provide a detailed pre-specified analysis plan identifying the exact estimand, analysis population, covariate adjustment, handling of missing data, or hierarchy among alternative analyses. The paper presents both ITT and per-protocol analyses and uses ANCOVA with baseline BP as a covariate, while also presenting observed mean changes. The registry results describe least-squares means, whereas the publication prominently reports observed mean changes and an ANCOVA comparison. Without a dated statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available, it cannot be verified that the particular numerical result selected for emphasis was determined in advance. There is no direct evidence of selective reporting, but this residual uncertainty supports a judgement of some concerns.
