**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was generated randomly, and allocation was adequately concealed. The text states: "eligible patients were assigned to escitalopram or placebo treatment according to a computer-generated randomisation list. The details of the randomisation series were unknown to any of the investigators and were contained in a set of sealed opaque envelopes." Furthermore, baseline characteristics were balanced between the two groups ("There were no differences in demographics and clinical parameters between the two treatment groups in the randomisation period"), indicating no issues with the randomization process. 

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The trial was double-blinded, and participants, carers, and personnel were unaware of the intervention assignments. The publication notes: "Study medications were tablets for oral administration, of identical appearance, taste or smell... All study personnel and participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire duration of the study." The analysis was appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention, as it used the intention-to-treat (ITT) population ("All efficacy analyses in the double-blind treatment period were conducted using the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, which consisted of all randomised patients who took at least one dose of trial medication and had at least one post-randomisation efficacy assessment"). 

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The rate of missing outcome data was low and comparable between both groups. The study reports: "During the 24-week double-blind period, the overall withdrawal rate, excluding relapses, was comparable for both treatments: 7.9% for patients treated with escitalopram (13 patients), and 8.9% for patients treated with placebo (14 patients)." Because time-to-event analyses (Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards) correctly account for censored data, and dropout rates due to reasons other than relapse were small and balanced, missing data is unlikely to have biased the result.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The method of measuring the outcome (Y-BOCS total score increase of ≥5 points, or lack of efficacy judged by the investigator) was appropriate and standard for this condition. Furthermore, the outcome assessors and the participants were blinded to the intervention received throughout the double-blind period, meaning the assessment of the outcome could not have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention assignment. 

**5. selective reporting: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The reported result corresponds directly to the prospectively defined primary analysis. The text states: "The prospectively defined primary analysis of efficacy was the time to relapse from the start date of double-blind treatment." There is no evidence that the reported time-to-relapse result was selected from multiple eligible measurements or analysis methods based on the p-value or direction of findings.
