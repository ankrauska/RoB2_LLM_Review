**Outcome assessed:** completed-survey response rate (postal mail, email, and hybrid delivery strategies; conclusions apply to comparisons between delivery methods). The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to delivery method at baseline**.

1. **bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  

   b. **Rationale:** Although the report uses the term “randomized” (e.g., “Pharmacists who were randomized to the postal mail group…”), it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated or concealed. More importantly, the sampling frame appears to have differed systematically by assigned delivery method:  
   - “A random sample of 7200 pharmacists … provided by the Medical Marketing Service (**for the postal mail and hybrid groups**) and Statlistics (**for the email group**) was used.”  
   - Thus, allocation to delivery method appears to have been linked to the commercial database from which the pharmacist was sampled, rather than being a random allocation of a common sampled population to the three delivery strategies.  

   The respondent characteristics also differ materially across delivery groups. For example, Table 1 reports that 3.2% of postal respondents, 26.4% of email respondents, and 19.2% of hybrid respondents were Black; hospital practice was reported by 19.0%, 35.0%, and 13.7%, respectively. These data are among respondents rather than all randomized participants, so they cannot alone diagnose failed randomization. However, together with the delivery-mode-specific sampling databases, they reinforce concern that the groups were not comparable at baseline. Since characteristics such as age, practice setting, and mode of contact may affect survey response, this threatens the validity of the estimated delivery-method effect.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  

   b. **Rationale:** Participants were necessarily aware of the delivery method they received, and personnel administering the survey would also have been aware. However, the distinct contact procedures—including postal questionnaires and reminders, email invitations and reminders, and hybrid postcards—were the intended intervention strategies, not deviations from them. There is no evidence that non-protocol co-interventions or protocol departures arising from the trial context differentially affected response rates.

   There are nevertheless concerns about the analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment. The analysis excluded 233 individuals whose questionnaires were not delivered:  
   - “Out of the original 7200 intended respondents … 233 of them did not receive the questionnaires, which yielded a net of 6967 delivered questionnaires.”  
   - Figure 1 shows 139 undeliverable postal surveys and 94 undeliverable hybrid surveys, versus no undeliverable email surveys.  

   The reported response rates were therefore calculated among delivered/eligible cases rather than all 2,400 participants assigned to each delivery method. The authors state that denominators were “adjusted to account for the questionnaires that could not be delivered.” Excluding post-allocation cases disproportionately from postal and hybrid groups is not a full intention-to-treat analysis of assignment to the delivery strategy. However, the exclusions were relatively few and inclusion of all assigned persons as nonresponders would not appear likely to overturn the large observed differences in response rates. This leads to some concerns rather than high risk.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  

   b. **Rationale:** For the outcome assessed, response status was administratively observable: a participant either returned a completed questionnaire or did not. The flow diagram accounts for all 7,200 intended participants, including surveys not returned and questionnaires returned incomplete:  
   - Postal: 2,400 allocated; 526 returned; 474 complete.  
   - Email: 2,400 allocated; 180 returned; 163 complete.  
   - Hybrid: 2,400 allocated; 82 returned; 73 complete.  

   The outcome was explicitly defined as return of a questionnaire “with no missing data for any questions.” Therefore, incomplete questionnaires were not missing outcome data for this defined outcome; they were classified as nonresponses. Although excluding undeliverable questionnaires from the analysis creates concern for the assignment-effect analysis, this is more appropriately considered under deviations from intended intervention/analysis population rather than missingness of the response-rate outcome itself.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  

   b. **Rationale:** Response rate was based on an objective administrative outcome: receipt of a completed questionnaire with no missing items. The study prespecified the operational definition:  
   - “The response rate was defined as the percentage of eligible respondents that returned completed questionnaires, with no missing data for any questions.”  

   Outcome ascertainment did not require subjective judgement by participants, investigators, or unblinded assessors. Although the delivery procedures differed by group, those differences constitute the interventions being compared and do not indicate differential or inappropriate measurement of whether a completed survey was received. The electronic system stored completed survey results in a database, while returned postal questionnaires could also be counted objectively.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  

   b. **Rationale:** The publication clearly identifies response rate as one of its principal outcomes and reports response-rate results by delivery method, questionnaire length, item placement, and gender. It also reports the numerical results underlying the principal conclusion: postal mail 21.0%, email 6.8%, and hybrid 3.2%, with a statistically significant difference between delivery methods.

   However, there is insufficient information to establish that the specific response-rate analysis reported was prespecified in a detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available. The report describes several possible response-related quantities—completed response rate, returned-survey rate, undeliverable rate, incomplete-response rate, and subgroup-specific response rates—and multiple possible analyses across the factorial classifications. For example, the discussion additionally reports a “combined response rate of questionnaires with no missing data plus some data missing (23.3%).” Without a dated, sufficiently detailed prespecified analysis plan confirming the outcome definition, analysis population, handling of undeliverable questionnaires, and planned subgroup analyses, selective selection among eligible response-rate analyses cannot be excluded. There is no direct evidence that the published result was selected based on its statistical significance or direction; therefore, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk.
