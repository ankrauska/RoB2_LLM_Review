1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** The study is described as randomized, and the authors state that baseline/pretest outcomes did not differ significantly between groups. However, neither the publication nor the registry describes the method used to generate the allocation sequence or to conceal allocation before enrolment. This prevents a low-risk judgement. There is no direct evidence of a non-random allocation process or important baseline imbalance; therefore, high risk is not warranted.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article states that eligible participants “were randomly assigned” to the three video groups or pamphlet group, but does not specify a random-number generation method, blocking/stratification, or allocation concealment procedure. It reports that “initial pretest analyses showed no statistically significant differences among the four study groups.” Group sizes were reasonably similar (20–22 participants per arm), without an obvious imbalance inconsistent with the intended allocation. Nevertheless, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns under RoB 2.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Participants necessarily knew whether they watched one or two videos or read a pamphlet, and personnel delivering the educational materials were also likely aware of assignment. The report does not describe whether trial-context deviations, contamination between groups, or differential additional asthma education occurred. The analysis appears broadly consistent with assignment groups, but it is unclear that all randomized participants were included in the relevant analyses. There is insufficient evidence for high risk, but insufficient information for low risk.  
   b. **Rationale:** The interventions were visibly different: “Group 1 viewed a physician-led knowledge video, Group 2 viewed the patient-generated community video, and Group 3 viewed both … and one comparison group … read an educational pamphlet only.” Thus, blinding of participants and those delivering the intervention was not realistically maintained, notwithstanding the registry’s designation of single participant masking.  

   The article states that facilitators “were not aware of the study hypothesis,” which may reduce expectancy effects, but this is not equivalent to being blinded to allocated intervention. No information is provided about contamination, additional co-interventions, or deviations arising specifically from the trial context. The analyses used ANOVA/ANCOVA and appear to compare the allocated groups, rather than explicitly conducting an as-treated analysis. However, results were based on fewer than all 92 randomized participants—for example, the inhaler-technique analysis reports degrees of freedom consistent with a reduced analysis sample—and a full intention-to-treat analysis is not documented. Under the assignment-to-intervention estimand, these issues raise some concerns rather than high risk because exclusions owing to missing outcomes are principally assessed in the missing-data domain.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Outcome data were not available for all, or clearly nearly all, randomized participants. Seven of 92 enrolled intervention participants did not complete the study, and the reported inhaler-use analysis appears to involve an even smaller sample. Reasons for missing inhaler-technique data by treatment group are not reported. Missingness could plausibly be related to participant engagement, ability, or asthma-management status, but there is no direct evidence that it was likely dependent on the unobserved outcome.  
   b. **Rationale:** The participant flow information indicates that 92 people entered the educational intervention, with “3 … excluded and 4 discontinued intervention”; the article states that 85 completed the study. Thus, approximately 7.6% did not complete follow-up. For the continuous inhaler-technique outcome, this exceeds the usual approximate threshold at which data for “nearly all” participants may be considered available.  

   Moreover, the main inhaler-use result reports *F* statistics with df = 1, 71, while Table 4 presents summary results for 85 completers, making the exact number included in the adjusted outcome analysis unclear. The report does not provide missing-data counts separately by randomized group, reasons for dropout, or sensitivity analyses/imputation assessing the effect of plausible missing outcomes. Nonetheless, there is no reported differential attrition or outcome-related reason for missingness; therefore, some concerns, rather than high risk, is appropriate.

4. **measurement of the outcome: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Inhaler technique was measured using a standardized observational nine-step checklist, which is appropriate and was apparently applied similarly across groups. However, outcome assessors were probably aware, or may have been aware, of the assigned educational intervention, and the report does not explicitly state that the two observers were blinded. Because checklist scoring can involve some judgement, knowledge of intervention could influence assessment, although the standardized checklist and dual-observer approach make major influence less likely.  
   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was assessed using “a standardized nine-step observational checklist,” with scores from 0 to 9 according to the number of correctly performed inhaler-technique steps. This is an appropriate direct measure of inhaler-use technique. The same instrument and assessment procedure appear to have been used at baseline and follow-up in all groups.  

   The authors state: “The assessment of the inhaler techniques during the face-to-face interviews was judged by two observers (the interviewer and the research coordinator)” and describe this as measurement by “two independent observers.” However, they do not report that these observers were blinded to group allocation. The facilitators conducted interviews in participants’ native languages and the interventions were educational videos or a pamphlet, making assessor awareness plausible. Although facilitators “were not aware of the study hypothesis,” that does not establish blinding to the intervention received. The structured checklist and involvement of two observers reduce the likelihood of major differential assessment bias, but lack of explicit assessor masking supports some concerns.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** There is no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan for inhaler-use technique against which the reported result can be verified. The registry identifies medication adherence as the primary outcome but does not clearly prespecify inhaler-use technique, its scoring method, the comparison of interest, the time point, or the statistical analysis. Multiple outcome-assessment times and multiple intervention groups were available. There is no direct evidence that the reported inhaler-technique result was selected because of its findings, so high risk is not justified.  
   b. **Rationale:** The trial registry lists the primary outcome as “medication adherence,” described as understanding physicians’ instructions and regular inhaler use, with a timeframe of “on year.” It does not set out a specific nine-step inhaler-technique endpoint, a prespecified 3-month comparison, or an analysis plan. In contrast, the publication reports repeated assessments: baseline, immediate post-test, 3-month follow-up, and a telephone assessment 6 months later. The article explicitly states that it “summarizes the analyses from pretest and 3-month post-intervention follow-up data.”  

   There were also four intervention groups and potentially several eligible analyses, including group comparisons, pooled analyses, adjusted ANCOVA analyses, and analyses at different follow-up times. The lack of a detailed pre-unblinded analysis plan means it cannot be established that the specific reported inhaler-technique result was selected without knowledge of results. However, neither the article nor registry provides clear evidence that alternative inhaler-technique measurements or analyses were selectively omitted because they were less favourable.
