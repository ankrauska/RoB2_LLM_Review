1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence was random, and reported baseline comparisons did not indicate an obvious problem; however, allocation concealment was not adequately described.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors state: “*intervention and control booklets were placed in a single pile in an order determined by a sequence of random integers generated by a true random number generator*.” This supports a random allocation sequence.

   However, the method does not establish that allocation was concealed until each participant had been enrolled and assigned. Booklets were distributed from “*a single pile*” in a lecture theatre/laboratory setting. There is no information that the booklets were opaque, sequentially numbered, sealed, or distributed by someone independent of recruitment who could not foresee the next allocation. Thus, the allocation sequence may not have been adequately concealed.

   The authors reported no statistically significant baseline differences between groups in cognition, behaviour, age, gender, or dropout rate, including “*no significant pre-intervention differences on intention*.” These findings do not suggest a clear failure of randomization. Nevertheless, under RoB 2, lack of information about allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew whether they received the fruit-and-vegetable intervention or the control questionnaire task. The intervention included persuasive messages and planning exercises, whereas the control task comprised questions about completing the questionnaire. Thus, participants were aware of their assigned condition.

   However, for the assignment effect, RoB 2 considers only deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, and could affect the outcome. The report does not describe differential co-interventions, crossovers, implementation failures, or other deviations from the assigned booklet/task. The request not to discuss booklet contents was intended to reduce contamination: “*To reduce the risk of cross condition contamination, participants were explicitly requested not to discuss the contents of their booklet with other participants over the following week.*”

   The immediate post-intervention intention analysis appears to retain all randomized participants: the ANCOVA testing post-intervention intention had \(F(1,215)\), consistent with analysis of the 218 participants who completed the baseline booklet. The analysis compared groups as randomized while controlling for pre-intervention intention. This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment, aside from missing data, which are considered separately.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome is immediate post-intervention intention to consume the RDIFV, measured in the same booklet session as baseline and the intervention/control task. The authors report that “*In total, 218 people completed the booklet*,” and describe the post-intervention cognitive measures as being completed directly after the assigned task.

   The reported ANCOVA for intention used \(F(1,215)\), indicating that data were analysed for approximately all 218 participants. Therefore, there is no indication of important missing immediate post-intervention intention data.

   Although 33% of participants were lost at the one-week behavioural follow-up, that attrition concerns the later behavioural outcomes, not the immediate post-intervention intention outcome being assessed here.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Intention was a participant-reported, subjective outcome, measured using five self-report items, such as “*I intend to eat the RDIFV every day over the next week*” and “*I am going to eat the RDIFV every day over the next week*.”

   Participants were aware of their allocated intervention. The intervention explicitly instructed participants: “*You can do it, so do it this week. Make a firm decision now that you will eat the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables each day this week.*” Immediately afterwards, they completed the intention measure. Therefore, knowledge of the assigned intervention was highly likely to influence how participants reported their intentions, through expectancy effects, demand characteristics, or a desire to respond consistently with the persuasive message.

   The same measure appears to have been used in both groups at the same time point, and the scale had high internal consistency (post-intervention Cronbach’s \(\alpha=.93\)). Nevertheless, the central concern is differential measurement error: because this subjective self-reported outcome was measured immediately after an unblinded intervention designed specifically to increase intention, assessment was likely influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article states a priori hypotheses, including that intervention participants would have higher “*scores on intention, perceived behavioural control, self-efficacy and anticipated regret*.” It also describes the intention scale and reports the immediate post-intervention ANCOVA for intention. This provides some reassurance that intention was a planned outcome.

   However, there is no trial registration, protocol, or statistical analysis plan available here that was demonstrably finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. Consequently, it cannot be confirmed that the specific reported result—immediate post-intervention intention analysed using ANCOVA controlling for baseline intention—was selected from multiple possible outcome measurements or analyses without reference to the results.

   Multiple cognitive outcomes were measured, and intention was measured both before and immediately after the intervention. Alternative analyses were also possible, including unadjusted post-intervention comparisons, change-score analyses, and analyses with different covariate sets. The report does not provide sufficient prospective analysis details to rule out selective selection of the reported result. There is no direct evidence that the reported intention result was selected based on statistical significance, so **high risk of bias** is not warranted; nevertheless, the absence of a prospectively finalized analysis plan gives **some concerns**.
