1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that women were “**assigned randomly via random number tables**” to supportive counselling, stress management, or alcohol-focused couples therapy (AFCT). This is an adequately random method of sequence generation. Baseline comparisons did not identify important imbalances: the authors report no significant pre-treatment differences in RSS or GHQ scores (nor demographic variables) between conditions.

   However, there is no information on allocation concealment—e.g., whether the random-number-table allocation was administered by an independent person, centrally, or using sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. Thus, it is unclear whether recruiters or therapists could foresee assignments before women were enrolled. Under RoB 2, otherwise adequate random sequence generation with no information on concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and therapists were necessarily aware of assigned treatment, since the three psychotherapy conditions were distinct. Nevertheless, for the assignment effect, awareness alone is not sufficient for bias: there must be deviations from protocol caused by the trial context that could affect the outcome and that were unbalanced between groups.

   The apparent differences in delivery were consistent with the protocol rather than deviations from it. In AFCT, husbands were invited to participate, but “**if the man refused to participate in therapy, the woman received the stress management treatment**.” This was an explicitly specified feature of the AFCT strategy, not an unintended departure. The low rate of husbands engaging in AFCT (“**Only six husbands of the 21 wives assigned to AFCT engaged in treatment**”) therefore reflects the effect of being assigned to the AFCT engagement strategy.

   The investigators also report use of treatment manuals, videotaped sessions, and weekly supervision “**to ensure adherence to the treatment protocol**.” The RSS analysis appears to retain women in their randomized treatment groups among those with observed data; there is no indication of an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis. Exclusion of those with missing follow-up data is addressed in the missing-outcome-data domain, rather than here. There is no evidence that trial-context deviations, other than the intended AFCT engagement strategy, differentially affected women’s burden/distress.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not available for all, or nearly all, randomized women. Of 61 randomized participants, “**Forty-seven participants completed treatment and 44 of these participants provided assessed data at 6-month follow-up**.” Thus, only about 72% (44/61) provided follow-up data, corresponding to approximately 28% attrition by follow-up.

   The RSS results in Table 1 are based on 44 participants overall (17 supportive counselling, 14 stress management, 13 AFCT), indicating that the reported repeated-measures analysis was effectively restricted to those with available data at the relevant assessments. No appropriate analysis or sensitivity analysis is reported to demonstrate robustness to the missing data.

   Importantly, missingness was plausibly related to the true burden/distress outcome. The authors state that “**most of these women reported they had left their partners soon after beginning the programme**” and that “**all the women who dropped out from treatment declined further assessment**.” Leaving a severely distressed, alcohol-affected relationship is likely related to women’s burden, distress, and wellbeing, and may have changed these outcomes substantially. Although dropout did not significantly differ by treatment group, the authors acknowledge that this test had low power. The extent of missing data, its relationship to major post-randomization circumstances, and absence of analyses addressing it support a **high risk of bias**.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Women’s burden and distress were measured using self-reported questionnaires: the Relative Stress Scale (RSS) and General Health Questionnaire (GHQ). These appear to be established instruments, so the measurement methods themselves were not inappropriate.

   However, these are participant-reported, subjective outcomes, and the women knew which intervention they received. The interventions were conspicuously different: supportive counselling was non-directive, whereas stress management included cognitive restructuring, behavioural strategies and homework; AFCT included efforts to engage the male partner. Therefore, knowledge of treatment assignment could plausibly influence how women perceived and reported burden and psychological symptoms.

   This risk is particularly relevant because the intervention target was explicitly women’s perceived stress and coping. The report itself describes the outcome as “**the women’s reported stress**” and reports differences in “**reported psychological symptoms**.” There was no participant blinding, nor is there evidence of blinded outcome collection that would remove bias for a self-reported outcome. Given the subjective nature of RSS/GHQ outcomes and likely expectancy or reporting effects in an unblinded psychotherapy trial, outcome assessment was likely influenced by knowledge of treatment received.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report identifies women’s burden and distress as a primary outcome area and specifies the RSS and GHQ as the relevant measures, assessed at pre-treatment, post-treatment, and 6-month follow-up. Results for both measures and all three time points are presented, which provides no direct evidence that a favourable burden/distress measure or time point was selected for reporting.

   However, no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the information provided, and the trial registration does not provide sufficiently detailed, prospectively dated analysis intentions to establish that the specific RSS/GHQ analyses, time points, handling of missing data, and repeated-measures models were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. Multiple eligible outcome measurements (RSS and GHQ), time points (post-treatment and 6-month follow-up), and analytical choices were possible.

   In the absence of a pre-specified, prospectively finalized analysis plan—and without clear evidence excluding selection among measures, time points, or analyses—the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is **some concerns**, rather than high risk, because there is no direct evidence that the reported burden/distress result was selected on the basis of its findings.
