1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the study reports sex-stratified randomization and balanced baseline characteristics, but allocation concealment using “sealed envelopes” is insufficiently described to establish that concealment was secure.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors report that “the distribution of subjects to the two treatment groups was performed using sex-stratified randomization, and the allocation sequence was blinded through the use of sealed envelopes.” This supports a *probably random* allocation sequence. Baseline characteristics also do not indicate a material problem with randomization: baseline VAS was similar (control median 7.0 versus experimental median 7.4; p=0.421), and groups had identical sex distributions (10 men and 17 women in each group).

   However, the report does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, or opened only after irrevocable enrolment. Under RoB 2, merely describing “sealed envelopes” does not provide enough assurance that the allocation sequence was concealed. There is no direct evidence of allocation subversion or problematic baseline imbalance, so high risk of bias is not warranted, but the incomplete concealment information raises some concerns.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to active versus placebo ultrasound at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and outcome assessors were reported to be blinded: the study was conducted “with blinding of patients and blind assessor.” The control group received sham ultrasound—“the ultrasound was off (sham US)”—while otherwise receiving the same treatment sequence as the experimental group. Thus, the design sought to maintain comparable co-interventions: both groups received 25 sessions, including massage and exercises, and after the first 10 sessions both received the same 15-session protocol without ultrasound.

   The treating physiotherapist could not be blinded, as acknowledged by the authors: “The blinding of the physiotherapist who applied the treatment was not possible.” However, the report identifies no deviations from the protocol, differential additional interventions, or differential implementation arising from this lack of therapist blinding. The authors also state that “the analysis was done by intention to treat,” and no participant was “lost or dropped out.” Therefore, participants appear to have been analysed in their assigned groups, consistent with estimation of the effect of assignment.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data appear to have been available for all randomized participants. The trial randomized 54 participants, 27 per group, and explicitly states: “During the study no patient was lost or dropped out.” The participant flow figure likewise shows 27 participants in each group progressing through treatment and final assessment.

   VAS is a continuous outcome, and complete follow-up for all 54 randomized participants means that missing data could not have materially affected the reported VAS comparison. No imputation procedures or exclusions due to missing VAS data are reported.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Pain was measured using a visual analogue scale (VAS), an appropriate and commonly used participant-reported measure of pain. The same measurement procedure and time points were used in both groups: pain was assessed before the first session, before the sixth session, before the eleventh session, and at completion of the 25th session.

   For VAS, the participant is the outcome assessor. The report states that patients were blinded and that the control intervention was placebo/sham ultrasound. Therefore, participants were probably unaware of whether they received active or placebo ultrasound. Although treating physiotherapists were unblinded, there is no indication that pain assessment procedures differed between groups or that the assessor influenced VAS reporting. The sham-controlled design and blinded participants substantially reduce the likelihood that knowledge of assignment biased self-reported pain.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report presents VAS results at more than one follow-up point and also reports several pain-related measures, including VAS and algometry at four anatomical locations. The authors report no statistically significant between-group VAS difference after 10 sessions (p=0.373) but a statistically significant difference at discharge after 25 sessions (p=0.021). Thus, there were multiple eligible outcome time points and potentially multiple possible analyses, including comparisons of change/improvement scores at different follow-up times.

   The publication does not provide a dated, sufficiently detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan confirming that the reported VAS result—particularly the discharge result—was selected according to plans finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The supplied material also does not include the contents of the referenced trial-registration record, so it is not possible here to verify whether the VAS measure, time points, and analysis approach were prospectively specified and consistently reported.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported discharge VAS result was selected because it was statistically significant; therefore, high risk of bias is not justified. Nevertheless, in the absence of verifiable prospective analysis intentions, the possibility of selecting from multiple VAS time points and analyses results in some concerns.
