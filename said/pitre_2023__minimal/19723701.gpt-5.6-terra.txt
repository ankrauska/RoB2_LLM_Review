1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because randomization was stated and baseline characteristics were well balanced, but the available report does not describe allocation concealment or provide sufficient detail to verify that the allocation algorithm included an unpredictable random component.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial reports that patients “were randomly assigned in a 3:2 ratio” and that randomization was “stratified according to clinical center and ischemic status with the use of an algorithm that ensured near balance in each stratum.” The registry likewise specifies a randomized, parallel-group design with stratification by centre and ischemic status. These features support a probably random allocation process in a large multicentre trial.

   However, neither the article nor registry describes how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random sequence or random element within the balancing algorithm) or how allocations were concealed from personnel enrolling participants. RoB 2 requires adequate concealment for a low-risk judgement. Baseline characteristics were generally very similar between groups (e.g., age, sex, ejection fraction, QRS duration, ischaemic status, and medication use), with no imbalance suggesting a failure of randomization. Nevertheless, absence of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to CRT–ICD versus ICD alone.

   b. **Rationale:** This was an unblinded device trial: “The treating physicians were aware of study-group assignments,” and the registry specifies no masking. Deviations from the assigned device occurred: 91 ICD-only patients received CRT–ICD during follow-up, including 30 before reaching an endpoint, while 82 CRT–ICD patients received an ICD-only device because of technical difficulty positioning the CRT lead.

   However, for the requested effect of **assignment** to intervention, these deviations do not themselves imply bias unless they arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, and were likely to affect the outcome in an unbalanced manner. The reported CRT-to-ICD changes were mainly due to technical implantation difficulties, and most ICD-to-CRT crossover occurred after a heart-failure event, so could not affect the first-event primary outcome. There is no clear evidence that differential non-protocol care or deviations arising specifically from the trial context biased the primary outcome.

   Importantly, the primary analysis followed the intention-to-treat principle: “Data analysis was performed according to the intention-to-treat principle.” The registry confirms that “Data from all patients enrolled were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis.” Participants were therefore analysed according to randomized assignment despite crossover or nonreceipt of the assigned device. This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment at baseline.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias because outcome follow-up was incomplete, the amount of missing/censored follow-up differed between groups, and the reasons for missingness could plausibly be related to the risk of death or heart-failure events.

   b. **Rationale:** The primary result was a time-to-first-event analysis of death or nonfatal heart failure. The publication reports that “44 patients in the CRT–ICD group (4.0%) and 55 in the ICD-only group (7.5%) declined to continue participating in the study, were withdrawn by a physician, or were lost to follow-up.” Thus, follow-up was not available for all randomized participants, and the proportion was appreciably higher in the ICD-only group.

   For this composite clinical outcome, withdrawal by a physician, participant discontinuation, or loss to follow-up could be associated with underlying health status and hence with the subsequent probability of death or heart failure. The imbalance in missing/censored follow-up between treatment groups further raises concern that censoring may have been informative. Neither the article nor registry reports outcome data for these participants, reasons separately by treatment group, analyses adjusting for informative censoring, or sensitivity analyses showing that plausible outcomes among missing participants would not materially change the hazard ratio. Therefore, under RoB 2, missingness is likely to depend on the true outcome value, leading to **high risk of bias**.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias because the composite outcome was driven by nonfatal heart-failure events whose clinical detection and management decisions were initially made by unblinded treating physicians.

   b. **Rationale:** Death from any cause is an objective outcome and is unlikely to be influenced by awareness of assignment. However, the primary composite was predominantly driven by heart-failure events: 151 of 187 primary events in the CRT–ICD group and 167 of 185 in the ICD-only group were heart-failure events, whereas there were relatively few first-event deaths.

   Although final adjudication was blinded, the initial diagnosis and clinical actions establishing a heart-failure event were made by unblinded physicians. The authors state: “The diagnosis of heart failure, which was made by physicians who were aware of study-group assignments,” required signs and symptoms consistent with heart failure and treatment with intravenous decongestive therapy or augmented therapy during hospitalization. These criteria involve clinical judgement and decisions about treatment or admission—decisions that may be influenced by knowledge of whether a patient received CRT–ICD or ICD alone.

   The investigators explicitly acknowledge this limitation: “Members of the heart-failure adjudication committee were unaware of study-group assignments, but the investigators who decided on therapy or hospital admission for patients with heart failure were aware of such assignments. It is possible that the investigators’ knowledge of study-group assignment contributed in some way to the lower frequency of heart failure in the CRT–ICD group.” Blinded adjudication cannot fully remove bias if unblinded clinicians differentially initiate admission or treatment leading to event ascertainment. Since heart-failure events made the dominant contribution to the composite outcome, this is judged **high risk of bias**.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because the reported primary outcome is consistent with the registered primary outcome, but there is insufficient publicly available information to establish that the precise analysis was prespecified in a detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial registry prespecified the primary outcome as “Mortality From Any Cause or First Heart Failure (HF) Event,” measured over an average follow-up of 2.4 years. This is consistent with the publication’s primary endpoint: “death from any cause or a nonfatal heart-failure event (whichever came first).” The registry also identifies an intention-to-treat, time-to-first-event analysis using a stratified Cox model/log-rank test, consistent with the published primary estimate: hazard ratio 0.66 (95% CI 0.52–0.84).

   The publication describes a group-sequential design with prespecified interim monitoring and stopping boundaries, and reports that the trial was stopped after the ninth planned interim analysis crossed the prespecified efficacy boundary. These are reassuring features.

   Nevertheless, the registry record does not provide a sufficiently detailed, dated statistical analysis plan to confirm all aspects of the reported analysis—such as the exact handling of censoring and missing outcome data, the final analysis dataset, and all analytic choices—were finalized before unblinded data were available. The article refers to a previously published design/protocol, but that full protocol and a dated statistical analysis plan were not available in the supplied material. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected from multiple outcome definitions or analyses on the basis of its findings, but the absence of a fully accessible prespecified analysis plan warrants **some concerns** rather than low risk.
