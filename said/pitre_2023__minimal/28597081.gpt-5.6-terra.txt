1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The study reports an adequately random allocation process and reasonable allocation concealment. Participants were allocated using “permuted block randomization with randomly varying block sizes of 3, 6, and 9” through an online randomization tool. The allocation list “was transmitted by a non-involved person using serially numbered sealed opaque envelopes.” Randomly varying block sizes reduce predictability of assignment, and use of sealed, opaque, serially numbered envelopes administered by a person not involved in the trial supports concealment.

   The analysed sample was balanced in size across groups (24 participants each in BRIGHT, BLUE and CONTROL), with no reported baseline imbalance suggesting a problem with randomization. Although the report does not explicitly state that envelopes were opened only after irreversible enrolment, the described safeguards are sufficient to judge allocation concealment as probably adequate.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants were aware of their own assigned light condition: “Participants only knew the light condition they were exposed to.” However, the test supervisor was blinded: “The test supervisor (RK) was blinded to the light condition each participant was exposed to.”

   For the assignment-effect estimand, participant awareness leads to bias only if it causes deviations from the protocol that arise because of the trial context, are likely to affect acoustic reaction time, and are unbalanced between groups. The report provides no evidence of such trial-context deviations or differential co-interventions. All groups underwent the same testing schedule, including the same reaction-time task before and after the exposure period, and the intervention period was standardized as 60 minutes.

   Actual light exposure varied from intended intensity, particularly in the BRIGHT and BLUE groups, but this concerns variability in the intervention received rather than evidence that participants were analysed contrary to their randomized assignment. The principal analysis used analysis of covariance comparing randomized groups and adjusted for baseline reaction time: “Analyses of covariance were used to compare reaction time … after the light exposure between the three groups adjusted for reaction time … before the light exposure.” This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment, provided participants remained analysed in their randomized groups.

   Two participants were retrospectively excluded because of invalid baseline peak-oxygen-uptake measurements. These exclusions were related to verification of the eligibility criterion rather than acoustic reaction-time results, and only two of 74 randomized participants were excluded. There is no indication that this small exclusion was related to intervention assignment or outcome values.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Of 74 randomized participants, two were excluded retrospectively because of “invalid measurements of peak oxygen uptake in the cardiorespiratory fitness test.” The remaining 72 participants were included, with 24 in each group. Thus, outcome data were available for approximately 97% of randomized participants.

   For this continuous outcome, availability of data for more than 95% of randomized participants would generally be considered “nearly all” under RoB 2, particularly where the missing/excluded data are unlikely to materially alter the estimated intervention effect. The reason for exclusion was unrelated to post-intervention acoustic reaction-time performance and was not described as differing systematically by group. There is therefore no persuasive reason to believe that missing acoustic reaction-time data depended on their unobserved values.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Acoustic reaction time was measured using a standardized, computer-based simple acoustic reaction-time task under the same room-light conditions for all groups. The report states: “Immediately before, as well as immediately after the light exposure, participants performed a computer-based simple acoustic reaction time task.” The acoustic signal occurred randomly at intervals of 3–7 seconds over five minutes, and participants responded using the computer keyboard. The participant-specific median reaction time was used.

   This is an objective, automated timing-based outcome measurement with little opportunity for assessor judgement. Although participants knew their own light condition, the outcome measurement itself was computer generated and based on recorded response times; it was not dependent on a subjective judgement by an unblinded assessor. The same task, timing, thresholds and outcome-processing approach were used across intervention groups. Consequently, knowledge of intervention assignment is unlikely to have materially influenced measurement or ascertainment of the outcome.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial registration identifies reaction time in milliseconds as a secondary outcome. The publication also explicitly states that reaction time was a “pre-specified secondary outcome” and that the statistical methods used for reaction time “were pre-specified and registered.” The reported primary analysis—post-exposure reaction time compared between randomized groups using analysis of covariance adjusted for baseline reaction time—is a plausible and appropriate analysis.

   However, the available registration record does not provide a sufficiently detailed, time-stamped statistical analysis plan for acoustic reaction time. In particular, it does not specify the measurement time point, the use of the median reaction time, handling of reaction-time lapses, the ANCOVA model, baseline adjustment, or a hierarchy among the several reported analyses. The publication reports both allocated-group analyses and additional analyses relating actual light intensity to reaction time. Thus, there were potentially multiple eligible analyses and measurements, but the registration information is insufficient to establish that the reported numerical result was selected according to an analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported reaction-time result was selected because of its magnitude or statistical significance; therefore, high risk of bias is not warranted. Nevertheless, the lack of a detailed pre-specified analysis plan supports a judgement of some concerns.
