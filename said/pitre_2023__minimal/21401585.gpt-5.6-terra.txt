1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was generated using a random method and was concealed from study personnel responsible for enrolment. Baseline characteristics, including the baseline MUUL score, were broadly comparable between groups and do not suggest a problem with randomization.

   The report states: “children were randomized to pairs using a **computer generated list of random numbers and concealed envelopes opened by non-study personnel**.” This supports both random sequence generation and allocation concealment.

   Matching was performed before randomization by age, sex, side of hemiplegia, and MUUL function. After allocation, the authors reported that “there was **no baseline difference between groups**.” The baseline MUUL means were 67.1% in CIMT and 70.8% in BIM; this modest difference is compatible with chance in a relatively small trial and is not evidence that randomization failed.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to CIMT versus BIM at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and those delivering the interventions were necessarily aware of treatment assignment because CIMT involved wearing a constraint glove and BIM involved explicitly bimanual activities. However, there is no indication of deviations arising from the trial context that were unbalanced between groups and likely to affect MUUL.

   Both groups received a comparable intervention dose and setting: “Each intervention was delivered… for **6 hours daily for 10 days**… (total **60 hours** of intervention).” The interventions were delivered in the “same environment,” by a core team, with the “same amount and overall content of intervention,” modified only to reflect the intended unimanual versus bimanual nature of the assigned treatment.

   Adherence was high: “Compliance was **100% for CIMT**, and **97% with BIM training**.” One BIM participant withdrew early because of pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties. The report also states that concurrent therapy did not change for participants, apart from one CIMT participant receiving botulinum toxin between 3 and 26 weeks.

   The analysis was stated to be “on an **intention-to-treat basis**” and used generalized estimating equations including repeated baseline, 3-week, and 26-week measurements. Although one participant did not complete the intervention and the analysed sample appears to be 63 rather than 64 randomized children, participants were analysed by randomized group; this does not indicate an inappropriate as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis. For the assignment effect, ordinary non-adherence not caused by the trial context is not itself a source of bias in this RoB 2 domain.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome-data completeness was high. Sixty-four children were randomized, and the report identifies one dropout from the BIM group on day two, owing to pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties. The baseline table and reported analyses include 32 CIMT and 31 BIM participants, indicating outcome data for 63 of 64 randomized children (approximately 98%).

   The authors describe “high retention with very few drop-outs with either intervention.” The missing participant represents a very small proportion of the randomized sample. For a continuous outcome such as MUUL, data available for approximately 98% of participants are sufficiently complete that the missing participant’s unobserved outcome is unlikely to have made an important difference to the estimated CIMT-versus-BIM effect.

   In addition, the reported reason for withdrawal—pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties—does not clearly indicate that the missing 26-week MUUL value depended on the participant’s true MUUL outcome. There is no reported evidence of differential loss to follow-up related to upper-limb outcome or treatment response.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** MUUL is an established structured assessment of unilateral upper-limb movement quality, applied similarly in both treatment groups at baseline, 3 weeks, and 26 weeks. Most importantly, the outcome was assessed from video recordings by raters masked to allocation.

   The report states: “The primary outcome measures were **video-taped and scored in random order by trained occupational therapists masked to group allocation**.” Thus, although the therapists/physiotherapists conducting in-person outcome assessments knew allocation, the relevant MUUL scoring—the numerical outcome used in the comparison—was undertaken by blinded independent raters.

   The MUUL also showed good reliability in this study: the intraclass correlation coefficient for the total MUUL percentage score was “**0.93** (95% CI 0.84–0.97).” The same measurement procedures and timing were used across groups, with no suggestion that ascertainment differed by intervention. Consequently, knowledge of assigned intervention was unlikely to influence the scored MUUL outcome.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** MUUL was clearly identified as a primary outcome, and the study reported results at the prespecified assessment occasions of baseline, 3 weeks, and 26 weeks. The publication reports the between-group MUUL comparison at 3 and 26 weeks, including the 26-week result relevant here: estimated mean difference 4.4 (95% CI 2.2 to 6.7; p<0.001), favouring CIMT. This reduces concern that the reported result was selected from only a favourable follow-up time point.

   The article also describes an intention-to-treat longitudinal GEE approach, with adjustment for matching characteristics. The protocol paper cited by the article describes the trial and its primary outcomes prospectively.

   However, low risk of bias under RoB 2 requires assurance that the precise analysis producing the assessed 26-week MUUL result was conducted according to a sufficiently detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. Neither the article nor the available registration information provides a dated, detailed statistical analysis plan specifying all analytic decisions relevant to this result—for example, the exact model specification, covariate adjustment, handling of missing data, and choice between final-value and change-score analyses. Because MUUL could plausibly be analysed in more than one eligible way, the absence of a fully documented pre-unblinding analysis plan leaves some concern about selection of the reported analysis, although there is no direct evidence that selective reporting occurred.
