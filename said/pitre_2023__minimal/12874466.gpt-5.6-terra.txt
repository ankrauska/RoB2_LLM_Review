1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias. The allocation sequence was computer generated and was concealed from those conducting the trial through third-party management of the allocation list and medication packaging. Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable; observed numerical differences are plausible in a 60-participant trial and do not clearly demonstrate a failure of randomization.

   b. **Rationale:** The investigators reported that: “Individuals were randomized by a **computer-generated list**, which was used for packaging of the tablets and then maintained by a **third party that was not involved in the conduction of the study**.” This supports both a random sequence and adequate allocation concealment.  
   
   The groups contained equal numbers (30 per group), consistent with the intended allocation. The report states that “There was no significant difference in any baseline parameter between the two groups.” Although there were numerical imbalances in some comorbidities (e.g., heart failure and cerebrovascular disease), these were not clearly implausible by chance in this small trial and do not constitute persuasive evidence of a problematic randomization process.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to oral sodium bicarbonate versus placebo at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This was a placebo-controlled, blinded trial. The bicarbonate and placebo tablets had “identical” appearance, packaging and labelling, and both had “artificial mint flavor to give an identical taste.” The authors state: “Both patients and investigators were blinded.” Thus, participants, carers and investigators were unlikely to have been aware of assigned intervention during follow-up.

   The study also used an appropriate intention-to-treat approach for the effect of assignment: “Analyses were intention to treat irrespective of adherence to treatment regimen.” Importantly, although seven participants discontinued study medication because of adverse effects, “biochemical tests and nutritional assessment were continued for 52 wk according to the study protocol.” This reduces concern that treatment discontinuation caused post-randomization exclusion or analysis in the wrong group.

   No evidence was reported of trial-context-related, outcome-relevant deviations from the assigned intervention that were unbalanced between groups.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias. Outcome data for SGA were not clearly available for all or nearly all randomized participants at each assessment time, and the extent, distribution and handling of missing SGA measurements were insufficiently reported. Deaths and other events that could prevent SGA assessment occurred more often in the placebo group and are likely related to nutritional/clinical status.

   b. **Rationale:** The SGA results table reports means and standard deviations at each time point but does not report the number of participants contributing SGA data at 12, 24, 36, and 52 weeks, nor does it provide a participant flow diagram or describe how missing SGA observations were handled in the repeated-measures analysis.

   During follow-up, two participants died in the treatment group and five died in the placebo group. In addition, one treatment participant underwent transplantation and one participant in each group transferred to long-term haemodialysis. Participants who died could not provide subsequent SGA scores. Consequently, SGA data cannot have been complete through 52 weeks for all 60 randomized patients.

   The greater number of deaths in the placebo group means that missingness was likely differential between groups. Further, death and clinical deterioration are plausibly associated with the true nutritional-status outcome measured by SGA. The report does not present sensitivity analyses or a justified statistical approach demonstrating that these missing data could not have materially affected the estimated intervention effect. Under RoB 2, this combination supports high risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias. SGA was measured using an established structured system by trained observers who were blinded to treatment allocation and biochemical results.

   b. **Rationale:** SGA is a subjective, observer-assessed nutritional outcome and therefore could be vulnerable to knowledge of intervention assignment. However, the trial took appropriate measures to prevent differential outcome assessment. The authors reported that SGA “was performed at 0, 12, 24, 36, and 52 wk by **two trained observers who were blinded from the treatment group allocation and biochemical results** of the patients.”

   The outcome method was described in sufficient detail: the “four-item seven-point system” was used, assessing weight change, anorexia, subcutaneous tissue, and muscle mass. The observers were trained before the trial and achieved “a Cohen’s kappa concordant coefficient for agreement of 0.84,” indicating strong interobserver agreement. The same scheduled assessment approach was used in both groups. Therefore, there is no indication that outcome measurement differed between groups or that knowledge of assigned treatment influenced SGA assessment.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns. SGA was specified as the primary outcome and was reported at the planned follow-up times, but there is no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan available to verify that the particular reported SGA analysis was selected without knowledge of the results.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication explicitly identifies SGA as the primary outcome: “The primary outcome measure was the SGA score.” It also reports SGA results at the scheduled assessment time points (0, 12, 24, 36, and 52 weeks) and reports the prespecified longitudinal repeated-measures analysis: “repeated measures ANOVA was used, with the nutritional indices (for example, SGA score) as the repeated measure [and] treatment group as the between-group factor.”

   Nevertheless, the report does not provide a date-stamped statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The trial registration information available for this assessment does not provide sufficient detailed pre-specified analytic intentions to exclude selection among potentially eligible SGA analyses—for example, alternative time points, analysis populations, covariate specifications, or approaches to missing data. There is no direct evidence that selective reporting occurred, but the absence of a sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan leads to some concerns under RoB 2.
