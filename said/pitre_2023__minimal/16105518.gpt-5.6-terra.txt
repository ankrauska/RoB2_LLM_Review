1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the report states that participants were randomly assigned but does not describe sequence generation or allocation concealment. Baseline characteristics by randomized group are also not reported, so successful randomization cannot be checked.

   b. **Rationale:** The article states that, after the pre-test, participants “**were randomly assigned to three experimental groups**.” However, it gives no information on whether the sequence was computer-generated or otherwise random, nor on whether allocation was concealed from those enrolling or assigning participants. There is also no baseline comparison table for the Functional-order, Random-practice, and Blocked-practice groups. Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to *some concerns* when there is no evidence of a baseline imbalance suggesting a definite problem.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and personnel were necessarily aware of the allocated practice schedule: the interventions consisted of visibly different practice arrangements (functional-order whole-task practice, random-order part practice, or blocked part practice). However, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding is problematic only if it leads to deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context and were likely to affect the outcome.

   The intervention procedures appear to have been delivered as planned:
   - Functional-order participants practiced the complete task in “**three 20-minute practice sessions**.”
   - Random-practice participants practiced the five skills in random order.
   - Blocked-practice participants completed “**five 12-minute practice sessions**” with three consecutive trials of each skill.
   - “**All participants in all groups practice[d] each skill 3 times for 2 minutes and 10 seconds.**”
   - Faculty feedback was applied across groups: “**an orthopedic faculty member circulated within the group systematically to provide feedback to the trainees**.”

   There is no reported evidence of differential co-interventions, departures from the allocated schedules, or trial-context deviations that would have affected checklist scores. The group comparison used the randomized practice groups in mixed ANOVAs of pre- to post-test difference scores; no as-treated or per-protocol reclassification is reported. Therefore, the analysis appears appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment, while uncertainty regarding missing data is addressed separately.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that 28 students consented and were assigned, but it does not provide a participant flow diagram, the number randomized to each group, the numbers assessed at each post-test, withdrawals, exclusions, or reasons for missing checklist data. Although the authors state that “**for each participant, difference scores were calculated**,” this does not establish that outcome data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants.

   The outcome is a continuous performance score, and it is plausible that missing post-test data—if any—could be related to participant performance, attendance, or engagement. No sensitivity analysis, imputation strategy, or other analysis demonstrating robustness to missing outcome data is reported. Under RoB 2, absence of information about the extent of missing outcome data will usually result in a high-risk judgement, particularly where it is not possible to determine whether missingness was related to the true outcome value.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The operation-specific checklist was a potentially appropriate measure of technical performance: it was developed from actions selected by a panel of orthopedic surgeons as necessary for effective performance of the task. The same testing task appears to have been used across groups and test phases, so there is no clear evidence that the method of ascertainment differed systematically between groups.

   However, the outcome was assessed by “**two experienced orthopedic surgeons**,” and the report does not state that these assessors were blinded to participants’ allocated practice schedule, test phase, or study hypothesis. Checklist scoring of operative performance requires observer judgement, even when the checklist is operation-specific. The study’s hypothesis and intervention groups were readily distinguishable, and the authors expected that practice order would influence learning. Thus, knowledge of the allocated practice schedule could plausibly influence scoring.

   Because assessor blinding is not reported and the outcome is observer-rated performance with scope for subjective judgement, RoB 2 indicates a high risk of bias in outcome measurement. This judgement does not imply that bias definitely occurred, but that the report provides insufficient protection against differential assessment of the checklist outcome.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The available materials do not provide a dated protocol, statistical analysis plan, or sufficiently detailed trial-registration record finalized before outcome data were available. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that the reported checklist analysis followed a pre-specified analysis plan.

   The article does describe the planned checklist outcome and analysis in the Methods: “**The difference scores for the practice-specific checklists, final product analysis, global ratings, and drilling times were analyzed in 4 separate mixed analyses of variance**.” It also reports the group effect for checklist change scores and states that there were no differences between immediate and delayed post-tests: “**There were no differences on this measure across the 2 post-tests**.” This reduces concern that only one of the two post-test time points was selectively reported.

   Nevertheless, change-from-baseline checklist scores could potentially have been analysed in several eligible ways—for example, as immediate and delayed post-test outcomes separately, as final scores adjusted for baseline, or as change scores—and there is no prospectively finalized analysis plan to establish that the reported analysis was selected independently of the results. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was favourable, so *some concerns*, rather than high risk of bias, is appropriate.
