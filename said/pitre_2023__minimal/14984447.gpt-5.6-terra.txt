**Effect of interest:** effect of assignment at baseline to no-cost glucometer strips versus requiring participants to purchase additional strips.

## Outcome: SBGM frequency

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The report states that patients were stratified by income and “**randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion to two groups**,” with 31 participants per group. However, it does not describe how the random allocation sequence was generated or how allocation was concealed until enrolment. Baseline characteristics were broadly similar between groups (including baseline self-reported SBGM frequency, HbA1c, diabetes type, insurance, income, and insulin use), and group sizes were equal, so there is no indication from the baseline table that randomization failed. Nevertheless, lack of information on sequence generation and, particularly, allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily had some awareness of their assigned strategy: the no-cost group received 100 test strips per month whereas the control group received one initial package and was instructed to purchase further strips. However, this awareness is inherent to the intervention being evaluated. The report does not identify deviations from the protocol that arose because of the trial context and that were likely to affect SBGM frequency differentially between groups.  
   
   The analysis was described as intention-to-treat: “**Efficacy data from the intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward, were analysed**.” Although last-observation-carried-forward has implications for missing outcome data, it retains participants in their randomized groups and is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment for this domain. Bias associated with dropout and imputation is considered under missing outcome data.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Attrition was substantial and markedly imbalanced: at 12 months, “**there were 25 patients remaining in the NC group and 16 patients in the C group, i.e. a dropout rate of 19% in the NC group and 48% in the C group**.” Thus, outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants.  
   
   The imbalance is plausibly related to the intervention and potentially to the outcome. The authors state that patients in the control group who found strips too costly “**may well have dropped out of the study**,” and also acknowledge that patients with poorer glycaemic control might have “**did less testing or dropped out of the study altogether**.” These explanations indicate that missingness may have depended on true SBGM behaviour and/or glycaemic status. Last-observation-carried-forward was used, but no sensitivity analysis demonstrates that this approach removed or rendered negligible the resulting risk of bias.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** SBGM frequency was objectively obtained by “**downloading the glucometer memory**,” rather than relying on participant recall or self-report during follow-up. Both groups received the same model of glucometer and the same download procedure was used at two-monthly visits. The research nurse recorded the data and “**did not provide any feedback to the patient concerning glucometer readings**.”  
   
   Although participants knew whether strips were supplied free of charge, this may influence their actual monitoring behaviour—which is the intended mechanism and outcome of the intervention—not the objective measurement of monitoring frequency stored in the meter. There is no indication that measurement or ascertainment differed between groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies SBGM frequency as an endpoint measured at each visit and reports that the frequency was higher in the no-cost group, particularly “**during the first 4 months**.” However, the supplied materials do not include a dated protocol, statistical analysis plan, or usable trial-registration information establishing that the precise time points and analytic approach were finalized before unblinded outcome data became available.  
   
   Multiple eligible time points were measured over 12 months, and the report emphasizes selected periods of follow-up. There is insufficient evidence to conclude that the reported result was selected on the basis of its statistical significance or direction, so high risk is not justified; however, the absence of a pre-specified analysis plan leads to some concerns.

## Outcome: HbA1c

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** As for SBGM frequency, patients were reported to be “**randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion**” after income stratification, but the report does not describe random-sequence generation or allocation concealment. The baseline HbA1c values were very similar (9.1% in the no-cost group and 9.2% in the control group), and other reported baseline prognostic characteristics were balanced. There is therefore no clear evidence that randomization was unsuccessful, but insufficient information on the randomization process precludes a low-risk judgement.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants could likely determine their allocation because one group received continuing free strips and the other had to purchase strips. Nevertheless, no evidence indicates that trial-context deviations from the assigned strategies occurred and were unbalanced in a manner likely to influence HbA1c. Participants were instructed to continue routine physician/endocrinology visits at their usual frequency, and the research nurse “**did not provide any feedback**” on glucometer readings, random glucose, or HbA1c results.  
   
   The report states that efficacy data were analysed in the “**intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward**.” This preserves analysis according to randomized group, which is appropriate for the effect of assignment. Any bias from the missing data and its handling is addressed separately.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** The same substantial, differential attrition applies to HbA1c: 19% dropout in the no-cost group versus 48% in the control group. Hence HbA1c data were unavailable for an important proportion of randomized participants, especially in the control group.  
   
   The authors’ discussion makes it plausible that dropout was related to cost, engagement with monitoring, and possibly glycaemic control: they note that participants with “**intrinsically poorer glycaemic control**” may have “**did less testing or dropped out of the study altogether**.” HbA1c in participants who discontinued therefore may have differed systematically from HbA1c in those retained, and this process may have differed by randomized group. The use of last-observation-carried-forward does not demonstrate that the result is robust to plausible missing-data assumptions. This supports a high-risk judgement.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** HbA1c was measured objectively using the “**DCA 2000 Analyser**” at baseline and each subsequent study visit. The same measurement method and schedule were used in both intervention groups. HbA1c is a laboratory-based physiological outcome with little scope for differential influence by participants’ or personnel’s knowledge of intervention assignment. The report provides no indication that outcome ascertainment differed between groups or that assessors’ awareness could have influenced the analyser result.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** HbA1c was identified in the article as an endpoint measured at every two-month visit, and the analysis is described as using a general linear model with baseline values and time as covariates. The report states that the HbA1c difference was “**most apparent at the 6th month of the study**,” despite repeated measurement over the 12-month follow-up.  
   
   No dated, sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan or trial-registration record was available in the supplied material to confirm that the reported time points, model, and HbA1c result were selected before unblinded data were available. Because there is no direct evidence that the HbA1c result was selected from multiple analyses or time points on the basis of its findings, high risk is not warranted. However, the lack of verifiable prospective analysis intentions raises some concerns.
