# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Smoking Abstinence (Breath CO < 3 ppm)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), per instructions.

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, primarily due to lack of information about allocation concealment and a significant baseline imbalance in education level between groups.

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation had a random component but was constrained/systematic: "Random assignment to one of the three groups was accomplished by randomly assigning 2 participants to the percentile group, 2 to the fixed criterion group and 1 to the random incentives group from each group of 5 participants completing intake. Randomization was stratified by intent to use medication...and by order of study entry." This is a random element within a restricted/blocked design → answer **Yes** to random sequence.
- No information is provided regarding allocation concealment (e.g., whether the sequence was concealed from research staff enrolling participants). This is a **No information** response to the concealment signalling question.
- Baseline imbalance: "A higher proportion of percentile incentive participants achieved this level of education, relative to fixed criterion participants (χ2 = 8.46, p < 0.01)." This is a statistically significant imbalance in a baseline characteristic (education), though not clearly established as a strong prognostic factor for breath-CO abstinence, and the p-value, while <0.01, is based on one comparison among many baseline variables tested (Table 1 has numerous baseline comparisons), so some of this could reflect multiple testing/chance rather than a randomization failure.
- Per the algorithm: random sequence = Yes/Probably yes, concealment = No information, baseline imbalance suggestive of a problem = Yes/Probably yes → this combination maps to **"Some concerns"** (not "High," since sequence generation had a genuine random element and only one of many baseline comparisons was significant).

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, due to lack of blinding combined with plausible deviations related to trial context that could differentially affect groups, though there is no evidence these substantially biased the ITT estimate, and appropriate ITT-type analysis was used.

**b. Rationale:**
- Participants and staff were clearly aware of intervention assignment: "Percentile incentive participants were told that the value of the breath CO changed to help them smoke less. Fixed criterion incentive participants were told that they could only earn incentives for not smoking." No blinding was possible or attempted (open-label incentive schedules), so signalling questions 2.1/2.2 = **Yes**.
- Potential trial-context-driven deviations: Awareness of the differing incentive schedules could plausibly influence participant behavior distinctly per group (e.g., percentile participants' behavior shaped explicitly by feedback on breath CO levels each visit: "Participants were also told what their breath CO criterion was for their next visit"), which is intrinsic to the assigned intervention (not a non-protocol deviation) rather than a deviation from intended intervention. There is no clear evidence of non-protocol interventions or contamination between groups reported.
- The analysis included all randomized HTT participants in their assigned groups; there is no indication of switching participants between analysis groups or of a naïve per-protocol/as-treated analysis being used instead of ITT. Data presented (e.g., Figure 1 event records, attendance rates) appear to reflect full randomized cohorts including those with no abstinent samples. This supports an appropriate ITT-consistent analysis (signalling question 2.6 = Yes).
- However, because participants/personnel were aware of assignment (unblinded), and the study intervention is behavioral/motivational by nature such that trial-context effects (e.g., differential engagement, reactivity to feedback) cannot be ruled out, and no explicit information is given to rule out contextual effects on the outcome, we judge **"Some concerns"** rather than "Low," consistent with the algorithm for unblinded trials with no clear evidence about problematic deviations.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, due to substantial and differential attendance/missing data across groups without full sensitivity analysis addressing whether missingness depended on true outcome value.

**b. Rationale:**
- Considerable missing data: "The average number of delivered breath CO samples per participant (out of 60) was 42 (SD = 22), 40 (24), and 52 (19) for the percentile, fixed criterion and random incentive groups, respectively." This indicates roughly 13-30% of visits were missed on average, with high variability (large SDs), and this differed significantly between groups: "The number of delivered breath CO samples was different between groups (H = 8.57, df (2), p < 0.02)."
- Missingness (non-attendance) is very plausibly related to the true (unmeasured) smoking/abstinence status — participants who resumed smoking heavily may be more likely to stop attending ("could depend on its true value" — signalling question 3.3 likely **Yes**), which is a common concern in smoking cessation trials.
- No sensitivity analyses were reported to assess robustness of the primary outcome results to these missing data (e.g., worst-case/best-case imputation), and outcome data were not available for all participants (many failed to attend all 60 visits).
- However, while attendance differed between groups overall, the key percentile vs. fixed-criterion comparison for missed visits was not significantly different ("there was no difference in the number of delivered breath CO samples between percentile and fixed criterion incentive participants (z = −0.10, p = 0.92)"), somewhat reducing (but not eliminating) concern for the primary comparison of interest.
- Given incomplete outcome data, no correction/sensitivity analysis, and plausible dependency between missingness and the true outcome, but no strong direct evidence that missingness likely depended on true outcome value differentially between the two key groups (percentile vs. fixed), the judgement is **"Some concerns"** rather than "High."

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias. The outcome (breath CO < 3 ppm) is an objective, biochemically verified measure that is not susceptible to influence by knowledge of intervention assignment.

**b. Rationale:**
- The method of measurement was appropriate and standardized: "Vitalograph CO monitors...were used to take breath CO samples. Participants were required to take a deep breath, hold it for 20 sec, and then to expire over 20 sec into the disposable mouthpiece of the CO monitor. The peak breath CO reading was taken as the participant's breath CO level." This is a validated, objective biochemical measure (Javors, Hatch & Lamb, 2005, cited to justify the <3ppm cutoff), not susceptible to subjective interpretation.
- Measurement/ascertainment procedures were identical across all three groups — same device, same protocol, same visit schedule and same personnel procedures regardless of group assignment. No differential surveillance or additional testing occurred based on group.
- Although outcome assessors (research staff administering the breath test) were not blinded to intervention assignment (since incentive schedules were openly implemented at each visit), this is an **observer-reported outcome not involving judgement** (an automated/objective biochemical readout), which per RoB2 guidance is "usually not likely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention received."
- Therefore, despite lack of blinding, the objective nature of the outcome measurement supports a **Low risk of bias** judgement for this domain.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias. The outcome and its definition (breath CO < 3 ppm) were pre-specified and consistently reported, and available registration information does not indicate selective reporting for this outcome.

**b. Rationale:**
- The primary abstinence outcome (breath CO < 3 ppm) is clearly and consistently defined throughout the manuscript, used identically in the eligibility/HTT classification phase and the main trial phase: "We used an abstinence breath CO criterion of < 3 ppm based on our previous smoking cessation studies."
- Multiple pre-specified analyses relating to this single outcome definition are reported comprehensively and consistently (proportion achieving ≥1 abstinent sample, time to first abstinent sample, total abstinent samples, consecutive abstinent samples/maintenance, second-consecutive-sample analysis) — there is no indication that alternative CO cutoffs, alternative time windows, or alternative abstinence definitions were tested and selectively withheld.
- Analyses reported (Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney, chi-square) appear consistent with a coherent, apparently pre-specified analytic plan tailored to the study's hypothesis (percentile vs. fixed comparison), with no evidence of switching outcome metrics or subgroup-selective reporting based on results.
- Cross-checking against the trial registration record did not reveal evidence of discrepancy in outcome definition or reveal additional pre-specified outcomes for this measure that were dropped or altered based on results.
- Therefore, there is no clear evidence that this particular result was selectively reported from multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses → **Low risk of bias**.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

Given "Some concerns" in the randomization, deviations from intended intervention, and missing outcome data domains (with no domain rated "High"), the overall judgement for this outcome (smoking abstinence, breath CO < 3 ppm) is **Some concerns**, per Table 1 criteria: "The study is judged to raise some concerns in at least one domain for this result, but not to be at high risk of bias for any domain."
