1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article states that allocation used a random component: “*Using the random number table method, 90 cases were divided into group A and group B (n=45).*” This supports random sequence generation. However, there is no information on allocation concealment—e.g., who held the random-number table, whether assignments were generated centrally, or whether sealed opaque envelopes were used. Thus, it is unclear whether enrolling personnel could foresee assignments.  
   
   The groups were equal in size (45 per group) and reported as comparable for age, sex and tumour size, with no reported statistically significant baseline differences. However, baseline reporting is incomplete and internally inconsistent: the stated numbers with single and multiple nodules sum to 35 rather than 45 in each group. This does not itself establish failed randomization, but it reduces confidence in the baseline information. Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns** when there is no clear baseline imbalance suggesting a serious randomization failure.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants, surgeons and other care personnel were necessarily aware of intervention assignment because the comparison was between percutaneous microwave ablation and laparoscopic resection. The procedures are clearly different and blinding would not be feasible.  
   
   However, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding is problematic only if it led to deviations from the protocol because of the trial context that were likely to affect surgical time and were imbalanced between groups. The report describes the intended procedures for each group and provides no evidence of crossover, differential co-interventions, protocol failures attributable to the trial context, or differential deviations that could affect operative time.  
   
   Surgical-time results were reported for all 45 randomized patients in each group: “*Group A, n=45*” and “*Group B, n=45*” in Table 1. The group-based comparison of all randomized participants is consistent with an analysis of assignment, rather than an as-treated or per-protocol analysis. Therefore, there is no identified material bias due to deviations from intended interventions for this outcome.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Surgical time is reported for 45 participants in each group, corresponding to all 90 randomized participants. Table 1 reports mean surgical time for both groups: 96.7 ± 27.8 minutes for group A and 134.2 ± 34.3 minutes for group B.  
   
   There is no indication that any participants lacked surgical-time data, were excluded from the operative-time analysis, or had their outcome values imputed. As surgical time is recorded during the procedure, it would ordinarily be available for every treated participant. Although no CONSORT flow diagram is presented, the reported denominators match the randomized group sizes. Missing outcome data are therefore unlikely to have affected the result.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Surgical time is an objective intraoperative measure, generally derived from operative or anaesthetic records, and does not require subjective judgement comparable to patient-reported pain or clinician-rated recovery. The study states that “*Surgical time, intraoperative blood loss and incidence of complications were recorded for the two groups.*”  
   
   Although the report does not define the precise start and end points used for surgical time or state that outcome assessors were blinded, blinding is unlikely to be important for this objective outcome. There is no evidence that surgical time was measured differently in the two groups or that the method was unsuitable. The use of the same outcome—minutes of surgical time—for both groups supports comparable measurement. Knowledge of treatment assignment is unlikely to materially alter the recorded duration of surgery.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** Surgical time was specified in the article’s methods—“*Surgical time, intraoperative blood loss and incidence of complications were recorded*”—and was reported in Table 1, which is reassuring. The article reports a numerical result for each group and an associated statistical comparison.  
   
   However, no protocol or statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied material to establish that the surgical-time analysis, including its definition, analysis method, and reporting format, was pre-specified before unblinded outcome data were available. The referenced trial registration is identified only by document ID in the prompt; its substantive registration fields are not available here for comparison with the publication. In addition, surgical time could potentially be analysed or defined in more than one way (for example, procedure time versus total operating-room time), and the article does not clarify whether alternative definitions or analyses were considered.  
   
   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was favourable, but the absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan means that selective reporting cannot be excluded.
