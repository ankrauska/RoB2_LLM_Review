1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was generated using an appropriate random method and allocation was concealed until enrolment. The article states: “*Procedure type was assigned by a computer-generated randomization list, and concealment of randomization was done by telephone inquiry.*” Telephone allocation is a centrally administered method consistent with adequate concealment.

   Baseline characteristics reported in Table 1—age, gravidity, parity, body mass index/Quetelet index, body weight, height, and sonographically estimated uterine weight—do not demonstrate an imbalance clearly incompatible with chance. Although the median estimated uterine weight was numerically lower in the LAVH group (283 g) than in the AH group (369 g), the authors state that the groups were similar in preoperative uterine weight and do not report a statistically significant difference. There is therefore no clear indication of a problem with the randomization process.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to LAVH versus AH at baseline. Participants and those delivering care could not have been blinded to the surgical approach. The article explicitly acknowledges that “*the patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment*.”

   There was also an imbalance in the experience of personnel delivering the procedures: “*Twenty patients (71.4%) were operated on by an attending physician*” in the LAVH group, compared with “*eight (40%)*” in the AH group; this difference was statistically significant. The authors further acknowledge that “*the surgeons performing LAVH were significantly more experienced*.” Differential operator experience could plausibly influence postoperative recovery and length of hospital stay.

   However, the report does not establish whether this imbalance represented a deviation from the protocol that arose specifically because of the trial context, rather than reflecting usual implementation of the two procedures. There is also no indication that participants were analysed other than in their randomized groups: Table 2 reports hospital stay for all 28 LAVH-allocated and all 20 AH-allocated women. Thus, the analysis appears consistent with an intention-to-treat comparison, but uncertainty remains regarding potentially outcome-relevant differences in intervention delivery.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Length of hospital stay appears available for all randomized participants. The study randomized 48 women—28 to LAVH and 20 to AH—and Table 2 reports hospital stay separately for exactly these group sizes: LAVH, *n* = 28, and AH, *n* = 20.

   There is no indication that any participant was lost before discharge or excluded from the analysis of hospital stay. Since hospital stay is measured during the index admission and the numerical result appears to include every randomized participant, missing outcome data are unlikely to have affected the result materially.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Length of hospital stay is strongly influenced by the discharge decision. The study explicitly states: “*Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital.*” Thus, the outcome was at least partly a participant-driven discharge-decision outcome, rather than a fully objective outcome measured independently of participant or clinician judgement.

   Neither participants nor care providers could be blinded to whether LAVH or AH had been performed. Knowledge of the intervention could plausibly influence patients’ expectations, willingness to leave hospital, reporting of symptoms, and clinicians’ advice. This is particularly pertinent because the authors themselves note that patients’ “*expectations may have influenced*” postoperative outcomes. Moreover, LAVH was associated with lower reported pain, which could interact with subjective readiness for discharge.

   Because the outcome measurement/ascertainment depended on unblinded, judgement-based decisions that were likely to be influenced by knowledge of the surgical approach, the result for hospital stay is at high risk of bias in measurement.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies length of hospital stay as the primary outcome: “*We defined the primary outcome measure as length of hospital stay.*” It also reports the result numerically in Table 2, including medians and interquartile ranges for both groups (6.5 versus 10 days).

   However, there is no available dated statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the precise analysis of length of stay—such as the chosen summary measure, statistical test, and handling of any departures from the original plan—was finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. The trial was conducted from 1995 to 1997, and the available information does not provide sufficient prospectively finalized analysis details to exclude selection among possible analyses of this continuous outcome.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported hospital-stay result was selected from multiple time points or outcome definitions based on its statistical significance. Nevertheless, in the absence of a sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan, RoB 2 indicates **some concerns** for selection of the reported result.
