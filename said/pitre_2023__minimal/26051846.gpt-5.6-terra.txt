1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence appears random: “*a numbered list generated through the random permuted blocks method, applied by a statistician*.” Random permuted blocks are an appropriate random sequence-generation method. Baseline characteristics were broadly balanced across the three groups (e.g., age, sex, duration of symptoms, dominant shoulder, and pathology type; all reported baseline P-values >0.05), and baseline VAS values were reasonably similar (5.7, 5.7, and 6.3).

   However, allocation concealment is not described. Although the statistician “*was not involved in data collection or analysis*,” the report does not state whether the allocation list was inaccessible to recruiting/enrolling personnel until irrevocable enrolment, nor does it describe central randomization, an independent allocation service, or appropriately secured sequential envelopes. Therefore, concealment cannot be confirmed. Under RoB 2, absence of information about allocation concealment, with no compelling indication of a problem in baseline balance, leads to **some concerns**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial compared assignment to three clearly specified treatment strategies: ultrasound, dexketoprofen phonophoresis, and dexketoprofen iontophoresis, each in addition to standardized exercises and cryotherapy. The authors state that “*all participants completed five sessions per week (total of 20 sessions)*” and that treatments “*were provided by physiotherapists following standardised protocols*.”

   Physiotherapists necessarily appear likely to have known which treatment was delivered, particularly because iontophoresis and ultrasound/phonophoresis have visibly and procedurally different administration methods. Nevertheless, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of personnel blinding only causes bias if it results in deviations from the protocol that arose because of the trial context, affected VAS pain, and differed between groups. The report provides no evidence of such deviations, unequal co-interventions, treatment switching, or differential implementation. Exercise therapy and cryotherapy were given to all groups, and the authors explicitly note that their effects “*were the same for all treatment groups*.”

   The analysis used group-based repeated-measures ANOVA and between-group ANOVA. Since all 99 randomized participants reportedly completed the treatment sessions and no post-randomization exclusions or as-treated/per-protocol analysis is reported, the analysis appears consistent with analysing participants according to their assigned groups. Thus, there is no material evidence of bias due to deviations from intended intervention for the effect of assignment.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Ninety-nine participants were randomized—32 to ultrasound, 33 to phonophoresis, and 34 to iontophoresis—and the report states that “*all participants completed five sessions per week (total of 20 sessions).*” Outcome results are reported for each group at baseline, post-treatment, and one-month post-treatment, with no reported loss to follow-up, withdrawal, or missing VAS assessments.

   There is no indication that the post-treatment VAS analysis excluded randomized participants because of unavailable outcomes. Consequently, outcome data appear available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants. There is also no indication of differential missingness or outcome-related reasons for missing VAS data.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** VAS pain is a participant-reported, subjective outcome: “*The VAS assesses pain on a scale of 0 to 10, with 0 being no pain and 10 being the worst pain.*” For such an outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor.

   The registry describes the study as single-masked with the participant masked, whereas the article states that “*all visits were attended to by the same blinded rehabilitation doctor using standardised protocols.*” However, the publication does not explain how participants could remain blinded despite receiving substantially different, readily distinguishable interventions: ultrasound/phonophoresis involved 5 minutes of pulsed ultrasound, whereas iontophoresis involved galvanic direct current for 20 minutes. The report does not describe sham procedures, matching equipment/application times, or a formal assessment of whether masking was maintained.

   The blinded rehabilitation doctor does not remove the concern for VAS, because the participant supplies the pain rating. It is highly plausible that participants knew, or could infer, their treatment. Knowledge of treatment is likely to influence self-reported pain immediately after treatment, especially in a comparison of active physiotherapy modalities with differing expectations and sensations. Therefore, assessment of post-treatment VAS was likely influenced by knowledge of intervention received, resulting in **high risk of bias** in measurement of the outcome.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The registration record identifies VAS pain as the primary outcome—“*Pain measure by the visual analogic scale pain score*”—and the article reports VAS at the planned general assessment occasions of baseline, post-treatment, and one month post-treatment. This supports that pain was an intended outcome.

   However, the registry does not specify the VAS time point, analytic approach, comparison of primary interest, handling of missing data, whether final values or change-from-baseline values would be analysed, or a pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available. The publication reports several outcome measures (VAS, CMS, DASH), several time points, within-group changes, and multiple between-group comparisons across three intervention groups. Thus, there were multiple potentially eligible outcome measurements and analyses, but there is insufficient pre-specified detail to establish that the post-treatment VAS result was selected independently of the observed results.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported post-treatment VAS result was selectively chosen because it was statistically significant or favourable; therefore, high risk is not justified. Nonetheless, in the absence of a sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan, **some concerns** are warranted.
