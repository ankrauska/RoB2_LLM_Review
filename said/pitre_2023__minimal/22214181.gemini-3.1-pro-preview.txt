**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
a. Judgement: The trial utilized an adequate method for random sequence generation, but there is no information provided regarding allocation concealment. Baseline characteristics appear balanced, suggesting no immediate evidence of a problem with randomization, but the lack of concealment details raises some concerns.
b. Rationale: The authors state, "a Microsoft Excel random number generator was used to randomize participants into the two conditions," which is an appropriate method for sequence generation. However, the report does not describe any mechanism used to conceal the allocation sequence until participants were enrolled. The article notes, "No significant pre-intervention group differences were found," which is reassuring but insufficient to rule out a risk of bias due to lack of concealment.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Although participants and the study investigator delivering the intervention were aware of the assigned groups, there is no evidence that this led to deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context. The study design (a single 1-hour session) minimizes the opportunity for such deviations. 
b. Rationale: The intervention consisted of reading an information sheet with the investigator. Because it was a brief, single-session experiment ("The study procedures were completed within a 1-hour testing session"), the opportunity for non-protocol interventions or lack of adherence was negligible. The analysis included all eligible participants who completed the task.

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Outcome data were available for nearly all participants randomized, and the small number of excluded participants is unlikely to have biased the results.
b. Rationale: The authors report that out of the participants recruited, five were excluded for objective reasons ("Two participants were younger than 18 years... one participant did not complete the demographic information, and two participants did not complete the outcome measures"). The remaining 88 participants completed the study and were included in the analysis. The proportion of missing data is very small and unlikely to depend on the true value of the outcome.

**4. measurement of the outcome: some concerns**
a. Judgement: The outcome measure (List of attributions task) is a self-report tool, meaning the participants are the outcome assessors. Because participants were aware of the intervention they received (information about fatigue vs. sleep), their responses could potentially have been influenced by demand characteristics.
b. Rationale: The authors acknowledge this potential limitation: "Given the nature of this study’s pre–post intervention design, the information-based intervention may have influenced participants to respond in a certain manner... participants in FI may have been primed to consider non-sleep causes of fatigue." While a blinded laboratory volunteer coded the responses, the primary generation of the data was unblinded and subjective, raising some concerns for measurement bias.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
a. Judgement: There is no mention of a pre-published trial protocol or statistical analysis plan available to confirm that the reported analyses were strictly pre-specified before unblinded outcome data were analyzed. 
b. Rationale: Although the reported outcomes correspond to the measures described in the methods section (NumberAttributionScore, FrequencyScore, ImportanceScore), the absence of a pre-specified, date-stamped analysis plan means it cannot be entirely ruled out that the specific scoring methods or presented results were selected from multiple possible analyses. Therefore, there are some concerns regarding selective reporting.
