# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment
**Outcome: Intentions to consume the RDIFV**
**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention**

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The trial used a true random number generator to determine allocation order, and there is no evidence of problems with allocation concealment or baseline imbalance. This meets criteria for low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The authors state: "To ensure randomization, intervention and control booklets were placed in a single pile in an order determined by a sequence of random integers generated by a true random number generator (Haahr, 2004). The booklets from this single pile were then distributed in a lecture theatre and laboratory class, and completed under examination conditions." This describes a genuinely random sequence generation. Because booklets were pre-ordered into a single pile before distribution under examination conditions, participants and researchers could not have known or influenced which booklet (intervention or control) a given participant would receive, satisfying allocation concealment. Baseline comparability was confirmed: "MANOVA performed on pre-intervention behaviour, F(4,141) = 1.37, ns, and cognition measures, F(6,139) = .06, ns, revealed no significant differences between the control and experimental groups... suggesting that randomization was successful." No information suggests deviation from random allocation ratio or problematic imbalance.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Participants and those administering the intervention could not be blinded given the nature of the leaflet-based intervention (persuasive text and implementation intention prompts vs. a control questionnaire task). This awareness could plausibly have led to deviations arising from the trial context (e.g., differential engagement, demand effects) that could affect the intention outcome, though there is no strong evidence that this occurred, and an appropriate (apparent ITT-like/ANCOVA on all available cases) analytic approach was used.

**b. Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew which booklet (intervention vs. control task) they received, since the "control task consisted of questions about the questionnaire itself" while the intervention involved explicit persuasive messages and implementation-intention planning exercises. There was no placebo/sham task designed to blind participants to condition, and the study does not report any blinding of research personnel administering the booklets. Because intention was measured via self-report immediately after the manipulation, participants' awareness of the intervention content itself is inherent to the manipulation (not a "deviation"), but the possibility of trial-context-driven effects (e.g., participants inferring the study's purpose and adjusting responses due to demand characteristics rather than true belief change) cannot be excluded and is not addressed by the authors. There is no reported evidence of non-protocol interventions, and analysis appears to include all randomized participants for whom post-intervention data were recorded ("Univariate ANCOVAs were used to test the difference between post-intervention means between conditions, controlling for pre-intervention scores"), consistent with an appropriate ITT-style analysis for this immediately-measured outcome. Given awareness of allocation without evidence of harmful trial-context deviations affecting this immediate cognitive outcome, but no explicit assessment of this issue by the study authors, a judgement of "Some concerns" is appropriate rather than "High risk."

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** For the intention outcome (measured immediately post-intervention, before follow-up), data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants, so missing outcome data are not a concern for this particular outcome.

**b. Rationale:** The intention outcome assessed here is the **post-intervention** measure taken immediately after the intervention/control task, not the 1-week follow-up behavioural outcome. The article states: "In total, 218 people completed the booklet... After completing the control or intervention task, participants completed a second set of cognition measures identical to those they completed before the intervention/control task." The cognitive analyses (Table 4, ANCOVAs on intention) were based on the full sample of 218 (e.g., "Univariate ANOVAs showed that there were no significant pre-intervention differences on intention... between the intervention and control group" and post-intervention ANCOVA on intention used df consistent with the full sample, F(1,215)). Thus, essentially all randomized participants contributed data to the post-intervention intention measure, as it was collected within the same testing session before attrition occurred (attrition of 33% applied only to the 1-week follow-up, not to the post-intervention cognitive measures).

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Intention was a self-reported outcome, meaning participants themselves were the outcome assessors and were necessarily aware of their intervention assignment; because this is a participant-reported outcome, assessment could plausibly be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received (e.g., demand characteristics, desire to appear consistent with persuasive messages just read).

**b. Rationale:** Intention was measured via self-report items such as "I intend to eat the RDIFV every day over the next week, definitely no, definitely yes." As a participant-reported outcome, "the outcome assessor here is the participant," and per RoB2 guidance, participant-reported outcomes are potentially influenced by knowledge of intervention received, warranting at least "Some concerns." Given that the intervention included direct persuasive messages explicitly designed to increase intention ("Make a firm decision now that you will eat the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables each day this week"), and the outcome was measured immediately afterward in the same session, there is a plausible risk that responses were influenced by social desirability or demand characteristics (i.e., participants reporting higher intention because they just read messages urging them to form such intentions), rather than reflecting a genuine change in underlying motivational state. However, there is no strong direct evidence that this occurred (e.g., no manipulation check for demand effects), so "High risk" is not assigned. The method of measurement itself (validated multi-item scales with good internal consistency, α = .90) was not inappropriate, and there is no indication that measurement differed structurally between groups (both groups completed identical post-intervention measures). This supports "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** No trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is available/referenced for this study, so it cannot be determined whether the reported intention analyses were selected from among multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses on the basis of the results.

**b. Rationale:** The provided trial registration document could not be matched to a pre-specified, date-stamped analysis plan for this specific study (no protocol, trial registry entry, or SAP is cited or discussed in the article). The article reports a single, clearly defined 5-item intention scale with consistent operationalization pre- and post-intervention (α = .90 and .93), and there is no indication of multiple alternative operationalizations of intention (e.g., different scales, time points) being tested with only one reported. Similarly, the hierarchical regression analyses (Tables 2 and 3) appear systematically presented rather than selectively chosen. However, given the complete absence of documented pre-specified analysis intentions to verify against, and given that this is an exploratory-appearing hypothesis-testing study with four hypotheses and multiple cognitive/behavioural outcomes, the possibility of selective reporting (e.g., choice of covariates, choice of ANCOVA vs. other models, choice to report anticipated regret and intention specifically as the significant cognitive findings out of the full set of TPB constructs measured) cannot be excluded. This leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" per the RoB2 decision algorithm for cases with "no information" regarding pre-specification.

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Although no domain was judged to be at high risk of bias individually, three domains ("deviations from intended interventions," "measurement of the outcome," and "selective reporting") were judged to raise "some concerns." Per RoB 2 guidance (Table 1), "'Some concerns' in multiple domains may lead the review authors to decide on an overall judgement of 'High' risk of bias for that outcome," particularly here because the domains of concern are interrelated and mutually reinforcing: unblinded participants who are also the outcome assessors for a self-reported outcome measured immediately after a persuasive intervention represents a substantial and compounding threat to the validity of the intention outcome specifically. This combination substantially lowers confidence in the reported effect of the intervention on intentions to consume the RDIFV.
