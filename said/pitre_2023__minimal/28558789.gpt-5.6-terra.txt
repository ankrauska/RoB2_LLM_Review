1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** The randomization sequence was computer generated, and allocation was concealed from participants, trainers and assessors. No important baseline imbalance suggesting failure of randomization is evident, although the sample was extremely small.  
   b. **Rationale:** The publication states that “an offsite investigator prepared the concealed randomization using a computer generated random number sequence (1:1).” Allocation was placed in “consecutively numbered opaque envelopes” and obtained after baseline assessment by the tRNS intervener. The prospective registry likewise specifies “a computer generated random number sequence” and concealed allocation prepared by an independent person. The four participants were allocated equally (two active tRNS and two sham tRNS), as intended. Table 2 shows variation in age, disability and mobility, but no striking or implausible imbalance sufficient to indicate a problem with randomization. Although the report does not describe tamper-proof envelope procedures in full detail, the independent, offsite preparation and allocation after baseline assessment provide reasonable assurance of concealment.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** For the effect of assignment to active versus sham tRNS at baseline, there is no evidence that deviations arising from the trial context biased the MAS6 result, and all randomized participants appear to have been retained in their allocated groups.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants, trainers and outcome assessors were reported to be blinded: “Study personnel involved in assessment and training, along with all participants were blinded to group allocation for the duration of the study.” The tRNS intervener necessarily knew allocation, but was separate from training and assessment, and stimulation parameters were kept in a separate locked log. Both groups received the same reaching-training programme, electrical stimulation as required, electrode placement and session schedule; the intended difference was active (2 mA) versus sham (0 mA) tRNS. Participants were told that they “may or may not feel the stimulation irrespective of whether they were receiving active or sham stimulation.”  

   All four participants completed all 12 scheduled sessions, and Table 3 reports MAS6 outcomes for each participant according to their randomized active or placebo/sham group. There is no report of crossover, differential co-intervention, unplanned treatment alteration, or post-randomization exclusion. The registry prespecified intention-to-treat analysis, and the reported individual-level results include all four randomized participants. Thus, there is no evidence of a deviation due to the trial context that would bias the effect of assignment.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** MAS6 data appear available for all randomized participants at baseline, post-training and follow-up.  
   b. **Rationale:** The report states: “All four participants completed the 12 training sessions over 4-weeks, with no training sessions missed.” Table 3 provides MAS6 values at T0, T1 and T2 for each of the four participants: P1, P2, P3 and P4. Therefore, for the assessed outcome there is no apparent loss to follow-up or missing MAS6 measurement at the reported assessment time points. No imputation or exclusion due to missing MAS6 data is reported.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** MAS6 was measured using the same established clinical scale at the same planned time points in both groups, by assessors reported to be blinded to allocation.  
   b. **Rationale:** The study identifies MAS6 as the “primary clinical outcome measure,” measured at baseline, post-training and follow-up. The trial registry prospectively specified assessment at weeks 0, 4 and 12. MAS6 is an established stroke-rehabilitation measure of upper-arm function, and there is no indication that its measurement method was inappropriate or differed between active and sham groups.  

   Importantly, “study personnel involved in assessment and training, along with all participants were blinded to group allocation for the duration of the study.” MAS6 involves assessor judgement during observation of upper-arm performance, so knowledge of treatment could potentially affect scoring. However, the reported blinding of assessors substantially mitigates this risk. There is no evidence of differential assessment procedures or unblinding of outcome assessors.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** There are some concerns because the prospectively registered primary outcome was a composite of MAS items 6–8, whereas the publication identifies and reports MAS6 separately as the primary clinical outcome. In addition, the registry specified a group-by-time mixed-effects analysis, while the publication presents descriptive individual case changes. There is insufficient evidence to conclude that the MAS6 result was selected on the basis of its findings; therefore, high risk of bias is not justified.  
   b. **Rationale:** The prospective registration, dated 4 September 2014 before enrolment, specified the primary outcome as: “Composite score of Motor Assessment Scale upper limb items 6-8,” assessed at weeks 0, 4 and 12. It also specified a “non-parametric, mixed effects model in a 2 group (active tRNS, sham-tRNS) × 3 phases (0, 4, 12 weeks) design … followed by between-groups comparisons.”  

   In contrast, the article states: “The primary clinical outcome measure was Motor Assessment Scale item 6 (Upper Arm Function, MAS6),” while items 7 and 8 are described as secondary measures. The reported results table provides MAS6 values at all three registered time points, but does not provide the registered composite MAS6–8 result or the planned group-by-time analysis. These discrepancies mean it cannot be confirmed that the reported MAS6 result was analysed exactly according to a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before outcome data were available. However, MAS6 was prespecified as part of the registered composite outcome, the time points were consistent with registration, and the report does not provide direct evidence that this component or analysis was selected because it was more favourable.
