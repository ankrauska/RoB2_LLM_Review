# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment
**Trial:** Ashman et al., "A Randomized Controlled Trial of Sertraline for the Treatment of Depression in Persons With Traumatic Brain Injury"
**Outcome assessed:** HAM-D (Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression) score
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The trial used an adequate random sequence generation method and a plausible allocation-concealment mechanism (central pharmacy dispensing with sequentially prepared, identically marked medications). Although the abstract notes a significant baseline difference in income between groups, this appears to be an isolated finding among many baseline comparisons and is consistent with chance given the small sample size (n=41).

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: *"random assignment to treatment conditions (sertraline or placebo) was balanced by using a block design (blocks of 8) with computer-generated random number sequences in individual presealed envelopes."* → clearly random (Y).
- Allocation concealment: *"The pharmacy implemented masking and random assignment of participants to sertraline or placebo conditions and was responsible for dispensing medications with identical markings to the project physicians for distribution."* This reflects a centrally controlled, concealed allocation process independent of enrolling clinicians (Y/PY).
- Baseline imbalance: The Results section states *"No significant differences were found between drug and placebo groups on sex; age; education; race; income; etiology of injury; time since injury; injury severity; or baseline measures of depression, anxiety, or QOL."* The abstract's statement about income appears to be a single flagged difference amidst many comparisons (Table 1 shows numerous baseline variables tested), consistent with a chance finding rather than evidence of a corrupted randomization process. Baseline HAM-D scores were similar (27.5±7.1 vs 25.2±8.0), the outcome of interest.
- Overall: sequence random (Y), allocation concealed (Y), baseline imbalance compatible with chance (N/PN) → **Low risk**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The trial was double-blind, with both participants and personnel blinded; no evidence is presented of unblinding or trial-context-driven deviations (e.g., non-protocol interventions) that affected the outcome. The primary analysis for group comparisons followed intention-to-treat/modified-ITT principles.

**b. Rationale:**
- Blinding: *"Double-blind, randomized controlled trial"*; *"both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received (ie, whether the participant was prescribed sertraline or placebo)."* Medications were *"packaged in 25-mg quantities and had the same markings so that increases in the number of pills taken in both drug conditions were identical."*
- No evidence in the report suggests participants or clinicians became aware of allocation status or that this awareness led to non-protocol interventions influencing the HAM-D outcome (e.g., no differential co-interventions, no crossover to non-protocol antidepressants — this was explicitly prohibited: *"abstain from seeking new psychosocial or pharmacologic treatments during the course of the intervention"*).
- Analysis approach: An intent-to-treat analysis was performed, supplemented by a modified analysis using last-observation-carried-forward for two participants who completed only 8 weeks: *"An intent-to-treat analysis compared the outcomes of all study participants... A last-observation-carried-forward procedure combined the 2 participants who completed 8 weeks with those who completed all 10 weeks of treatment."* This is consistent with an acceptable mITT approach (potential bias from the exclusion of true non-completers is addressed under the missing-data domain, not here).
- Therefore: participants/personnel blinded → no further deviation signalling questions triggered; appropriate analysis used → **Low risk**.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** A substantial proportion of participants (11/52, ~21%) had missing HAM-D outcome data, and there is direct evidence that missingness was related to the true (severity) value of the outcome, with some indication that reasons for dropout differed between arms.

**b. Rationale:**
- Extent of missing data: Of 52 randomized, only 41 completed the trial (22/27 sertraline = 81%; 19/25 placebo = 76%), a meaningfully unequal and non-trivial proportion, especially given the small total sample.
- Evidence that missingness depended on the true outcome value: *"The intent-to-treat analyses found differences between the last assessment HAM-D score for those who dropped out of treatment as compared with the assessment HAM-D score of those participants who remained in treatment (F=4.0, P=.052), with the dropouts tending to have higher HAM-D scores."* This is a clear (borderline-significant) signal that those with worse depression were more likely to leave the study — a classic mechanism producing missing-not-at-random data in a depression trial.
- Reasons for missingness differing by group: Clinically-driven dropouts (as opposed to logistic non-compliance) were concentrated in the placebo arm: *"a second [participant withdrew] because of increased depression after a death in the family 4 weeks into the treatment, and a third because of a mild allergic reaction 4 weeks into the treatment (the latter 2 were receiving placebo)."* This suggests that missingness related to worsening of the outcome domain occurred disproportionately among placebo recipients, raising the risk that the reported (completer-based) group means in Table 2 are biased.
- The circumstance of the trial (a depression trial in a population with TBI, low SES, poor engagement, "8 other noncompleters were noncompliant with the protocol by missing multiple follow-up appointments") is one in which continuing/worsening symptoms plausibly drive both non-adherence and missing follow-up assessments.
- No sensitivity analyses were reported that quantify the robustness of the primary result under alternative assumptions about missing data.
- Because the key outcome-level analyses (ANCOVA, random-effects models, Table 2) rely on the completer sample and there is credible evidence that this missingness is related both to the true HAM-D value and (at least partly) to group assignment → **High risk of bias**.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome (HAM-D) is an observer-reported outcome involving some judgement, but assessors were blinded to intervention allocation, and there is no indication that measurement/ascertainment methods differed between groups.

**b. Rationale:**
- Method of measurement: The HAM-D is a validated, standard instrument (*"widely used clinician-rated measurement of depression severity"* with established reliability and validity) — not inappropriate for this outcome.
- Comparable ascertainment: All participants were assessed on the same schedule *"every 2 weeks for the duration of the clinical trial"* using the same instrument, following the same procedure regardless of group.
- Blinding of assessors: *"a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist rated the clinical severity of the participant's depressive (HAM-D) and anxiety (BAI) symptoms independently from the physician at each biweekly visit"* within an overall double-blind design, and *"participants were not necessarily seen by the same staff member... at each visit,"* with a different assessor administering posttreatment vs baseline assessments. There is no reported evidence of unblinding of outcome assessors (e.g., no report of assessors guessing allocation or differential side-effect profiles revealing assignment).
- Because assessors were blinded and no evidence suggests this blinding was broken → **Low risk**.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome (HAM-D) and its analysis correspond to the pre-registered primary outcomes in the trial registry (NCT00233103), and the study reports multiple pre-specified/exploratory HAM-D-derived analyses (total score and 4 subscales) transparently, including both significant and non-significant results, rather than selectively suppressing unfavourable findings.

**b. Rationale:**
- Registry concordance: The ClinicalTrials.gov record lists as primary outcomes *"Depression at Baseline"* and *"Depression at End of Treatment"* using the HAM-D, exactly as reported in the article, with the same responder definition: *"Participants were considered treatment responders if their initial HAM-D decreased by 50% or dropped below a score of 10 at the end of the intervention."*
- Full reporting of the primary analysis: The main ITT/random-effects model result for HAM-D was reported as non-significant despite this being an unfavourable result for the sponsor-associated hypothesis: *"The group effect was NS (F HAM-D(1,222.93)=.481, NS)."*
- Additional subscale analyses were disclosed and clearly labelled as secondary/exploratory rather than substituted for the primary result: *"Two of the subscales, HamG and HamFive, did show a significant fixed effect for group... suggesting that 2 scales were more sensitive to changes in depression symptoms related to medication use."* Both significant and non-significant subscale findings were reported, reducing concern that only favourable comparisons were selected.
- No indication of selective outcome-measurement or selective-analysis reporting (e.g., no discrepant definitions of the outcome across sources, no evidence of withheld unfavorable time points or scales).
- Some residual uncertainty exists because no statistical analysis plan (SAP) was available to confirm all analyses were pre-specified before unblinding, but the consistency between registry, methods, and full transparent reporting of exploratory analyses supports a "Low risk" judgement rather than "Some concerns."

---

# Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement for HAM-D outcome: **High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Although randomization, deviation-from-intervention, outcome-measurement, and selective-reporting domains are judged at low risk, the domain of missing outcome data is judged at **high risk of bias** due to (1) a non-trivial proportion of missing HAM-D data (~21%), (2) documented evidence that dropout was associated with higher (worse) HAM-D scores, and (3) qualitative evidence that clinically-driven dropouts (related to worsening of the outcome domain) were concentrated in the placebo arm. Per RoB 2 guidance, a "High" risk-of-bias judgement in any single domain results in an overall "High risk of bias" judgement for this result.
