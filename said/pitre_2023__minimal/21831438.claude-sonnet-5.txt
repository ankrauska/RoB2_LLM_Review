# Risk of Bias Assessment: CAESAR Trial
## Outcome: Change in mean sitting DBP from baseline after 4 weeks of therapy
## Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- Signalling Q1.1 (sequence random?): The article states "Study patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio by using the mixed block randomization (4 and 6 blocks) method." This indicates a random component was used → **Yes**.
- Signalling Q1.2 (allocation concealed?): No information is provided in the article, protocol, or trial registry about the concealment mechanism (e.g., central randomization, sealed envelopes). This is an open-label trial, and there is no description of how allocation was concealed from investigators enrolling participants → **No information**.
- Signalling Q1.3 (baseline imbalance suggesting a problem?): The article explicitly states: "Although patients were randomized, the mean age in the candesartan-HCT groups (51.0 [10.2] years) was higher than that in the candesartan monotherapy group (47.8 [10.7] years; P <0.02)." This is a statistically significant baseline difference (~3.2 years) that the authors themselves flag as unexpected "despite random allocation." While a single significant baseline difference could occur by chance, the authors' explicit surprise, combined with lack of information on concealment, raises some concern about the randomization process → **Yes/Probably yes** for possible problem, though age difference alone is modest and plausibly a chance finding given multiple baseline comparisons were made (Table I).

Given: sequence was random (Yes), concealment unknown (No information), and there is a baseline imbalance (age) that is statistically significant but of a magnitude that could plausibly arise by chance among multiple compared baseline variables — per the algorithm, "No information" on concealment combined with a flagged baseline difference leads to **Some concerns** (not High, since only one variable among many was imbalanced and the difference, while significant, is not extreme, and other baseline characteristics were well-balanced, per Table I).

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- This was an **open-label study**: "Open-label, Randomised, 2-Arm Parallel Group... Study" (protocol) and masking is explicitly "NONE." Thus participants, carers, and treating physicians were all aware of assigned intervention (Signalling Q2.1 and Q2.2 = **Yes**).
- Because participants and personnel were aware of the intervention, the question of whether deviations from intended intervention arose *because of the trial context* must be considered (Q2.3). The article does not report any specific evidence of non-protocol interventions or trial-context-driven deviations (e.g., no mention of participants seeking alternate treatment because of dissatisfaction with allocation, or investigators preferentially treating one group). There is no explicit discussion of this issue → **No information**.
- The paper explicitly acknowledges limitations related to unblinding: "the open study design could be the potential source of bias regarding the safety profile," although this comment is made regarding AE detection rather than the BP outcome itself. No comparable caveat is given for the BP outcome, but the general absence of blinding creates a background risk of differential trial engagement effects (e.g., patients/investigators aware of the "less potent" monotherapy might increase lifestyle changes, engagement, or non-protocol antihypertensive use).
- Signalling Q2.6 (appropriate analysis for effect of assignment): Analysis was conducted using the ITT population, defined as "patients who had baseline BP values and ≥1 postrandomization BP values and who had taken ≥1 dose of the study drug" — this is consistent with a modified ITT approach with exclusion primarily related to missing outcome data, which is considered appropriate for this domain (excluding missing outcome data is addressed separately under Domain 3) → **Yes**.
- Because participants/personnel were unblinded (Q2.1/2.2 = Yes) and there is no information on whether trial-context deviations occurred and affected the outcome (Q2.3 = No information), and the analysis was appropriate (Q2.6 = Yes), the algorithm maps to **Some concerns** (per Table 6: "Either 2.1 or 2.2 Y/PY/NI" + "2.2 Y/PY" + "2.3 NI" → Some concerns for Part 1; Part 2 = Low). Combined judgement: **Some concerns**.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- Of 233 randomized patients, the ITT analysis for the primary DBP outcome at 4 weeks included only 111/117 (94.9%) in the combination group and 113/116 (97.4%) in the monotherapy group (Table II, III). This is reasonably high but not complete: "224" is the reported ITT total vs 233 randomized, with attrition described in Figure 2.
- Signalling Q3.1: Outcome data were available for most, but not "nearly all," randomized participants — a small number of participants were excluded from primary analysis for reasons including consent withdrawal, protocol violations, etc. (Figure 2 flow diagram) → **Probably yes/Probably no** (borderline; treated as "not all" but close).
- Signalling Q3.3/3.4: The protocol registry data show meaningful differential dropout by 8 weeks (though less pronounced at 4 weeks): "Discontinued" 20/117 (17%) in combination arm vs. 15/116 (13%) in monotherapy arm over the full study, with different reasons (lost to follow-up: 5 vs 1; withdrawal by subject: 4 vs 5; safety reason: 8 vs 7). At the 4-week timepoint specifically, the numbers analyzed (111 vs. 113) suggest relatively balanced and small amounts of missing data specific to the primary outcome.
- However, no explicit reasons for exclusion from the 4-week DBP analysis are provided distinctly from the overall study dropout data, and it is unclear whether any missingness is related to the true DBP value (e.g., a patient stopping due to poor BP control or adverse effects related to BP lowering) — this cannot be ruled out based on reported information.
- Given the relatively small amount of missing data at 4 weeks and lack of clear evidence of informative missingness, but no explicit reassurance (e.g., sensitivity analysis) that missingness didn't depend on the outcome, this is judged as **Some concerns** rather than Low or High.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- Signalling Q4.1 (inappropriate method?): BP was measured using "a fully automated device produced by Omron" with a standardized protocol (rested 5 minutes, three consecutive measurements, averaging after discarding extremes). This method is appropriate and objective → **No**.
- Signalling Q4.2 (differed between groups?): The measurement method (automated device, protocol for averaging) was identical in both arms, and "every attempt was made to have the same individual perform all BP readings in the same patient at each visit" → **No**.
- Signalling Q4.3 (outcome assessors aware of intervention?): This is an open-label study with no blinding. DBP was measured by investigators who were aware of the assigned treatment (candesartan alone vs. candesartan+HCT) → **Yes**.
- Signalling Q4.4 (could assessment be influenced by knowledge of intervention?): Although BP measurement was performed with an automated device (reducing subjective interpretation), unblinded assessors could subconsciously influence measurement through technique variability, patient positioning, timing relative to dosing, whitecoat effects, or reporting inclinations, especially since this is a continuous, somewhat operator-dependent outcome despite automation → **Yes/Probably yes**.
- Signalling Q4.5 (likely influenced?): There is no specific evidence that assessment was actually influenced by knowledge of the intervention (e.g., no evidence of differential technique or falsification), and the use of an automated device with a standardized protocol mitigates substantial concern → **Probably no**.
- This pattern (aware, could be influenced, but unlikely to be actually influenced given objective automated measurement) maps to **Some concerns** per the algorithm.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registry (NCT00621153) prospectively specifies the primary outcome as "Changes in Mean Sitting DBP From Baseline After 4 Weeks of Therapy," which matches exactly the outcome and result assessed in the article ("The primary objective of this study was to compare the changes in the mean sitting DBP from the baseline after 4 weeks of therapy..."). This alignment supports low risk for this specific measurement.
- However, there is a **discrepancy in the actual numerical results** reported between the published article and the trial registry results: the article reports ITT DBP change at 4 weeks as **-17.8 (10.2) mmHg** (combination) vs. **-14.1 (10.1) mmHg** (monotherapy), while the registry results report **-17.0 (SE 0.89)** vs. **-14.1 (SE 0.88)** using "LEAST_SQUARES_MEAN" rather than raw mean/SD. This suggests that multiple analytic approaches (raw mean change vs. ANCOVA-adjusted least-squares mean) were available, and it is unclear which was pre-specified as the primary analysis, or whether the reported result in the article (unadjusted descriptive change scores in Table II, with the ANCOVA-based P-value comparison) fully aligns with a single, pre-specified analysis plan.
- No full statistical analysis plan (SAP) was available to confirm that the analysis method (ANCOVA model with baseline BP as covariate, as stated in Methods) was finalized prior to unblinding of outcome data, and it's unclear whether alternative analyses (e.g., unadjusted vs adjusted) were selectively reported.
- Given the outcome measurement itself matches the pre-registered primary outcome (mitigating concern about selection among multiple outcome measurements), but there is ambiguity about whether the specific analysis (raw mean difference vs ANCOVA-adjusted) reported in the article was the single pre-specified analysis, and no SAP was available for verification, this is judged as **Some concerns** rather than Low risk.

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

**Rationale:** No domain was judged to be at High risk of bias. However, "Some concerns" were identified in four of the five domains (randomization process, deviations from intended intervention, missing outcome data, and selection of the reported result), and a borderline "Some concerns" for measurement of outcome. Per RoB 2 guidance, "Some concerns in multiple domains may lead review authors to decide on an overall judgement of 'High' risk of bias." However, none of the individual concerns are severe or clearly indicative of substantial bias (the randomization concern relates to one plausible chance imbalance; the open-label design's impact on an objective, device-measured outcome is limited; missing data was modest; and reporting concerns relate to analytic method transparency rather than clear evidence of selective outcome reporting). Therefore, the overall judgement is **Some concerns**, reflecting cumulative minor uncertainties rather than a single or compounding source of substantial bias.
