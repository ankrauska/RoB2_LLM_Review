1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Randomization used an appropriate, centrally administered process with allocation concealment. The report states that, after eligibility assessment, patients were “**randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio via a centralized Interactive Web Response System**.” Randomization was stratified by prior treatment intensity and relapse/refractory status, with “**a block size of four patients … within each stratum**.” These procedures support both a random allocation sequence and concealment until enrolment/assignment.

   Importantly, investigators selected and recorded the intended control regimen before randomization: “**To avoid bias, investigators chose and recorded a control treatment for each patient before random assignment.**” This reduces the possibility that knowledge of allocation influenced choice of comparator treatment.

   Baseline characteristics were generally similar between groups. There was an imbalance in the proportion aged ≥70 years (30% elacytarabine versus 18% control; *P*=.007), but mean and median ages were similar and there was no pattern of substantial imbalance across key prognostic characteristics. In the context of adequately centralized randomization, this isolated imbalance does not by itself demonstrate a failure of the randomization process.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to interventions at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This was explicitly an “**open-label, randomized phase III study**,” so participants and treating personnel were aware of assigned treatment. However, for the effect of assignment, lack of blinding causes bias only if it leads to protocol-inconsistent deviations arising from the trial context that are likely to affect overall survival and are imbalanced between groups.

   Some deviations occurred: six control-arm participants received treatment other than their pre-specified investigator’s-choice regimen. However, these deviations were few relative to the full randomized population (6/190), and the report provides no evidence that they arose because of trial-context effects or that they materially affected overall survival. Treatment exposure was broadly similar: “**The average number of treatment courses per patient was 1.4 in both arms**,” and 95% of patients receiving one course and 77% receiving a second course received at least 80% of assigned doses.

   The primary survival analysis was conducted in the intention-to-treat population: “**The intention-to-treat population comprised 191 patients in the elacytarabine group and 190 in the control arm**.” The reported primary OS result—HR 0.97 (95% CI 0.77–1.22)—therefore estimates the effect of assignment, including patients who were randomized but did not initiate treatment. This is appropriate for the specified effect of interest.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Overall survival data were available for nearly all randomized participants. Of 381 randomized participants, the analysis was triggered after 302 deaths/events. At the end of follow-up, only one patient in the elacytarabine arm and four in the control arm were lost to follow-up or withdrawn (5/381; approximately 1.3%).

   The report specifies appropriate survival-data handling: “**For patients confirmed to be alive on the cutoff date, this date was used to censor for survival**,” whereas “**for patients lost to follow-up before the cutoff date, censoring was done on the last date the patient was known to be alive**.” Administrative censoring at the common event-analysis cutoff is not missing outcome data. The very small number lost to follow-up is unlikely to have materially changed the overall-survival estimate, and losses were not markedly differential between arms (1 versus 4).

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Overall survival is an objective outcome: death or time to death. Although the trial was open label, knowledge of allocated treatment is not plausibly able to influence whether a participant dies or the date of death to an extent likely to affect the treatment comparison.

   The same survival definition and Kaplan–Meier/log-rank and Cox regression methods were used across treatment arms. The study states that the primary endpoint was “**overall survival (OS)**” and that OS was evaluated by Kaplan–Meier methods and compared using the log-rank test. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment, definitions, or follow-up procedures differed systematically between arms. Thus, measurement of overall survival was unlikely to be influenced by awareness of treatment assignment.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study was prospectively registered (NCT01147939), and both the registry identifier and publication identify overall survival as the primary endpoint. The publication is consistent with this: “**The primary end point was overall survival (OS)**.” The main ITT result is reported clearly: median OS 3.5 versus 3.3 months, HR 0.97 (95% CI 0.77–1.22), *P*=.96.

   However, there is insufficient information in the available materials to establish that the precise OS analysis was specified in a detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. Multiple potentially eligible survival analyses were reported, including the primary ITT analysis, an age-adjusted analysis, subgroup analyses, and an analysis censoring participants at stem-cell transplantation. In the absence of a dated, sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan confirming the intended censoring rules, covariate adjustment, and priority among these analyses, selective choice among analyses cannot be fully excluded.

   The concern is limited because the primary OS outcome and the principal ITT analysis appear pre-specified and are fully reported; nevertheless, the evidence is not sufficient for a low-risk judgement in this domain.
