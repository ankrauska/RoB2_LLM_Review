1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was generated using an appropriate random process: “a computer-generated list of random numbers” with “random permuted blocks with randomized order of block sizes” (block sizes 6 and 4). Allocation concealment was also adequately implemented using “sequentially numbered and sealed opaque envelopes,” which were concealed from researchers and outcome assessors until after consent.

   Baseline characteristics, including age, skeletal measurements, overjet, and mandibular crowding, were broadly comparable between the 16 participants in each group. Although some numerical differences occurred (e.g., MP/SN and MMP), there is no indication of substantial imbalance unlikely to be due to chance or of a compromised randomization process.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Patients and clinicians could not be blinded because the addition of mini-implants was apparent: “Blinding of the clinicians and patients to the interventions in each group was impossible.” However, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding leads to bias only where deviations from intended intervention arose because of the trial context, were unbalanced, and were likely to affect the outcome.

   The report does not describe important trial-context-related deviations from protocol that differed between groups. Both groups received the assigned appliance strategy until an edge-to-edge incisor relationship was achieved. The same follow-up procedures were described for appliance monitoring. Although treatment duration varied between individuals, the mean follow-up duration was similar between groups (FMI 5.08 months; FFRD 4.96 months; *P* = 0.80).

   The analysis was consistent with assignment to randomized intervention: “The primary analysis was planned to be an intention-to-treat analysis to involve all patients who were to be randomly assigned”; all randomized participants were included because there were no dropouts. Thus, there is no evidence that participants were analysed according to treatment received or excluded post-randomization.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were available for every randomized participant. The CONSORT flow diagram shows that all 32 participants were randomized, none were lost to follow-up or discontinued treatment, and all 32 were analysed (16 per group). The authors state: “The 32 surveys were retrieved from the patients; there were no dropouts,” and “there were no missing answers.”

   Therefore, missing outcome data could not have materially affected the estimated comparison of patient acceptance between the FFRD and mini-implant-anchored FFRD groups.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Patient acceptance was measured using questionnaires completed by the patients at appliance removal. This is a participant-reported outcome, so the patients themselves were the outcome assessors. The study explicitly states that patients could not be blinded to their intervention: “Blinding of the clinicians and patients to the interventions in each group was impossible.”

   Although the investigators attempted to blind data extraction, coding, and statistical analysis, this does not blind the participants’ assessment of their own acceptance, discomfort, functional effects, or satisfaction. The report states that “Blinding was done for outcomes assessment,” but the described procedure concerns removal of identifying information from questionnaires and blinding of the person extracting data/statistician. It cannot prevent participants from knowing whether mini-implants were used.

   Acceptance, satisfaction, noticeability, pain, eating, speech, sleep, and oral-hygiene effects are subjective outcomes for which knowledge of the received intervention could influence reporting. This is particularly pertinent because the comparison involves an additional invasive component (mandibular mini-implants), and the questionnaire included questions directly concerning ease of insertion, pain, swelling, and satisfaction. It is therefore likely that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received. The direction of any bias is uncertain.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article states: “This trial was not registered” and “The protocol was not published before trial commencement.” Accordingly, there is no prospectively registered outcome specification or pre-specified statistical analysis plan available to verify that the reported patient-acceptance result, questionnaire items, time point, and analysis were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The investigators report that there were “no outcome changes after commencement of the trial,” but this assertion cannot be independently checked against a dated protocol or trial registry. There were numerous questionnaire-derived outcome measurements—ease of insertion, noticeability, oral hygiene, speech, eating, sleep, external appearance, pain, gum bleeding, swelling, satisfaction, breakage, separation of components, and recommendation to others—and potentially multiple ways to summarize or analyse them. The report presents results for the questionnaire items, but absence of prospective analysis intentions means selective choice of outcome measurements or analyses cannot be excluded.

   There is no direct evidence that a particular acceptance result was selected because it was statistically significant, favourable, or otherwise noteworthy. Therefore, high risk of bias is not warranted; nevertheless, the lack of registration and a publicly available pre-specified protocol/analysis plan leads to some concerns.
