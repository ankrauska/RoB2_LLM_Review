# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Pain Intensity Outcome

**Preliminary considerations:** This is an individually-randomized parallel-group trial (3 arms: TENS+standard care, IFT+standard care, standard care alone). Effect of interest assessed: effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), as instructed.

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, primarily driven by a significant baseline imbalance in a variable (affected side) that plausibly relates to prognosis, combined with lack of detail on allocation concealment.

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: "computerized block randomization" was used — this indicates a random component. Signalling question 1.1 → Yes.
- Allocation concealment: "allocation group concealed in envelopes" — no detail is given on whether envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, or opened sequentially. This is insufficient to confirm adequate concealment. Signalling question 1.2 → No information/Probably no.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows a statistically significant difference in "affected side" between groups (χ²=5.952, P=0.015), which is below the conventional 0.05 threshold and notable given the small sample. Although "affected side" (right vs. left) is not an obvious major prognostic factor, it could plausibly relate to pain intensity given discussion in the article regarding laterality and muscular activity differences. Age also approached but did not reach significance (P=0.061).
- No information is given on random sequence generation method in detail (e.g., software, block sizes) or robust concealment procedures.
- Given: adequate concealment cannot be confirmed (no information) AND there is a borderline-significant baseline imbalance that could suggest a problem → this maps to "Some concerns" per the algorithm (Table 4: NI concealment + Y baseline imbalance suggestive of problem = Some concerns, moving toward High only if concealment was clearly inadequate).

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias due to lack of blinding of participants and personnel, with the outcome (self-reported pain) highly susceptible to influence by the trial context, particularly because the control group received no attention/sham modality equivalent to TENS/IFT device application.

**b. Rationale:**
- The study is explicitly described as "single-blind" — only assessor-blinding to allocation was attempted after randomization, but "the participants were blind to the allocation to treatment groups after randomization" is stated, yet this claim is undermined because participants would obviously know whether they received an active electrical modality (TENS/IFT) or not (control group only received hot pack/myofascial release/exercise). Signalling question 2.1 → Yes/Probably yes (participants aware, especially in comparing device arms vs. no-device control).
- "The principal investigator acted as both outcome assessor, taking all study measurements, and as therapist administering all study treatment" — this means personnel (and the outcome assessor) were entirely unblinded. Signalling question 2.2 → Yes.
- The authors explicitly acknowledge this as a limitation: "carrying out the clinical procedures and measurements by the principal investigator and not using a placebo for the patients of the control group could be considered as limitations of the present study."
- Since pain intensity is a self-reported (participant-reported) outcome, and participants in the control group knew they were not receiving an electrical stimulation device while TENS/IFT participants knew they were, deviations from intended intervention (e.g., differential expectation effects, contextual effects on reporting of pain) plausibly arose because of the trial context (lack of blinding, absence of sham device) — Signalling question 2.3 → Yes/Probably yes.
- These deviations (expectation/placebo effects from device application) were likely to affect the outcome (pain is highly susceptible to expectation effects) — Signalling question 2.4 → Yes.
- These effects were clearly unbalanced between groups (control had no device, TENS/IFT had a real device but no sham comparator) — Signalling question 2.5 → No (not balanced).
- Per algorithm, this pattern (aware, deviations occurred due to trial context, affecting outcome, unbalanced) → **High risk of bias**.
- Analysis: All 105 randomized participants were analyzed (100% completion), so an appropriate ITT-consistent analysis was used for Part 2 (Low risk for Part 2). However, Part 1 being High risk drives the overall domain judgement to **High**.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias — no missing outcome data.

**b. Rationale:**
- "There were 105 individuals who expressed interest in participating in the study. All completed the study and were entered into the analysis (Fig. 3)."
- The CONSORT flow chart confirms n=105 randomized, n=35 in each arm, and "post-treatment measurements (n=105)" — indicating complete outcome data for all randomized participants at all four measurement time points.
- Signalling question 3.1 → Yes (data available for all randomized participants) → Low risk of bias, no further questions needed.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias due to unblinded outcome assessment of a subjective, participant-reported outcome, with high likelihood that assessment/reporting was influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

**b. Rationale:**
- Pain intensity was measured via a 100-mm visual analog scale, a **participant-reported outcome**. The outcome assessor here is effectively the participant (self-report), and participants were not blinded to which intervention they received (device vs. no device), as discussed above.
- Additionally, "The principal investigator acted as both outcome assessor, taking all study measurements, and as therapist administering all study treatment" — the same unblinded individual who delivered treatment also recorded pain intensity scores, introducing further potential for conscious or unconscious bias in measurement/recording, particularly given the investigator's likely expectation that TENS or IFT would outperform standard care alone.
- Signalling question 4.3 (outcome assessors aware of intervention) → Yes.
- Signalling question 4.4 (could assessment be influenced by knowledge of intervention) → Yes, since pain intensity is a subjective, self-reported outcome highly susceptible to expectation and placebo effects, especially when compared against a no-device control.
- Signalling question 4.5 (likely that assessment was influenced) → Yes/Probably yes, given: (1) the comparator for two of the three arms was essentially "no treatment/attention" beyond standard care, which per RoB2 guidance raises higher risk of bias than active-vs-active comparisons; (2) the same person delivered treatment and recorded outcomes; (3) authors themselves flag absence of placebo as a limitation.
- This maps to **High risk of bias** per the algorithm (aware, could be influenced, likely influenced → High).

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, due to inability to confirm that the reported pain intensity analysis corresponds to a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial was registered ("the study was registered as a clinical trial with the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry"), which is a positive indicator; however, the published article does not present or reference specific pre-specified outcome definitions, time points, or analysis methods from the registry entry that can be directly compared against what was reported, based on the information provided in the doc_id source for trial registration.
- Pain intensity was measured at four consistent time points (baseline, immediately after first treatment, before eighth treatment, 1 week after eighth treatment), and all four time points are reported in Table 2 with consistent statistical methods (one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc) — there is no evidence of selective outcome-measurement or selective-analysis reporting within the article itself (i.e., no indication that other pain scales, other time points, or alternative analyses were conducted but omitted).
- However, without independent confirmation from the trial registry/protocol that this exact set of measurements, time points, and analytic approach was pre-specified prior to unblinding, and given no statistical analysis plan was referenced, signalling question 5.1 cannot be answered "Yes" with confidence — answered as "No information."
- Signalling questions 5.2 and 5.3 (selection from multiple eligible measurements/analyses) → No information/Probably no, as no clear evidence of selective reporting is apparent from the single available outcome measure and consistent analysis approach reported.
- Per the algorithm, "No information" on 5.1 combined with "No/Probably no" on 5.2/5.3 → **Some concerns** (rather than Low, because pre-specification finalized before unblinding cannot be confirmed).

---

## Overall Risk of Bias for Pain Intensity Outcome: **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Summary:** The overall judgement is High risk of bias, driven by High risk of bias judgements in two domains — "Deviations from intended intervention" and "Measurement of the outcome" — both stemming from the fundamental lack of blinding of participants, personnel, and the outcome assessor (who was the same person delivering treatment) in a trial assessing a highly subjective, self-reported outcome (pain), compounded by the absence of a placebo/sham comparator for the control group.
