**Outcome assessed:** severe peer aggression at 12 months, comparing the **computer-assisted therapist brief intervention (TBI)** with the brochure control condition (reported group-by-time odds ratio 0.40, 95% CI 0.23–0.69).

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because random assignment was stated and stratified by age and gender, and no important baseline imbalance is apparent, but the method used to generate and conceal the allocation sequence was not reported.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article reports that participants “were randomly selected (stratified by gender and age: 14–15, 16–18 years), and assigned to 1 of the 3 study conditions.” The registry similarly states that participants would be “stratified by age and gender and randomized to conditions.” However, neither source describes the random-sequence generation method (e.g., computer-generated sequence) nor allocation concealment (e.g., central randomization or secure envelopes). The authors report no significant condition differences in age, race, or gender, and baseline severe peer aggression was relatively similar across groups (TBI 82.7%; control 77.9%). Thus, there is no evidence of a failed randomization process, but insufficient information on concealment raises some concerns.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants and intervention personnel would necessarily have known whether a therapist/computer brief intervention or only a brochure was received. However, for the assignment effect, awareness alone does not establish bias: there must be deviations from intended intervention arising because of the trial context, which are unbalanced and likely to affect peer aggression. No such deviations are reported. The interventions were delivered during the ED visit as intended: “.95% received their assigned intervention during the ED visit.”  

   The analysis was appropriate for the assignment effect: “An intent-to-treat approach was used. All randomly assigned participants were included whether the intervention was received or not.” Participants were analysed according to random assignment, and the reported GEE analysis used available longitudinal data. There is no indication of an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis or of post-randomization exclusion because of intervention receipt.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because outcome data were missing for a non-trivial proportion of randomized participants, and the available information does not establish that missingness was unrelated to the unobserved peer-aggression outcome.  
   b. **Rationale:** Of 726 randomized adolescents, 607 completed 12-month follow-up, a follow-up rate of 83.6%; thus, outcome data were unavailable for approximately 16% of randomized participants. The article notes: “although attrition was low (14%) and an intent to treat analysis was conducted, it is always possible that those lost to follow-up may have biased results.”  

   The authors state that GEE analyses “use all data available for participants, including those lost to attrition,” but this does not by itself demonstrate that the result was robust to plausible missing-data assumptions. No arm-specific 12-month retention figures, reasons for missing peer-aggression data, or sensitivity analyses addressing missing-not-at-random outcome data are reported in the supplied materials. Because loss to follow-up in a study of self-reported violent behaviour could plausibly relate to the underlying outcome, there are some concerns, though insufficient evidence to conclude that missingness was likely dependent on the true outcome value.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias because peer aggression was a self-reported, subjective behavioural outcome assessed by participants who were aware of their assigned intervention.  
   b. **Rationale:** Severe peer aggression was measured by self-report using conflict-tactic-scale items: “Items from the conflict tactic scale assessed past-year severe aggression toward peers,” including having “hit or punched,” serious physical fighting, or weapon use. The 12-month follow-up was obtained via a “self-administered computer survey.” Therefore, the participant was the outcome assessor.  

   Although the trial registry identifies single masking of the “OUTCOMES_ASSESSOR,” this does not blind the participant for a participant-reported outcome. Participants receiving a motivational, therapist-assisted intervention directed specifically at violence and alcohol use would know their assignment, whereas control participants received a community-resource brochure. Knowledge of receiving an intervention intended to reduce aggression is likely to affect reporting through expectations, motivation to demonstrate change, or social-desirability effects. The article itself acknowledges self-report as a limitation, although it notes that computerized confidential surveys can improve reliability and validity. These features support data quality but do not remove the substantial potential for differential self-reporting by intervention group.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because the registration record prospectively identifies violent behaviours and a 12-month assessment, but does not provide a sufficiently detailed, time-stamped analysis plan to verify the specific peer-aggression definition and analysis that generated the reported result.  
   b. **Rationale:** The trial was registered before recruitment (first posted November 2005; recruitment began September 2006). The registry specifies primary outcomes broadly as “alcohol misuse and violent behaviors” at “12-months,” and states that outcomes would be evaluated at 3, 6, and 12 months. This supports prospective intention to evaluate violence outcomes at 12 months. The publication also states an a priori hypothesis that the brief interventions would decrease peer violence relative to control.  

   However, the registry does not pre-specify the particular severe-peer-aggression binary outcome, its instrument/scoring threshold, the precise contrast, or the GEE model and covariate structure. Peer violence was measured in multiple ways—peer aggression, peer victimization, and violence consequences—and the article reports several time points and analytic possibilities. No detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available was provided. There is no direct evidence that the reported peer-aggression result was selected because of its findings, but the absence of a sufficiently specific pre-specified analysis plan prevents a low-risk judgement.
