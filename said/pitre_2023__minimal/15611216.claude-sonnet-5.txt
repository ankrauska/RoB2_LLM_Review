# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0 Tool
## Outcome: Agitation Score (Table 4)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise because although a random component was used in sequence generation, there is no information about allocation concealment, and there was a statistically significant baseline imbalance in a key prognostic variable (arousal imbalance) that is directly relevant to the outcome.

**b. Rationale:** 
- Sequence generation: "We randomly assigned participants to study groups in time blocks of 1 week... We also randomized time blocks as treatment or control condition weeks." This supports a "Yes" to randomness (signalling question 1.1).
- Allocation concealment: No information is provided about how allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants. This warrants "No information" for signalling question 1.2.
- Baseline imbalance: The article explicitly states: "There was a statistically significant difference between the treatment and control groups in time spent in arousal imbalance at pretest (t = 2.57, p < .01), with the experimental group averaging 7.16 hr (SD = 2.5) and the control group averaging 5.64 hr (SD = 2.7)." Table 1 confirms this (p = .012). This is a key prognostic factor directly related to the outcome of agitation (the paper itself notes "respondents had significantly more agitation during periods of arousal imbalance... than during periods of arousal balance"). Although the authors appropriately controlled for this in the ANCOVA, this baseline imbalance—despite randomization—raises concern about whether randomization was fully successful in balancing groups on a variable highly relevant to the outcome.
- Per the algorithm: Random sequence (Y/PY) + No information on concealment (NI) + Baseline imbalance suggestive of a problem (Y/PY) → maps to "Some concerns" (per Table 4 in the guidance: "Any response / NI / Y/PY → Some concerns" for weaker conditions, though it borders "High" given the p<.01 imbalance). Because the trialists appropriately used statistical adjustment for this imbalance and it is plausible this arose from chance given a modest sample size (n=78) rather than subversion of the randomization process, "Some concerns" rather than "High" is the appropriate judgement.

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns are raised due to lack of blinding of personnel/carers implementing the intervention (which is unavoidable given the nature of the intervention), combined with an appropriate ITT-style analysis that nonetheless excluded some randomized participants for reasons that could relate to trial context.

**b. Rationale:**
- Participants and outcome assessors (data collectors) were blinded: "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions." This addresses signalling question 2.1 (participants aware) — likely "No" or "Probably no," since severely demented participants (median MMSE=3) would be unaware of intervention allocation in any meaningful sense, and data collectors were blinded as well.
- However, carers/personnel delivering the intervention (nursing staff, GNP) could not be blinded, since they had to implement the new activity schedule: "the nursing staff implemented the new activity schedule." This is "Yes" for signalling question 2.2.
- Deviations arising from trial context: The nursing staff were aware of assignment and had discretion in implementing the new schedule. The paper states dosing varied: "Participants were prescribed between one and four changes in activity schedule... The full dose of the prescribed activity schedule change was given to 72% of the sample, whereas 22% received 50–75%... and 6% received less than 50%." This is a deviation from intended intervention, but it appears to arise from practical/staffing constraints ("being short-staffed, being busy, or having more emergent needs") rather than from the trial context (e.g., participant awareness or investigator lack of equipoise) per se. This is more relevant to non-adherence.
- Analysis: The published analysis excluded some randomized participants: "Twelve died, 10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention, and 2 were excluded for erroneous or missing data." Excluding participants who received zero dose of intervention deviates from strict ITT principles, is inconsistent with intention-to-treat analysis for the effect of assignment to intervention, but the exclusion criteria (death, erroneous data) were largely unrelated to intervention success/failure rather than selection based on outcome. Still, dropping 10 people specifically because "not being given the intervention" is a deviation from ITT principles (a naive per-protocol-like exclusion), raising concern about the assignment effect estimate.
- Given: personnel not blinded, dose variability across participants (only 72% received full dose), and post-randomization exclusion of 10 non-treated participants from the primary analysis, this is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low," but likely not "High" since deviations do not appear systematically biased in favor of one specific direction, and it is unclear that the trial context (rather than practical logistics) drove non-implementation.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Missing outcome data are present (12 died, 2 excluded for erroneous/missing data, 10 dropped for not receiving intervention), and although reasons for exclusion are provided, they are not entirely unrelated to prognosis, raising some concern, though not clear evidence of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial reports: "During the time period allotted for this study, 102 people participated in the study. Twelve died, 10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention, and 2 were excluded for erroneous or missing data. The final sample was 78, with 36 receiving the experimental intervention and 42 in the control group."
- This represents roughly 24 of 102 (~24%) not included in the final analysis with the reported outcome — a non-trivial proportion.
- Reasons for missingness: Death (12) is plausibly unrelated to the true agitation outcome value in a way that would bias results, though death is itself sometimes associated with health decline that could correlate with agitation severity. The "erroneous or missing data" (2) is likely unrelated to true outcome value. However, the 10 dropped "for not being given the intervention" is more concerning as this exclusion is post-randomization and could be related to unmeasured prognostic or logistic factors — though this specifically pertains to the "deviations from intended intervention" domain as well, its consequence is missing outcome data for these individuals in this analysis.
- There's no explicit statement of a formal sensitivity analysis testing robustness to missing data assumptions.
- It is not clear whether missingness differed proportionally between experimental and control arms — the article does not explicitly break down the 12 deaths, 10 dropped, and 2 excluded by group assignment.
- Given the moderate amount of missing data (~24%) without clear evidence that missingness was unrelated to the outcome, and without sensitivity analyses, but with plausible, largely non-outcome-related explanations (death, data errors) for most exclusions, this is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "High."

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome was measured using an appropriate, validated method by blinded, well-trained assessors, and there is no indication that measurement differed systematically between groups or that lack of blinding influenced assessment (since assessors were, in fact, blinded).

**b. Rationale:**
- Appropriateness of measurement: Agitation was measured via direct observation using a "visual analog scale from 0 to 100," anchored using detailed "Wisconsin Agitation Intensity Parameters" (Table 3) with clearly defined criteria linking duration/intensity/number of behaviors to scores. This is an appropriate, operationalized method, not an inappropriate or insensitive measure.
- Comparability of measurement across groups: The same 15-minute observation protocol was used for both experimental and control participants over a 12-hour period from 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m., with the same exclusion criteria for atypical days (bath days, doctor visits, weekends) applied to both groups: "We did not collect data on days in which potentially negative or uncommon events occurred, such as on bath days, monthly doctor visit days, weekends... or days in which a new or noxious exam or treatment was scheduled." This indicates comparable ascertainment methods across groups.
- Blinding of outcome assessors: Explicitly stated: "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions." This directly addresses signalling question 4.3 ("No" — outcome assessors were not aware of intervention assignment).
- Reliability: Rigorous training and interrater reliability checks were conducted: "Training continued until interrater reliability was .85. Following training sessions, interrater reliabilities were between .88 and 1.0 for arousal and ranged from .95 to .984 for agitation," and reliability was rechecked every 2–3 months, with retraining if it dropped below .80.
- Given appropriate measurement method, comparable ascertainment, and blinded, well-trained/reliable outcome assessors, this domain is judged at low risk of bias.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** There is no accessible trial registration or protocol confirming a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available; while the primary agitation outcome and analysis (repeated measures ANCOVA controlling for arousal imbalance) appear to align with the stated theoretical framework and hypothesis, the absence of a verifiable pre-registered protocol prevents confirmation that outcome measurements/analyses were not selected based on results.

**b. Rationale:**
- No trial registry information is provided in the article itself, and the task materials note a separate trial registration document (doc_id: 4dab5cab-2b44-42f8-8559-2dd9073b18b5) was to be consulted; however, no specific registry details for this BACE trial are quoted or evident in the provided article text to confirm a pre-specified plan predating unblinded data.
- The agitation outcome measure itself changed during the study: "Our initial plan called for us to assess agitation by using the Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument (ABMI)... We were, however, unable to achieve sufficiently consistent interrater reliability estimates during training... We then trained RAs to assess agitation by using a visual analog scale from 0 to 100." This is a legitimate, well-justified, and clearly reported change made during training (before outcome data collection/unblinding), which reduces (but does not eliminate) concern, since it was driven by measurement reliability rather than by the results themselves.
- Multiple additional analyses are reported (stratified analyses, three separate ANCOVA models with different interaction variables, dose-response regression), but these appear to be reported comprehensively regardless of significance (e.g., the non-significant dose analysis and non-significant interaction models are all reported), which is reassuring and suggests less likelihood of selective reporting of analyses.
- However, without access to a date-stamped, pre-specified protocol or statistical analysis plan finalized before outcome data were unblinded, it cannot be confirmed with confidence that the specific reported result (the repeated measures ANCOVA with arousal imbalance and MMSE as covariates) was not selected, in some part, from among multiple possible covariate/model specifications based on the results obtained.
- Given the absence of clear documentation of a pre-specified plan (signalling question 5.1 = "No information"), but no strong positive evidence of selection based on results (signalling questions 5.2/5.3 also lean toward "No information" rather than clear "Yes"), the judgement defaults to "Some concerns" per the RoB2 algorithm (No information on pre-specification combined with no clear evidence of outcome/analysis selection maps to "Some concerns").
