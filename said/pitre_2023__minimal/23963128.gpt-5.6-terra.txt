1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the report states that residents were randomized but does not describe either generation of the allocation sequence or concealment of allocation before enrolment/assignment. Baseline characteristics do not indicate a clear failure of randomization.

   b. **Rationale:** The article states only that “**Residents were randomized to one of three levels of supervision**,” with groups of 18 (direct), 16 (immediately available), and 19 (distant). It does not report a random component used to generate the sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers) or how allocation was concealed from those recruiting/assigning residents. Under RoB 2, absence of information on allocation concealment leads to *some concerns* when baseline differences do not themselves suggest a problem.

   Table 1 shows generally comparable baseline characteristics across groups, including postgraduate training level, sex, ICU experience, and confidence score. Differences in age and prior simulation experience are plausible chance imbalances in this small trial and do not provide evidence that the randomization process was compromised.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to supervision level at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants, fellows, and scenario staff could not realistically be blinded to supervision level because the intervention was defined by the supervisor’s physical proximity: “**direct**” supervision involved the supervisor being in the room from the beginning; “**immediately available**” supervision involved availability by phone and attendance within 1 minute if needed; and “**distant**” supervision involved phone availability only.

   However, awareness of allocation does not itself establish bias for the assignment effect. The observed differences in fellow involvement appear to be part of the intended supervision strategies rather than deviations caused by the trial context. For example, in the immediately-available group, some residents did not call the supervisor, some received telephone advice only, and others received bedside assistance. This is consistent with an intervention strategy in which the supervisor is available if needed.

   The report indicates that all 53 randomized residents were included in the group analyses: direct *n* = 18, immediately available *n* = 16, and distant *n* = 19. The team checklist-score analysis used supervision level as the independent variable, consistent with analysing residents according to their assigned supervision strategy. There is no indication of an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis, or of post-randomization exclusion of eligible participants.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data for the team checklist score appear available for all randomized residents. The group sizes in the supervised-scenario outcome table—18 direct, 16 immediately available, and 19 distant—sum to the 53 residents reported as recruited.

   The article reports team checklist scores for each randomized group and gives no indication that any resident lacked checklist data, withdrew, or was excluded from the analysis. Thus, any missing data, if present, were not of a magnitude likely to materially affect the result.

4. **measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The team checklist score was based on whether clinical tasks were completed by any team member: “**A checklist task was considered done if performed by any member of the team (resident, fellow, and nurse)**.” This is a reasonably direct measure of care processes, and the same general checklist-based approach appears to have been used across groups.

   However, outcome assessment was based on observation of videotaped simulation performance, and the report does not state that checklist assessors were blinded to supervision assignment. Blinding would be difficult because direct, immediately available, and distant supervision would be apparent from the video recordings. Although the report states that two independent raters reviewed videotaped performance and that discrepancies in checklist scores were resolved by a third assessor, it does not state that these assessors were independent of the study hypothesis or blinded to supervision level.

   Determining whether a clinical task was completed can involve some judgement. Therefore, assessors’ knowledge of the visibly different supervision conditions could potentially influence scoring. The report does not provide evidence that such influence was likely, so *some concerns*, rather than high risk of bias, is appropriate. The authors also acknowledge that the scenario-specific checklists were nonvalidated: “**The checklist development process ensured a high face and content validity, but we did not assess its construct validity.**” This further limits confidence in the measurement, although it does not by itself demonstrate an inappropriate outcome-measurement method.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication reports the team checklist score, as well as other supervised-scenario outcomes (duration of abnormal vital signs and definitive management), and presents the three supervision groups. This comprehensive presentation provides no direct evidence that the reported team checklist result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable.

   However, the available material does not provide a pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The supplied text identifies a trial-registration document as a possible source, but its substantive registry fields, pre-specified outcome definitions, time points, and analysis plan are not reproduced here. Consequently, it cannot be verified whether the reported team checklist score, its percentage metric, or the reported analysis was pre-specified, nor whether other eligible checklist measures, scenario-specific results, time points, or alternative analyses were undertaken but not fully reported.

   Under RoB 2, when there is no adequate pre-specified analysis plan and insufficient information to exclude selection among multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses, the appropriate judgement is *some concerns*.
