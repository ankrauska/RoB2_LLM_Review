1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** The allocation process was likely random and adequately concealed, with no baseline imbalances suggesting a failure of randomization.

   b. **Rationale:** The investigators report that “the dementia family care coordinator had prepared 100 sealed nontransparent envelopes, of which 53 contained allocation to the intervention group and 47 to the control group,” and allocation occurred by the patient or caregiver “drawing one envelope.” Drawing from sealed, nontransparent envelopes provides a random component and likely prevented foreknowledge of the allocated group at enrolment. Although details such as sequential numbering, tamper-proof sealing, and the method used to arrange allocations within envelopes are not reported, the participant/caregiver-draw procedure makes manipulation of a forthcoming allocation unlikely.

   Baseline characteristics were reasonably comparable. There were somewhat more patients with severe dementia in the intervention group (36% versus 24%), but the overall dementia-severity distribution was not statistically distinguishable between groups (*P* = .244), and this difference is compatible with chance in a 100-participant trial. Other patient and caregiver characteristics were also broadly balanced.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** The effect of assignment to the intervention at baseline was estimated using an appropriate intention-to-treat–consistent analysis, and there is no evidence of outcome-relevant deviations caused by the trial context.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and caregivers necessarily knew their assigned care strategy: the intervention was an intensive, visible programme involving a dementia family care coordinator, counselling, calls, home visits, annual training, service coordination, and telephone availability. The coordinator and relevant care providers were also aware of assignment.

   However, the differences in care between groups were the intended intervention, rather than non-protocol deviations. The control group received usual municipal or private-sector care, whereas intervention participants received the specified support programme. No evidence is reported that the trial context caused unplanned, differential co-interventions or protocol failures that would have affected institutionalization.

   The analysis included all randomized participants: “There were no dropouts from the study during the 2-year follow-up.” Institutionalization was analysed according to randomized group using Kaplan–Meier methods and a time-dependent Cox regression model. There is no indication of as-treated, per-protocol, or post-randomization exclusion analyses. This is appropriate for the effect of assignment to intervention.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Outcome information was available throughout follow-up for all randomized participants, with no loss to follow-up.

   b. **Rationale:** The article explicitly states: “There were no dropouts from the study during the 2-year follow-up.” Institutionalization and deaths at home were ascertained from the Social Insurance Institution register. At two years, all participants were accounted for as remaining at home, institutionalized, or deceased at home.

   Participants who died before institutionalization were censored in the time-to-event analysis: “In the survival analyses, times were censored at death if the patient died in community care before institutionalization.” Death is a competing event and can potentially affect interpretation of a time-to-institutionalization analysis. However, death at home was equally frequent in both groups at two years (9/53 [17%] intervention versus 8/47 [17%] control), there were no losses to follow-up, and there is no evidence that censoring differed materially between groups or was handled selectively. Therefore, there is no material concern about bias from missing outcome data.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Institutionalization was measured using comparable and reliable methods in both groups, and the decision-makers were blinded to allocation.

   b. **Rationale:** The main outcome was time from enrolment to placement in long-term institutional care. Institutionalization and death were identified through the Social Insurance Institution register, an objective and consistently available source for both groups.

   Importantly, the article states that permanent institutionalization was decided by a local expert group—usually “a chief physician, head nurse, and social worker”—and that these groups “were blind to the allocation and generally unaware that a patient was even participating in the study.” Although institutionalization decisions involve clinical and social judgement, blinding of the relevant decision-makers substantially reduces the likelihood that assignment to the support programme influenced outcome ascertainment or the decision to institutionalize. There is also no indication that institutionalization was assessed differently between groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** There are some concerns because no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan or sufficiently detailed pre-specified outcome-analysis plan is available for comparison with the published analysis.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was conducted between October 1993 and January 1995, before prospective trial registration became routine. The supplied publication identifies time to institutionalization as “the main outcome measure” and reports Kaplan–Meier estimates and a time-dependent Cox model because proportional hazards were not met. The reported institutionalization outcome is consistent with the stated objective of determining whether community care could be prolonged.

   Nevertheless, the available materials do not provide a protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial-registration record specifying in advance the exact time points, survival-analysis approach, handling of deaths, covariate strategy, or subgroup analyses. Several potentially eligible analyses were possible, including analyses at 6, 12, and 24 months; conventional versus time-dependent survival models; and analyses by dementia severity. The article reports subgroup findings descriptively, particularly for severe dementia, but it is not possible to establish whether these analyses were pre-specified.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable. Therefore, the judgement is **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.
