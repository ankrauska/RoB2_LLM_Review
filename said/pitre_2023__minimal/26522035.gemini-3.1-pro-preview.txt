Based on the RoB 2.0 guidance, here is the assessment of the risk of bias for the trial regarding the outcome of patient acceptance of the appliance:

1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias
       a. Judgement: Provide rational for your judgement.
       The trial used an adequate random sequence generation method and successfully concealed the allocation sequence. Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups, indicating no apparent issues with the randomization process.
       b. Rationale: The authors state, "Computer random sequence generation was done using block sizes of 6 and 4. Allocation concealment was achieved with sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes." Additionally, Table I shows that baseline data were similar between the two groups.

2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias
       a. Judgement: Provide rational for your judgement.
       Although participants and personnel were not blinded to the intervention assignment, there is no evidence that this lack of blinding led to deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context. Furthermore, all participants were analyzed in the groups to which they were randomized (an intention-to-treat approach).
       b. Rationale: The text notes, "Blinding of the clinicians and the patients to the intervention was impossible". However, no deviations are reported, and the authors state, "The primary analysis was planned to be an intention-to-treat analysis to involve all patients who were to be randomly assigned; however, since there were no dropouts in the study, all the patients were included in the analysis."

3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias
       a. Judgement: Provide rational for your judgement.
       There is no risk of bias due to missing outcome data because outcome data were available for all randomized participants.
       b. Rationale: The trial flowchart (Figure 4) and text confirm complete follow-up: "The 32 surveys were retrieved from the patients; there were no dropouts."

4. measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias
       a. Judgement: Provide rational for your judgement.
       The outcome of patient acceptance is a participant-reported outcome. Because participants were aware of their assigned intervention (presence or absence of surgically inserted mini-implants), they acted as unblinded outcome assessors. Given the subjective nature of the questionnaire assessing pain, comfort, and acceptance, the assessment was highly likely to be influenced by their knowledge of the intervention received.
       b. Rationale: The study states, "Blinding of the clinicians and the patients to the intervention was impossible". The RoB 2.0 guidelines stipulate that for participant-reported outcomes without blinding, if the assessment is likely to be influenced by knowledge of the intervention (which is highly plausible when comparing an intervention with mini-implants to one without), the risk of bias is high.

5. selective reporting: some concerns
       a. Judgement: Provide rational for your judgement.
       The trial was not registered and no protocol was published beforehand, meaning there is no pre-specified statistical analysis plan to verify against the final reported outcomes. While there is no obvious evidence that the reported questionnaire results were selected based on statistical significance, the lack of a finalized, pre-specified plan raises some concerns.
       b. Rationale: The authors explicitly state, "Registration: This trial was not registered. Protocol: The protocol was not published before trial commencement." According to the RoB 2.0 tool, when data are not analyzed in accordance with a pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded data were available, the judgement defaults to at least "some concerns."
