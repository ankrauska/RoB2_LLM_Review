1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the random sequence was computer-generated and baseline characteristics of analysed patients were comparable, but allocation concealment was described only generally as use of “sealed envelopes.” The report does not confirm that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, and opened only after irrevocable enrolment.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The authors report that eligible patients were “randomly assigned (**computer-generated sequence**) in a 1:1 ratio” and that “randomization was not stratified.” This supports a random allocation sequence.  
   - They also state that “allocation was concealed (using sealed envelopes).” However, RoB 2 requires sufficient assurance that envelope allocation could not be predicted or manipulated. No details are provided on opacity, sequential numbering, or safeguards against opening/manipulation.  
   - The analysed groups appeared similar at baseline (e.g., age, sex, BMI, spinal region, baseline anxiety and Claustrophobia Questionnaire score; all reported P values ≥0.1), with no evident imbalance suggesting a failure of randomization.  
   - Thus, there is no evidence that randomization failed, but concealment cannot be judged adequately from the information reported.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of *assignment* to open versus short-bore MRI at baseline, because the image-quality analysis was effectively based on the scanner on which imaging was ultimately performed rather than on the randomized allocation. Cross-referral between scanners was frequent and could materially affect the comparison.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - Participants could not be blinded: “Patients could not be blinded to the assigned study group due to the MR imaging setting.” Staff delivering the intervention would also necessarily have known which scanner was used.  
   - The study protocol allowed cross-referral: “If patients did not complete imaging in their assigned MR scanner due to claustrophobia, they were cross-referred to imaging in the other scanner.”  
   - Crucially, the authors state: “**Examinations performed after cross-referral, with or without sedation, were included in the primary analysis**.” They report that “25 examinations were performed after cross-referral, 16 of them with conscious sedation.”  
   - Consequently, the reported comparison of 48 short-bore versus 55 open-scanner examinations does not preserve the original random allocation groups. Participants who could not tolerate their allocated scanner could contribute image-quality data from the other scanner, and sedation was used for some examinations.  
   - For the requested effect of assignment at baseline, a suitable analysis would retain participants in their randomized groups regardless of cross-referral or actual scanner received. The analysis presented is therefore inappropriate for this estimand, and the extent of cross-referral is large enough to have a substantial impact on image-quality results.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because image-quality data were unavailable for a non-negligible proportion of patients with an indication for spinal imaging, and the reasons for missing imaging could plausibly be related to factors affecting image quality; however, there is insufficient information to conclude that this dependence was likely.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - Of 106 patients with a clinical indication for spinal MRI, “93 underwent MR imaging, and all were included in this analysis.” Thus, image-quality outcomes were unavailable for 13/106 patients (about 12%) in the relevant spinal-imaging subgroup.  
   - The principal reason for unavailable imaging was claustrophobia: “All but one patient with a claustrophobic event rejected MR imaging before the examination started.” One patient “aborted MR imaging on the short-bore MR scanner after acquisition of one sequence, which could thus not be included in the analysis.”  
   - No analysis addressing the potential impact of missing image-quality outcomes was reported, and arm-specific outcome availability among all randomized spinal-imaging candidates is not fully presented.  
   - Claustrophobia, inability to remain in the scanner, and scan interruption may be associated with motion artefacts and other aspects of qualitative image quality. Therefore, missingness could be related to the unobserved outcome.  
   - Nevertheless, the report does not demonstrate that missingness differed materially by randomized group or that the missing image-quality values were likely systematically different enough to establish high risk of bias.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because the image-quality outcomes were measured using prespecified and appropriate qualitative and quantitative methods, and the assessors were blinded to imaging technique and patient identity.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - Image quality was assessed using several suitable technical measures: qualitative ratings of overall image quality, contrast, contour sharpness, noise and artefacts; quantitative signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR); and quantitative contour sharpness.  
   - The qualitative analysis was performed “by two examiners in consensus,” while quantitative analysis was performed by one examiner using standardized regions of interest and described formulae.  
   - Importantly, “**At the time of the analysis the examiners were blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity**.” This reduces the risk that knowledge of scanner assignment influenced subjective image-quality ratings or region-of-interest placement.  
   - Image acquisition and rating procedures were applied to both groups, with the authors reporting that sequence parameters were intended to provide comparable voxel sizes and scan times.  
   - Although scanner-specific image characteristics might theoretically allow partial inference of scanner type, there is no reported evidence that blinding was broken or that outcome ascertainment differed systematically between groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the trial registry did not pre-specify image-quality parameters or their analyses, and no pre-unblinding statistical analysis plan for this derived image-quality analysis is available. There is, however, no direct evidence that reported results were selected on the basis of their findings.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT00715806) identifies the primary outcome as “the ability of an open MRI scanner to reduce claustrophobic reactions that prevent MR examinations,” assessed “before or during MRI.” The listed secondary outcome was “impact of MR imaging results on subsequent measurement,” at 6 months.  
   - The registry does not specify image-quality outcomes, qualitative rating scales, SNR, CNR, contour sharpness, particular spinal sequences, time points, or planned statistical analyses for the present comparison.  
   - The publication describes the spinal image-quality analysis as involving a subset of the larger CLAUSTRO cohort: “The patients included in the analysis were part of a larger cohort.”  
   - The detailed design/protocol publication cited by the authors was published in 2011, after recruitment and completion (June 2008–August 2009); therefore, the supplied information does not establish an image-quality analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.  
   - The article reports a broad set of qualitative and quantitative image-quality findings across the main sequence types, which reduces—but does not eliminate—concern that only favourable results were presented. There is no clear evidence of outcome- or analysis-selection based on statistical significance; hence, some concerns rather than high risk of bias.
