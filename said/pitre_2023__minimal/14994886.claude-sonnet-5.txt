# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment: "Perception of Pain During Orthodontic Treatment with Fixed Appliances" — Outcome: Initial Pain

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The article states: "Insertion of either a 0.014 or 0.016 inch wire was by random selection" and "The patients were randomly selected for insertion of the 0.014 or 0.016 inch wires." No further detail is provided about the method of sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number tables) or how allocation concealment was achieved prior to and during enrolment. There is no mention of a randomization protocol, allocation concealment mechanism (e.g., sealed envelopes, central randomization), or who performed the randomization. Given this lack of detail:
- Signalling question 1.1 (Was the allocation sequence random?): "No information" — only a bare statement of "randomly selected" is provided, with no elaboration on method.
- Signalling question 1.2 (Was the allocation sequence concealed?): "No information" — nothing is reported about concealment.
- Signalling question 1.3 (Baseline imbalance suggesting a problem?): Baseline group sizes were reasonably similar (56 vs 53 patients; 29 vs 23 boys), and mean ages are not reported separately by wire group, so no evidence of imbalance is apparent, but this cannot be fully assessed due to lack of reported baseline comparisons between wire groups specifically.

Because there is no information for both randomization and allocation concealment (with no evidence of a problem otherwise), this maps to "Some concerns" per Table 4 (NI/NI/N-PN-NI → Some concerns).

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** This trial compares two active interventions (0.014 vs 0.016 inch wires) rather than a placebo-controlled or sham-controlled design, and blinding of participants or personnel to wire size is largely irrelevant to the *initial pain* outcome because:
- The intervention (wire size) is a physical device inserted once at baseline; there is no plausible mechanism by which trial-context factors (e.g., recruitment/consent processes) would differentially affect participants' *initial* pain perception based on knowledge of wire size, since patients are unlikely to have strong preconceptions about differences between 0.014 and 0.016 inch NiTi wires.
- There is no description of non-protocol interventions, co-interventions, or failures in delivering the assigned wire. The wires were "ligated to all teeth" in both groups as intended, and there is no reported deviation from the assigned archwire.
- Signalling questions 2.1/2.2 (awareness of assignment): Likely "No information," but even if participants/personnel were aware, there is no indication that this awareness led to deviations from intended intervention arising from the trial context that would affect the initial pain outcome.
- Analysis: All 109 randomized patients appear to have completed and returned questionnaires ("The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned"), consistent with an intention-to-treat approach for this outcome, with no exclusions reported affecting the initial-pain result.

Given the nature of the outcome (early, objective/self-reported time-to-pain-onset unlikely to be manipulated by trial context) and no evidence of deviations, this domain is judged as low risk.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The article explicitly states: "The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned at the following appointment." This indicates outcome data (including the initial pain question) were available for all randomized participants (56 in the 0.014 inch group and 53 in the 0.016 inch group), matching the total enrolled sample. Table 1 percentages for initial pain (e.g., 7.1%, 32.1%, etc.) appear to sum to the full sample size in each wire group without evident dropout.

- Signalling question 3.1 (Data available for all/nearly all participants?): "Yes" — 100% follow-up reported for the questionnaire, including the initial pain item.

Since outcome data were available for all randomized participants, this domain is low risk, and no further signalling questions are required.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The outcome "initial pain" (time at which pain was first perceived after archwire insertion) is a **participant-reported outcome**, assessed via a retrospective questionnaire item ("Question 1 asked the time at which pain was first perceived after archwire insertion") with response options limited to specific hourly intervals (1st–6th hour, per Figure 1) and then longer periods (up to 7 days, per Table 1 categories that include "6 days" and "7 days" alongside hourly options), which is somewhat inconsistent in its granularity and relies on patient recall.

Analysis of signalling questions:
- 4.1 (Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?): Probably no — VAS/questionnaire-based self-report of pain onset is a reasonably standard, albeit crude (categorical/interval), approach, and the paper cites precedent for VAS use in pediatric/dental pain research (Huskisson, 1974; Seymour et al., 1985) supporting reliability of similar instruments. However, "initial pain" itself was captured only as a categorical time bracket rather than through the VAS, which may reduce precision.
- 4.2 (Could measurement have differed between intervention groups?): No — the same questionnaire and administration procedure was applied to both wire-size groups.
- 4.3 (Were outcome assessors aware of intervention received?): Yes — because this is a participant-reported outcome, participants (the "outcome assessors") were inherently aware of which wire size they received, since the study was not blinded (no mention of blinding of patients to wire size, and given wires are visually/physically distinguishable in principle, though 0.014 vs 0.016 differences may be subtle to patients).
- 4.4 (Could assessment have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?): Possibly — although patients are unlikely to have strong prior expectations about which wire size causes more pain (unlike a placebo vs. active drug comparison), some influence cannot be excluded, particularly given known nocebo/expectation effects in pain perception research.
- 4.5 (Is it likely that assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention?): Unlikely to be strongly influenced, since 0.014 vs 0.016 inch NiTi wires are not associated with strong patient beliefs about differential pain in general practice, and there is no placebo comparator to create strong expectation bias. This points to "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

Because the outcome is participant-reported and non-blinded, but there is insufficient reason to believe strong influence occurred, this domain is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low" or "High."

"No information."
- Signalling question 5.3 (Selected from multiple eligible analyses?): Only one analysis approach is presented (chi-squared/Fisher's exact tests comparing wire groups). "No information."

Given the absence of any accessible protocol or trial registry information to confirm whether the reported initial-pain analysis matches pre-specified intentions, and no information available to rule out selective reporting, this results in "Some concerns" per the decision algorithm (Table 14: NI on all three questions → Some concerns).
