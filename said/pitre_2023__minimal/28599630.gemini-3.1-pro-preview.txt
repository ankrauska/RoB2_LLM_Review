**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The trial used an adequate method for random sequence generation and allocation concealment, and baseline imbalances do not suggest a problem with the randomization process. 
b. Rationale: The authors state that "randomization was performed by a researcher not involved in the data collection using the participant identification (subject ID) after stratification for EPDS score and previous history of depression." Additionally, "Participants and all research assistants and technicians responsible for running both the cohort study and the RCT were blinded to the study allocation." While there was a difference in skin color at baseline, this is likely compatible with chance and does not strongly indicate a failure of randomization.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Participants and trial personnel were successfully blinded to the intervention assignment, mitigating the risk of deviations from the intended interventions due to the trial context.
b. Rationale: The trial was double-blind. The authors note, "The capsules were identical regarding color, shape, size and packing." Furthermore, "Participants and all research assistants and technicians responsible for running both the cohort study and the RCT were blinded to the study allocation." 

**3. missing outcome data: high risk of bias**
a. Judgement: There was a substantial amount of missing outcome data, and missingness could plausibly depend on the true value of the outcome.
b. Rationale: Out of 60 women randomized (32 to fish oil, 28 to control), only 32 completed the trial (nearly a 47% attrition rate). The reasons for dropout included transferring prenatal care, miscarriages, stillbirths, and general dropouts. In trials assessing depression, loss to follow-up is frequently related to the participants' mental health status (the true value of the outcome). Although mixed-effects models were used for the intention-to-treat analysis, these models rely on the "missing at random" assumption, which is difficult to guarantee with such high attrition.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The outcome was measured using an appropriate tool, and the assessors/participants were blinded to the intervention assignment.
b. Rationale: Depressive symptoms were measured using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), a validated and widely used tool. Since the trial was double-blind, the participants (who self-report the EPDS) were unaware of their intervention status, so their responses were not influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
a. Judgement: The trial was retrospectively registered and nested within an observational cohort, making it difficult to verify if the analysis plan was finalized prior to unblinding, though the reported outcomes generally align with typical measures for this field. 
b. Rationale: The trial registration provided (NCT01660165) characterizes the study as an observational cohort rather than a randomized trial, and the manuscript notes it was "Retrospectively registered on 24 May 2012." There is no detailed, prospectively registered statistical analysis plan available to confirm that the specific EPDS cut-offs, time points, and subgroups (e.g., women with a previous history of depression) were entirely pre-specified before data analysis. Therefore, there are some concerns regarding the selection of the reported result.
