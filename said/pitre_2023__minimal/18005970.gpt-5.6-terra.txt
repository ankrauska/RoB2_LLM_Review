**Outcome assessed:** number of mistakes in diagnostic hypotheses identified by students in Phases 1 and 2. The assessment below concerns the randomized Phase-2 within-faculty comparisons (Trento: intensive clinical tutoring versus routine tutoring; Udine: weekly tutorials versus routine tutoring) and the assignment effect (intention-to-treat effect). The Phase-1 comparison of all Trento students receiving laboratory teaching with all Udine students not receiving it is an inter-faculty, non-randomized comparison and cannot, by itself, be validly assessed as an individually randomized comparison using RoB 2.

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that students were randomized using a randomization table, which supports random sequence generation. However, it provides no information on allocation concealment—i.e., whether those recruiting/enrolling students could foresee the next assignment before allocation. Baseline characteristics were reported as not significantly different between trial and control groups within faculties, but this provides only limited reassurance and does not replace information on concealment.

   Relevant report text:
   - “In each campus, the students enrolled in the research project were consequently randomised with a randomisation table into two different groups.”
   - For Phase 2, “The two groups of students in both Faculties of Nursing … were randomly selected at each centre using a randomisation table.”
   - Table 2 footnote: “no significant difference between Trial and Control groups in each group, p>0.05.”

   RoB 2 signalling considerations:
   - **1.1 Random sequence:** *Probably yes*, because a randomization table is reported.
   - **1.2 Allocation concealment:** *No information*, because the report does not describe central allocation, sealed opaque envelopes, independent administration, or another concealment method.
   - **1.3 Baseline imbalance suggesting a problem:** *Probably no*, because no important within-faculty baseline imbalance is reported.

   Under RoB 2, a random sequence with no information about allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**, absent evidence that concealment was inadequate.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and tutors were necessarily likely to know which educational strategy was being delivered; however, lack of blinding alone is not sufficient for bias in this RoB 2 domain when assessing the assignment effect. Bias requires deviations that arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, affected the outcome, and were unbalanced between groups. The article reports no such deviations. It explicitly states that teaching practices did not change during the study. The analysed Phase-2 group sizes correspond to the randomized group sizes, indicating that students were analysed in their assigned groups.

   Relevant report text:
   - “During the study, none of the teaching practices changed in either of the degree courses.”
   - “The trial group in Trento had intensive clinical tutoring while the control group had routine tutoring; the group in Udine were further randomly divided into two different groups, one having weekly tutorials and the other routine tutoring.”
   - Phase-2 group sizes were reported as Trento intensive tutoring (n=41), Trento routine tutoring (n=41), Udine weekly tutorials (n=39), and Udine routine tutoring (n=23).

   Although educational interventions could not plausibly be blinded, there is no reported evidence that knowledge of allocation caused differential non-protocol tuition, contamination, or implementation failures attributable to trial participation. The comparisons appear to have retained students in their assigned intervention groups. Therefore, for the assignment effect, this domain is at **low risk of bias**.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data appear available for all 144 eligible students at the relevant Phase-1 and Phase-2 assessments. The reported denominators at Phase 2 total 144 and correspond to the enrolled sample: 41 + 41 + 39 + 23 = 144. No losses to follow-up, withdrawals, exclusions from the outcome analysis, or missing diagnostic-hypothesis assessments are reported.

   Relevant report text:
   - “From the 179 students enrolled … 144 subjects were eligible for the study.”
   - Table 3 reports Phase 1 results for Trento (n=82) and Udine (n=62), totaling 144.
   - Table 3 reports Phase-2 results for all four randomized groups, totaling 144.
   - “The group included was stable in the first six months…”

   Since the numerical outcome appears to have been measured and reported for all randomized students, and there is no indication that missingness could have influenced the result, the risk of bias due to missing outcome data is **low**.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was assessed using a common simulated clinical case, with modified contextual details across phases to retain comparable complexity. Diagnostic hypotheses were assessed against a predefined professional standard. Importantly, two researchers evaluated students’ responses while blinded to identities of the nursing schools; this substantially reduces the risk that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced classification of diagnostic hypotheses as mistaken.

   Relevant report text:
   - “Researchers decided to note mistaken diagnostic hypotheses made by the students using the same ‘case history’ (‘Valentina’s case’).”
   - “This case was modified with demographic, contextual and family data and used in all three phases of the study.”
   - “It was decided to keep the same case study to guarantee a homogenous level of complexity for all the students.”
   - “Two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students and these were compared to the ‘standard’.”
   - “A panel of 15 professional nurses independently defined the standard problems of the case history used.”

   The outcome measure is a direct count of diagnostic hypotheses considered incorrect against a predefined standard. The same basic assessment approach and time allowance were used across groups. While judgment is involved in deciding whether a hypothesis is erroneous, blinded outcome assessment and use of a professional consensus standard make differential measurement bias unlikely. Therefore, risk of bias in measurement is **low**.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies the number of mistaken diagnostic hypotheses in Phases 1 and 2 as the main outcome and reports results for that outcome. However, there is no accessible prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan or sufficiently detailed prospective protocol establishing the exact prespecified outcome metric, cut-points, comparison(s), analysis model, and handling of the multiple possible analyses.

   Relevant report text:
   - “The main outcome was the calculation of the number of mistakes made in diagnosing problems (diagnostic hypotheses) identified by the students in Phases 1 and 2.”
   - “The median values of error in Phases 1 and 2 were regarded as reference ‘cut-off’ points to estimate the differences within campuses and between the groups in each.”
   - The study reports several analyses, including intra-trial comparisons, inter-trial comparisons, univariate analyses, and multivariable logistic regression.

   The use of post hoc-looking median cut-offs (“≥Median” versus “<Median”), alongside several potential analyses and contrasts, creates scope for selection among analyses. The report does not demonstrate that these choices were fixed before outcome data were available. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because of its statistical significance or direction, so **high risk of bias** is not justified. Nevertheless, absence of a verifiable prespecified analysis plan leads to **some concerns**.
