1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Allocation used a valid random component and was centrally administered. The report states that patients were assigned 1:1 using “*a minimisation algorithm incorporating a random element*,” with randomisation conducted through the University of Leeds Clinical Trials Research Unit’s “*automated 24-hour telephone randomisation system*.” This provides adequate allocation concealment from recruiting clinicians until enrolment and assignment.  
  
   Baseline characteristics were closely balanced between groups, including the minimisation variables: age younger than 50 years (23% in each group), age ≥50 years (77% in each group), and breast-density categories (13% versus 13% for BI-RADS group 1). The authors also state that “*Characteristics were well balanced between the groups*.” There is no indication of a meaningful baseline imbalance or manipulation of allocation.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to MRI versus no MRI at baseline.  
   b. **Rationale:** This was an open-label imaging trial, so both participants and personnel were necessarily aware of allocation. However, for the requested estimand—the effect of assignment—lack of blinding is problematic only if it caused deviations from protocol due to the trial context that were likely to affect reoperation and were unbalanced between groups. There is no convincing evidence of such deviations.  
  
   Some non-receipt and crossover occurred: in the MRI group, 761/816 received MRI, while 9/807 participants allocated to no MRI underwent MRI. Most MRI non-receipt was attributable to practical or participant-related reasons, including MRI technical failures, MRI unavailability before surgery, refusal, non-attendance, unsuitable MRI status, or claustrophobia. These do not, by themselves, indicate trial-context deviations that would bias the effect of assignment.  
  
   Importantly, the primary analysis retained all randomized participants in their allocated groups: “*Analysis was by intention to treat*,” and the trial profile shows that all 816 MRI-assigned and all 807 no-MRI-assigned participants were analysed for the primary endpoint. Thus, the analysis was appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention. Although the outcome necessarily reflects subsequent clinical management decisions, this is part of the intended effect of preoperative MRI assignment rather than evidence of bias caused by non-adherence.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Primary-outcome data were available for nearly all randomized participants. The flow diagram indicates that 816 participants in the MRI group and 807 in the no-MRI group were included in the primary-endpoint analysis, i.e., all 1623 randomized participants.  
  
   A total of 10 participants had primary-endpoint data unavailable during follow-up (4 MRI and 6 no-MRI), and these participants were “*regarded as not having a primary endpoint event*.” This is a limitation, but the amount of missing data was extremely small (approximately 0.6% overall), balanced between groups, and far smaller than the number of observed primary events (309 reoperations). Consequently, even extreme outcomes among the few participants with unavailable data would be unlikely to materially change the estimated odds ratio (0.96; 95% CI 0.75–1.24).

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Reoperation rate was a clinical-management outcome that depended substantially on decisions by surgeons and multidisciplinary teams, who were aware of the assigned strategy. The trial was explicitly “*open-label*,” and the intervention itself—MRI—provided information that could change surgical decision-making.  
  
   The primary composite outcome included repeat wide local excision, further mastectomy, and “*a pathologically avoidable mastectomy at initial operation*.” The latter component was particularly susceptible to knowledge of MRI findings and clinician decision-making: MRI findings were reviewed by a multidisciplinary team and could lead to a change from planned wide local excision to mastectomy. The report confirms that MRI altered management: “*A change in clinical management that was attributable to MRI findings was proposed for 55 patients (7%) in the MRI group*,” and 16 MRI-group participants underwent a pathologically avoidable initial mastectomy.  
  
   Although operations and pathology results are objectively documented, the occurrence of surgery is an intervention-provider decision outcome. In an unblinded trial, knowledge of MRI assignment and findings was likely to influence decisions to perform more extensive initial surgery or reoperation. Under RoB 2, this constitutes a high risk of bias in measurement/ascertainment for this outcome, particularly because the outcome incorporates a judgement-dependent classification of “pathologically avoidable” mastectomy.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The publication and registration identify reoperation as the primary clinical outcome, and the reported effect estimate corresponds to that outcome: 153/816 (19%) in the MRI group versus 156/807 (19%) in the no-MRI group; adjusted OR 0.96 (95% CI 0.75–1.24). The article also describes the intended analysis: “*We compared the reoperation rate between groups with use of logistic regression, adjusting for the minimisation factors*.” These features reduce concern that an entirely different outcome was selected for reporting.  
  
   Nevertheless, there is insufficient evidence that a detailed statistical analysis plan, including the precise outcome definition and analytic choices, was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. In particular, the composite outcome included “pathologically avoidable mastectomy,” with the definition specified by the independent DMEC and agreed by the Trial Steering Committee; the publication does not clearly establish the timing of finalization of this definition relative to access to outcome data. The publicly described registration supports the broad primary outcome but does not fully rule out selection among eligible definitions, time points, or analyses. Therefore, the result raises some concerns rather than meeting RoB 2 criteria for low risk of bias.
