# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment: Simvastatin as Adjuvant Therapy in RRMS (Outcome: Attack Number)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise because, while randomization appears to have used an adequate random sequence generation method, there is no information about allocation concealment, and analysis of baseline characteristics reveals an imbalance in a key prognostic factor (baseline Gd+ lesions) that is directly relevant to disease activity/relapse risk.

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: "A computer-based blocking method (four blocks) was used for the randomization" — this indicates a random component (Y to signalling question 1.1).
- Allocation concealment: The report states "Separate physicians were assigned to randomly allocate patients into two groups" and that "patients were unaware of their group assignment and all medications were given in similar packages." However, there is no explicit description of how the allocation sequence was concealed from the physicians performing the enrollment/allocation (e.g., no mention of sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes, or central randomization). This is answered as **No information** to signalling question 1.2.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows differences between groups compatible with chance for most variables (all p>0.05: sex p=0.62, age p=0.32, EDSS p=0.13). However, baseline Gd+ lesions differed notably (0.85 ± 1.59 placebo vs. 1.3 ± 2.0 simvastatin, p=0.28) and "No Gd+ lesion (%)" differed (55.3% vs 47.6%, p=0.51). While none reached statistical significance individually, this is a small trial (n=85) with limited power to detect imbalance, and Gd+ lesions are a strong prognostic factor for future relapse. The authors themselves note in the paper: "the baseline EDSS (p=0.0) and the number of baseline Gd-enhanced lesions (p=0.025) had a significant influence on the clinical and imaging outcomes" — indicating these baseline factors were prognostically important, even though the between-group difference itself was not formally significant.
- Given (i) no information on allocation concealment, AND (ii) baseline differences compatible with chance (per formal p-values) but with a plausible clinically important imbalance in a highly prognostic covariate, the algorithm supports "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The trial was double-blind (participants, care providers, and outcome assessors all blinded), reducing risk of bias due to trial-context-driven deviations. However, there were post-randomization exclusions/analytic choices (per-protocol and LOCF analyses reported alongside ITT) and unequal, non-trivial early dropout/switching between arms that raise some concern regarding whether the appropriate ITT analysis was fully and cleanly implemented.

**b. Rationale:**
- Blinding: "The patients were assigned (in random and double-blinded fashion)"; "patients were unaware of their group assignment and all medications were given in similar packages, every 3 months"; physicians assessing patients "were blinded to the patients' assignment to treatment group." This supports **No** to signalling questions 2.1 and 2.2 (participants and personnel unaware) → default would be "Low" for Part 1 of this domain.
- However, examining the analysis: "The analyses were performed according to intention-to-treat methods... In order to assess the dropouts, per protocol analysis and last observation carried forward (LOCF) methods were performed." Multiple analyses (ITT, per-protocol, LOCF) were presented, with the per-protocol analysis given prominence in the results ("A Mann-Whitney U-test revealed a significant difference (p=0.004) in total attack number between the placebo... and simvastatin... groups" using per-protocol population), raising concern that the primary reported result may not consistently reflect a clean ITT analysis.
- Fourteen patients withdrew (4 simvastatin, 6 placebo formally "excluded" for 2 relapses, and additional dropouts), and the numbers were somewhat imbalanced between groups (per Figure 1: 3 premature withdrawals in simvastatin vs 6 in placebo due to exacerbation), which — combined with unclear specification of how the "final" ITT dataset handled these withdrawals for the attack-number outcome — introduces some uncertainty as to whether the reported ITT-labelled result was fully appropriate and unaffected by deviations.
- No overt evidence that deviations arose specifically because of "trial context" (e.g., no reported non-protocol interventions or contamination); therefore this does not reach High risk of bias. Overall: **Some concerns**.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Outcome data (attack number) were not available for all randomized participants at the primary analysis point, differences in dropout existed between groups, and although the reasons are documented, it is plausible (though not clearly established) that missingness could relate to the true outcome (worsening disease/relapse).

**b. Rationale:**
- Of 85 randomized, only 40 (simvastatin) and 36 (placebo) contributed to the primary Mann-Whitney analysis of relapse count ("simvastatin (median=0, n=40) and placebo (median=1, n=36)"), i.e., ~89% total, meaning around 11% missing.
- Reasons for missing data are reported: "Five of these refused to cooperate without any specific reason," and "Of the nine patients who were excluded from the study later on, six had two relapses (two from the simvastatin and four from the placebo group)." Critically, exclusion due to "having two relapses" is a reason for missing data that is **directly related to the outcome being assessed** (attack number) — this is a strong signal that missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome.
- The number of patients withdrawing for relapse-related reasons differed between groups (2 simvastatin vs 4 placebo), suggesting differential missingness related to the outcome and group assignment.
- The authors attempted sensitivity analyses (per-protocol, LOCF) that showed consistent direction and significance of effect (p=0.004 and p=0.03 respectively, both favoring simvastatin), which does provide some reassurance that the finding is robust to different ways of handling the missing/dropout data. This mitigates concern about High risk, keeping the judgement at "Some concerns" rather than "High."

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome (relapse/attack number) was assessed using a pre-specified, objective clinical definition, and the assessors were blinded to intervention assignment; it is unlikely that knowledge of allocation influenced relapse ascertainment.

**b. Rationale:**
- Definition of relapse was pre-specified and objective: "any new or worsening neurological deficit in the absence of high body temperature that lasted for more than 24 h after 1-month stability was considered a relapse and was treated by IV methylprednisolone."
- Outcome assessors were blinded: "Four neurologists were asked to assess the patients' eligibility for the study and perform the follow-up examinations. They were blinded to the patients' assignment to treatment group."
- While relapse assessment does involve some clinical judgement (new/worsening neurological deficit), the use of a structured definition and confirmed blinding of assessors substantially reduces the risk that knowledge of allocation influenced this determination.
- Method of measurement was not inappropriate, and there is no indication that ascertainment of relapses differed systematically between groups (e.g., same follow-up schedule "every 3 months" for both groups).
- This supports: 4.1 No (method not inappropriate), 4.2 No (ascertainment didn't differ between groups), 4.3 No (assessors unaware) → **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The trial registry (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00668343) pre-specifies "attack number" as the (sole) primary outcome measured at "1 year," which matches the outcome and timing reported in the publication, supporting low risk for this specific outcome/analysis. However, lack of an available detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan (SAP) date-stamped prior to unblinding, together with the reporting of multiple alternative analyses (ITT, per-protocol, LOCF, MANOVA, analysis of covariance) for the same outcome without a clearly pre-specified hierarchy, raises some concern that the specific reported comparison/statistic may have been selected partly on the basis of results.

**b. Rationale:**
- Registry confirms: primary outcome "attack number," timeframe "1 year" — consistent with the published primary result ("The total attack number in the simvastatin group was significantly lower than placebo group... (p=0.01)").
- However, the methods section describes multiple analytic approaches applied to the same outcome (attack number): the main Mann-Whitney test (n=40 vs 36, p=0.01), per-protocol analysis (p=0.004), and LOCF analysis per 3-month period (p=0.03), plus MANOVA/ANCOVA. No pre-specified statistical analysis plan was available (none referenced in the article or registry) to confirm the hierarchy or primary designation of these analyses in advance.
- All reported results for attack number happen to be statistically significant and directionally consistent (favoring simvastatin); we cannot rule out that alternative analyses, had they been non-significant, might not have been foregrounded — but no clear evidence of selective suppression exists either.
- Given the outcome domain and timing itself match the registered primary outcome (reducing concern about outcome-switching), but the absence of a pre-specified analysis plan for handling multiple statistical approaches to the same outcome introduces uncertainty, this is judged as **Some concerns** rather than "Low risk."
