1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The sequence-generation method appears genuinely random: “268 three-digit random numbers from the table were written in the patient registers in advance,” with allocation by odd/even number. However, allocation concealment was not described. In particular, because the numbers were entered in patient registers in advance, it is unclear whether the personnel recruiting and enrolling patients could foresee the next allocation. There were no major baseline imbalances strongly suggesting that randomization had been subverted: age differed modestly (mean 36.4 vs 40.2 years; *P*=0.059), and disease duration was similar. Thus, the concern arises principally from insufficient information about allocation concealment, rather than evidence of a flawed random sequence or problematic baseline imbalance.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This was an open-label trial: “Blinding to treatment was not undertaken.” There is no direct evidence that deviations arose specifically because of the trial context, so lack of blinding alone does not necessarily imply bias for the assignment effect. However, the reported 2-year cure-rate analysis did not include all randomized participants in their assigned groups.

   The article reports that 107/134 PB-MDT participants and 115/134 ROM-6 participants completed treatment; 27 (20.1%) and 19 (14.2%), respectively, defaulted. The 2-year cure result was then based on only 100 PB-MDT and 103 ROM-6 participants followed at 2 years: “At the end of 2 years, 100 patients in the PB-MDT group and 103 in the ROM-6 group were followed up.” The reported cure rates—97% and 99%—therefore exclude substantial proportions of randomized participants, including treatment defaulters.

   This is not an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis of the effect of assignment at baseline. Excluding approximately one quarter of randomized participants from the 2-year analysis has clear potential for a substantial effect on the result, especially because default was related to factors such as drug intolerance, vomiting/diarrhoea, perceived cure, pregnancy, and loss to treatment.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Two-year cure-status data were not available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants. Only 100/134 (74.6%) PB-MDT participants and 103/134 (76.9%) ROM-6 participants were followed at 2 years. Thus, cure data were unavailable for 34 PB-MDT and 31 ROM-6 participants.

   The missing data are substantial, and no sensitivity analysis or appropriate method for addressing missing outcomes was reported. The authors acknowledge this limitation: “A number of patients were lost to follow up after release from treatment and thus actual number of relapses in the study could not be assessed.”

   Missingness could plausibly depend on the true cure status. Reasons for discontinuation included “weakness/intolerance,” “vomiting/diarrhea,” “felt cured/leprosy stigma,” pregnancy, and being lost to treatment. Some of these reasons are plausibly associated with treatment response, adverse effects, or disease activity. Moreover, default rates differed between groups (20.1% for PB-MDT versus 14.2% for ROM-6). These features make it likely that missingness was related to outcome and could have materially biased the reported 2-year cure rates.

4. **measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Cure was assessed clinically and included components requiring judgement: “complete resolution of the lesion or patch becoming flat, hypopigmented and with decrease in size of the lesion and/or regaining of sensation.” The trial was explicitly unblinded: “Blinding to treatment was not undertaken.” The outcome assessors—trained paramedical workers and study clinicians—could therefore have been aware of treatment allocation.

   The cure definition included relatively observable clinical features, and the same stated criteria appear to have been used in both groups. There is no explicit evidence that assessment methods differed between groups or that assessors deliberately assessed one group differently. Nevertheless, lesion activity, lesion size, flattening, and sensory recovery can involve clinical judgement, and knowledge of the assigned regimen could have influenced assessments. This creates some concerns, but the available information does not establish that such influence was likely sufficient to justify a high-risk judgement.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial registration cited in the article is retrospective: “This study is now registered on Clinical Trial Registry of India vide REF/2013/11/005995 dated 22 November, 2013.” Recruitment occurred from June 2001 to July 2004, follow-up was conducted through November 2010, and the registry entry was dated after completion of the study and after outcome data would have been available. Therefore, the registration cannot provide a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The publication reports cure outcomes at several time points—release from treatment, 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months, as well as later follow-up. Although the article reports the 2-year cure result, there is no prospectively finalized plan specifying the eligible time point, precise cure analysis, population to be analysed, or handling of missing outcomes. There is no direct evidence that the 2-year result was selected because it was favourable, so high risk is not warranted; however, the absence of a prospective analysis plan and the presence of multiple reported time points lead to some concerns.
