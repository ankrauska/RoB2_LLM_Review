Based on the provided RoB 2.0 guidance and the study report, here is the risk of bias assessment for the outcome Agitation Score (Table 4), considering the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline (intention-to-treat effect).

1. bias due to randomization: some concerns
a. Judgement: There are baseline imbalances that suggest potential issues, though the randomization process itself may have been adequate.
b. Rationale: The study states, "We randomly assigned participants to study groups in time blocks of 1 week". However, Table 1 shows that "The experimental group participants spent significantly more time in arousal imbalance at pretest" (M=107.4 vs M=84.6, p=0.012). Because arousal imbalance is a key prognostic factor strongly related to agitation (the outcome), this significant baseline imbalance raises some concerns about the randomization process.

2. deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias
a. Judgement: The intervention could not be blinded, deviations from the protocol occurred due to staffing/time issues (trial context), and analysis was not strictly intention-to-treat.
b. Rationale: Participants, carers, and staff delivering interventions could not be blinded due to the nature of the BACE intervention (altering daily activity schedules). The report states, "Nurses stated the intervention was not given in 10 cases because of being short-staffed, being busy, or having more emergent needs the day of the scheduled intervention" and "10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention". Because these 10 randomized participants were excluded from the analysis rather than analyzed in the groups to which they were randomized, an appropriate ITT analysis was not used. Given the small sample size (102 original, 78 final), excluding 10 participants for post-randomization reasons has the potential for a substantial impact on the result.

3. missing outcome data: high risk of bias
a. Judgement: Outcome data were not available for all randomized participants, and missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome.
b. Rationale: Out of 102 randomized participants, only 78 were included in the final analysis. Twelve died, 10 were dropped for not receiving the intervention, and 2 were excluded for missing data. The 10 participants dropped for not receiving the intervention were likely dropped because of factors related to the trial context (short-staffed, emergent needs), which could be related to the participants' clinical status or agitation levels. Therefore, missingness in the outcome could depend on its true value, and the proportion of missing data (24/102, ~24%) is large enough to potentially bias the results.

4. measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias
a. Judgement: Outcome assessors were not blinded, and the assessment involved some judgment.
b. Rationale: Agitation was measured via direct observation using a visual analog scale. The study states, "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions." However, because the intervention inherently alters the participants' daily activity schedule—which the data collectors were directly observing over 12-hour periods—true blinding of the observers is highly unlikely. Since assessing agitation (e.g., distinguishing purposeful vs nonpurposeful movement) involves judgment, the assessment of the outcome was likely influenced by knowledge of the intervention received. 

5. selective reporting: some concerns
a. Judgement: There is no reference to a pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial registry.
b. Rationale: The report does not mention a trial registry or a pre-published protocol. Without a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available, it is not possible to verify if the reported ANCOVA adjusting for arousal imbalance and MMSE was planned a priori or selected based on the data. Therefore, there are some concerns regarding the selection of the reported result.
