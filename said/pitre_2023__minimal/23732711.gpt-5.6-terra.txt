**Assessment target:** change in photoaging (microtopography grade from 1992 to 1996), for **daily sunscreen versus discretionary sunscreen use**, estimating the **effect of assignment** to intervention at baseline.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was adequately random: “one of our study investigators, who had no knowledge of the participants, randomly assigned them by using a computer-generated randomized list.” Baseline characteristics, including factors relevant to photoaging (age, sex, skin colour, sun-exposure history, smoking and prior sunscreen use), were broadly similar between sunscreen groups; no imbalance clearly suggests a failed randomization process.

   However, allocation concealment is not described. The report does not state that allocation was administered centrally, by an independent party, or through an adequately secured sequentially numbered system. The statement that the investigator generating/using the list “had no knowledge of the participants” provides some reassurance but does not establish that recruiters could not foresee allocations before enrolment. Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns when baseline balance does not itself indicate a problem.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew whether they had been assigned to apply sunscreen daily or to use sunscreen at their discretion; consequently, blinding of participants and those delivering the sunscreen intervention was not feasible. Nevertheless, for the effect of assignment, lack of adherence alone is not a bias unless deviations arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with protocol, and were likely to affect the outcome differentially between groups.

   The trial reports expected differences in actual sunscreen use—by 1996, “77% of daily sunscreen users were applying sunscreen at least 3 to 4 days per week compared with 33% of discretionary users.” This reflects incomplete adherence to the assigned strategy, rather than evidence that trial-context deviations or unbalanced non-protocol interventions undermined the randomized comparison. Other sun-protective behaviour was comparable: “54% of the daily sunscreen group and 53% of the discretionary sunscreen group usually sought shade,” while 63% and 67%, respectively, usually wore a hat.

   Importantly, analyses were conducted according to randomized allocation: “Data were analyzed according to treatment as randomly allocated.” The generalized estimating equation analysis included participants with data at either time point and did not use an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis. Thus, the analysis was appropriate for the effect of assignment.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants. Of 903 eligible participants, 886 had at least one good-quality replica, but only 604 contributed replicas at both baseline and follow-up. Specifically, 817 had a usable baseline replica and 673 had a 1996 replica; 213 contributed baseline data only and 69 follow-up data only. Thus, missingness was appreciable, especially for the follow-up measurement.

   The authors investigated missingness and reported that persons contributing only one replica “were more likely to have severe photoaging of the neck and 2 to 5 total sunburns.” These factors could be related to subsequent photoaging and therefore missingness could depend on the true outcome. Reassuringly, “having a missing microtopography grade in 1996 was not associated with baseline microtopography grade,” and treatment allocation was reportedly not associated with missing grades. The primary analysis was adjusted for factors associated with incomplete data (sunburns and neck photoaging), and complete-case and multiple-imputation analyses were said to be consistent with the primary result.

   However, the amount of missing data remains substantial, and the reported multiple-imputation and complete-case checks do not establish robustness under plausible missing-not-at-random assumptions. Therefore, there are some concerns rather than low risk of bias.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Photoaging was measured using a pre-specified, objective and appropriate method: silicone skin-surface replicas from the back of the left hand, graded with the Beagley and Gibson microtopography scale. The authors report that this measure had been validated against dermal elastosis and that intra- and inter-grader repeatability was high (weighted kappa statistics 0.81 and 0.86, respectively).

   Measurement was comparable between groups because the same replica-taking procedure, anatomical site, grading scale, and follow-up schedule were used. Crucially, “experienced assessors who were unaware of treatment allocation graded replicas.” Blinded assessment makes it unlikely that knowledge of sunscreen assignment influenced grading of this observer-assessed outcome. The authors also noted that measurement error “seemed to be nondifferential with respect to treatment groups, particularly for microtopography, because assessors were blinded to allocations.”

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication clearly identifies change in photoaging, measured by microtopography grades from 1992 to 1996, as the primary outcome for this analysis and reports the principal randomized comparison, including both minimally adjusted and adjusted models. It also reports sensitivity analyses using complete cases and multiple imputation, which is reassuring.

   However, the trial registration (ACTRN12610000086066) was registered long after the trial was conducted (the trial ran from 1992 to 1996). Therefore, the registration cannot demonstrate that a detailed outcome measurement and statistical analysis plan was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The cited 1994 design/baseline publication supports that the trial was prospectively designed, but the available information does not provide a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan to rule out selection among eligible outcome measurements (for example, alternative time points or photoaging measures) or analyses (for example, alternative covariate adjustment and handling of missing data).

   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was favourable, so high risk of bias is not justified. Nonetheless, absence of a prospectively finalized detailed analysis plan results in some concerns.
