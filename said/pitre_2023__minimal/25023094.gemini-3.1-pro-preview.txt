Based on the RoB 2.0 guidance, here is the risk of bias assessment for the trial for the outcome of pain measured by the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), considering the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline:

    1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias
           a. Judgement: The trial used a random sequence generation process and allocation was concealed. Baseline characteristics were balanced.
           b. Rationale: The authors state, "The distribution of subjects to the two treatment groups was performed using sex-stratified randomization, and the allocation sequence was blinded through the use of sealed envelopes." Furthermore, Table 1 shows no significant differences between the two groups at baseline, indicating the randomization process was successful.

    2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias
           a. Judgement: Although the personnel delivering the intervention were not blinded, the participants were blinded, and an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis was conducted.
           b. Rationale: The study was designed "with blinding of patients and blind assessor", using a sham ultrasound for the control group. While the authors note that "blinding of the physiotherapist who applied the treatment was not possible," there is no evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose because of the trial context. Finally, the authors explicitly state, "The analysis was done by intention to treat."

    3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias
           a. Judgement: Outcome data were available for all randomized participants.
           b. Rationale: The authors clearly report, "During the study no patient was lost or dropped out." Therefore, there is no risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

    4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias
           a. Judgement: The outcome was measured appropriately, and the outcome assessors (the patients, for a self-reported VAS) were blinded to the intervention received.
           b. Rationale: For participant-reported outcomes like the VAS, the participant is considered the outcome assessor. Because a sham ultrasound (device turned off) was used, participants were unaware of their assigned intervention. Furthermore, the clinical measurements were taken by a blinded independent assessor: "A physiotherapist appraiser assessed the patients’ pain... After the completion of the preliminary measurements, another physiotherapist executed the interventions."

    5. selective reporting: some concerns
           a. Judgement: While the reported outcomes match those described in the methods section, there is insufficient information regarding a pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available.
           b. Rationale: The study reports the outcomes exactly as defined in the methods section (VAS, algometer, and inclinometer) at the specified time points. However, without a formal pre-published protocol or statistical analysis plan detailing the exact analysis methods (e.g., changes in scores vs. final values), we cannot definitively rule out selection of the reported result from multiple eligible analyses. Thus, it raises some concerns.
