**Outcome assessed:** HAM-D (Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression) score, comparing sertraline with placebo at the end of the 10-week intervention / longitudinal follow-up. The assessment considers the effect of assignment to sertraline versus placebo at baseline.

1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** The report describes an appropriate random sequence and reasonably adequate allocation concealment. Participants were assigned using “a block design (blocks of 8) with computer-generated random number sequences in individual presealed envelopes.” Randomization occurred after eligibility and medical clearance, and “all participants were randomized into the medication or placebo arm of the study by the pharmacy.” The pharmacy also implemented masking and dispensed identically marked medication.  
   
   Baseline characteristics do not provide compelling evidence that randomization failed. Although the trial was small and some numerical differences existed (e.g., injury severity), the authors report no statistically significant between-group differences for demographic or baseline clinical measures, including HAM-D. The observed differences are plausibly compatible with chance in a small trial. Therefore, the randomization process is judged at low risk of bias.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** This was a double-blind placebo-controlled trial. The article states that “both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received,” and that active medication and placebo were packaged with “identical markings.” Thus, participants, treating clinicians, and study staff were unlikely to have systematically altered care or behaviour because of knowledge of treatment allocation.  
   
   The intervention procedures were comparable between groups: both groups attended regular physician and assessment visits, dose adjustments were made under the same protocol, and staff contact was standardized “in an attempt to control for psychologic support.” The authors also state that an “intent-to-treat analysis compared the outcomes of all study participants, including participants who received minimal treatment.” The longitudinal random-effects models used repeated observations and can include participants with differing numbers of assessments.  
   
   There is no evidence of deviations arising from the trial context that were unbalanced between groups and likely to affect HAM-D scores. One participant withdrew because of side effects, but this is not, by itself, evidence of a trial-context deviation affecting the effect of assignment. Therefore, for the effect of assignment to intervention, risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions is low.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Missing HAM-D outcome data were substantial. Of 52 randomized participants, only 41 completed the clinical trial: 22/27 in the sertraline group and 19/25 in the placebo group. Thus, 11/52 participants (21%) did not complete follow-up. The end-of-treatment HAM-D values shown in Table 2 are based on 22 sertraline and 19 placebo participants, rather than all randomized participants.  
   
   Although dropout numbers were broadly similar between groups (5 versus 6), there is evidence that missingness may have depended on the underlying outcome. The authors report that “the dropouts tend[ed] to have higher HAM-D scores” at their last assessment than those remaining in treatment (F=4.0, P=.052). Reasons for non-completion also included clinical deterioration or adverse events: one participant was removed because of increased anxiety, one because of increased depression following a family death, and one because of an allergic reaction; the remaining participants had protocol non-compliance, such as missed visits or failure to collect medication.  
   
   The report used last-observation-carried-forward for some analyses and random-effects models for repeated measures, but it does not provide sensitivity analyses demonstrating that the treatment comparison was robust under plausible assumptions about the missing HAM-D scores. Given the amount of missing data and the indication that dropout was associated with higher depression severity, missingness was likely related to the true outcome. This meets RoB 2 criteria for high risk of bias.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** HAM-D is a standard clinician-rated measure of depression severity, and the article describes it as a “widely used clinician-rated measurement of depression severity” with established reliability and validity. The same outcome measure was used in both intervention groups at the same scheduled time points.  
   
   Importantly, the trial was double-blind: “both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received.” HAM-D ratings were conducted independently from the study physician by “a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist,” and post-treatment assessments were administered by a different staff member from the baseline assessor. These procedures reduce the likelihood that knowledge of intervention assignment affected outcome assessment.  
   
   While successful blinding was not formally tested and adverse effects could theoretically have revealed allocation for some participants, there is no evidence that HAM-D ascertainment differed between groups or that assessor knowledge influenced ratings. The outcome-measurement domain is therefore at low risk of bias.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The trial registration identifies HAM-D depression at the end of treatment as a primary outcome, which is consistent with the article’s designation of HAM-D as the primary outcome measure. The publication reports several relevant HAM-D results, including end-of-treatment scores, responder analyses, longitudinal random-effects analyses, repeated-measures analyses, and analyses of HAM-D subscales. There is no direct evidence that the reported total HAM-D result was selected because it was favourable or statistically significant.  
   
   However, a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is not available. The registry record was first posted after the trial began (trial start June 2003; first posting October 2005), and it gives limited detail on the planned analysis, such as the precise preferred time point, whether final values or change scores would be used, covariate adjustment, handling of missing data, or the specific primary analysis. The registry describes a “12-week” period, whereas the publication reports a 10-week intervention. In addition, the publication reports several eligible HAM-D analyses and subscales, creating opportunities for selective choice among outcome measurements and analyses.  
   
   Since the available registration information does not allow confirmation that the reported HAM-D analysis was specified before unblinded outcome data were available, but there is no clear evidence of result-based selection, this domain raises some concerns rather than high risk of bias.
