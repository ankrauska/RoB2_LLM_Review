# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Surgical Time
## Effect of Interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The trial states that a "random number table" was used for allocation, which satisfies the requirement for a random sequence generation method. However, there is no information whatsoever regarding allocation concealment. Additionally, baseline characteristics, while reported as non-significant, cannot be fully verified as compatible with chance given the absence of p-values for these comparisons.

**b. Rationale:** The article states: "Using the random number table method, 90 cases were divided into group A and group B (n=45)." This indicates a random component was used (satisfies signalling question 1.1 as "Yes"). However, there is no description of how the allocation sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants (signalling question 1.2 = "No information"). The report only states baseline characteristics "did not show significant differences... (P>0.05)" without providing actual p-values or effect sizes for age, tumor size, or nodule distribution, making it difficult to fully rule out problems with randomization (signalling question 1.3 = "No information," though no strong evidence of imbalance is apparent). Per the algorithm: Random=Yes, Concealment=No information, Imbalance=No information → **Some concerns**.

---

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** This trial compares two fundamentally different procedures (percutaneous microwave ablation vs. laparoscopic resection), making blinding of participants and personnel impossible. Surgical time is a variable that is directly determined by the intervention delivery itself, and lack of blinding of personnel could plausibly influence procedural conduct/duration, but more importantly, the analysis does not appear to follow strict ITT principles given no reporting of dropouts, exclusions, or crossovers.

**b. Rationale:** Because this is a surgical vs. percutaneous procedure comparison, blinding of participants and personnel is not feasible ("Were participants aware of their assigned intervention" = Yes; "Were carers/personnel aware" = Yes) — this is inherent to the trial design. There is no discussion in the manuscript of any protocol deviations, crossovers, or exclusions after randomization, and no CONSORT flow diagram is provided to confirm all 90 randomized participants were analyzed for surgical time. Given the complete absence of information on whether deviations occurred and whether analysis strictly followed ITT principles (no explicit statement of an ITT analysis, no accounting for exclusions), and given that surgical time itself is entirely dependent on how personnel (who are unblinded and non-blindable) conduct the procedure, there is substantial uncertainty. Because personnel awareness of intervention could directly and substantially affect conduct of the procedure (e.g., conversion decisions, deviations in technique) and there is no evidence this was accounted for or that deviations were balanced/absent, this is judged as **High risk of bias**.

---

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** All 90 randomized patients (45 in each group) appear to have contributed surgical time data, as reported in Table 1, with no indication of missing data for this outcome.

**b. Rationale:** Table 1 reports surgical time data for the full sample: "Group A | 45 | 96.7±27.8" and "Group B | 45 | 134.2±34.3." Both groups show n=45, matching the number of patients originally randomized to each group ("90 cases were divided into group A and group B (n=45)"). There is no mention of loss to follow-up, dropout, or exclusion affecting the surgical time outcome, since this outcome is collected intraoperatively and is not vulnerable to post-treatment dropout. This satisfies signalling question 3.1 ("Yes") leading to **Low risk of bias**.

---

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Surgical time is an objective outcome measure (an observer-reported outcome not involving judgement) that is unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of the intervention assignment, since it is typically recorded directly (e.g., via clock time from incision/puncture to closure) rather than subjectively assessed.

**b. Rationale:** Surgical time is a objectively measurable, recorded variable (start to end time of the procedure) rather than a subjective clinical judgement or participant-reported outcome. While personnel were not blinded (blinding was not feasible given the different procedures), the measurement method itself (recording of time) is not the type of outcome "likely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention received" in the way a subjective symptom or judgement-based outcome would be. There is no indication that the method of measuring the outcome was inappropriate, or that measurement/ascertainment methods differed between groups in a way that would bias the timing measurement itself. This corresponds to signalling question 4.1 = "No," 4.2 = "No," and question 4.3 (awareness of assessor) is not likely to translate into biased measurement for this objective/mechanical outcome, yielding **Low risk of bias**.

---

### 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Surgical time appears to have been measured and analyzed in a single, pre-specified way, with results reported completely and consistent with a straightforward, single primary analysis; there is no trial registration available to verify a pre-specified analysis plan, but there is also no evidence of selective reporting (e.g., multiple time-point measures, multiple analytic approaches, or selective outcome switching) for this specific outcome.

**b. Rationale:** The methods section states plainly: "Surgical time, intraoperative blood loss and incidence of complications were recorded for the two groups," and Table 1 reports this outcome fully for both groups with means, standard deviations, t-statistic, and p-value, with no indication of multiple scales, timepoints, or alternative analyses from which this result could have been selectively chosen. No trial registry record was identified/available for this trial to confirm a pre-specified statistical analysis plan (signalling question 5.1 = "No information"), but there is no evidence suggesting the reported surgical time result was selected from multiple eligible measurements or analyses (signalling questions 5.2 and 5.3 = "No" / "Probably no," as surgical time has only one plausible operationalization and analytic approach). This yields **Low risk of bias**.
