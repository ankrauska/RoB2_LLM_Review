1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The study reports computer-based randomization: “*Students were randomized into three groups using a computer program*.” This supports a random allocation sequence. However, the report does not describe allocation concealment—e.g., central allocation, sequential opaque envelopes, or an independent allocator—before students were assigned to teaching modules. Therefore, it is unclear whether those enrolling or allocating students could have foreseen assignments.  
   
   Baseline characteristics do not indicate an evident failure of randomization: the three groups each included 40 students, had broadly similar sex distributions (CT 22/40 female; 3D 21/40; 3Dp 22/40), and similar mean age and medical-school seniority. Nonetheless, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the teaching module at baseline.  
   b. **Rationale:** Students and teachers were necessarily aware of the allocated teaching module (CT images, manipulable 3D images, or physical 3D-printed models). However, for the requested effect of assignment to intervention, awareness alone does not establish bias. Bias would require deviations from the allocated module that arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, affected performance, and were unbalanced between groups.  
   
   The intervention procedures appear to have been delivered as assigned: “*The TM of the first group… was based on the CT images*”; “*The TM of the second group… was based on the 3D images*”; and “*In the last group (3Dp group), each student received the two fracture models*.” The authors also state that “*A teacher provided a detailed interpretation of the fracture in each group*,” indicating a common instructional component. There is no report of cross-over, exclusion from the allocated module, differential co-interventions, or other trial-context deviations likely to affect performance.  
   
   All 120 randomized students appear to have been analysed in their allocated groups: “*a total of 120 medical students… participated, and no one dropped out*.” Thus, the analysis is consistent with estimating the effect of assignment.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** The study explicitly states: “*a total of 120 medical students… participated, and no one dropped out*.” The flow diagram likewise indicates 40 students in each randomized group proceeding to answer the performance questions. Table 3 reports results for all students in each group, with sex-specific sample sizes adding to 40 per group.  
   
   Therefore, performance-score data appear available for all randomized participants, and there is no indication of missing outcome values, differential missingness, or imputation. Missing outcome data are consequently unlikely to have affected the result.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Performance was measured using a common examination administered after the teaching session: “*each student answered 10 questions regarding spinal fractures… assessed by an examination paper*.” The outcome was the number of correct answers to predefined anatomical questions with specified correct responses (Table 1). This is an objective, appropriately matched method for assessing the stated learning outcome—identification of spinal-fracture anatomy.  
   
   The same examination was used across groups, so there is no evidence that outcome measurement or ascertainment differed by allocated teaching module. Although students were aware of their teaching module, the performance outcome consisted of correct versus incorrect answers to fixed questions, rather than a subjective self-rated outcome. There is no indication that scoring was influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment. The examination contained “*many ‘3D images’*,” but this was applied uniformly to all randomized groups and does not itself constitute differential outcome measurement.  

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article reports the principal performance outcomes—sum of correct answers and time required to answer the 10 anatomical questions—and reports all four evaluative questions. However, neither the article nor the available registration information as provided documents a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan specifying the precise performance outcome, analysis model, handling of sex, and planned comparisons before unblinded outcome data were available.  
   
   The report describes several possible analyses, including two-way ANOVA, sex-stratified analyses, and multiple post-hoc comparisons: “*The data was analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)… Tukey’s or Sidak’s multiple comparison test was used for post hoc analysis.*” Because multiple analytical choices and comparisons were possible, and because a prospectively finalized analysis plan cannot be verified from the available materials, it cannot be excluded that the reported performance result was selected from multiple eligible analyses. There is no direct evidence of selective reporting; therefore, the appropriate judgement is **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.
