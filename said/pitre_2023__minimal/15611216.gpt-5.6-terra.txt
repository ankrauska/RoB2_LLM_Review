**Outcome assessed:** Agitation Score, pretest to posttest Group × Time comparison reported in Table 4 (BACE intervention versus control), considering the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants were “randomly assigned,” but does not describe how the random sequence was generated or how allocation was concealed before enrolment/assignment. Allocation appears to have been organized by weekly time blocks: “We randomly assigned participants to study groups in time blocks of 1 week” and “also randomized time blocks as treatment or control condition weeks.” The absence of information on sequence generation and, especially, allocation concealment raises concerns that assignment could potentially have been predictable to recruiting or study personnel.

   Baseline characteristics were broadly similar, including pretest agitation (BACE mean 38.97 versus control 32.59; *p* = .186), MMSE, age, functional stage, and length of stay. However, the groups differed in pretest arousal imbalance (107.4 versus 84.6 minutes/12 hr; *p* = .012). This is a relevant prognostic characteristic given that the intervention was intended to alter arousal imbalance and that it was related conceptually to agitation. The investigators adjusted for it, but adjustment cannot fully resolve a possible failure of randomization. On balance, this single imbalance may be compatible with chance, but the combination of incomplete reporting of randomization/concealment and the observed imbalance warrants some concerns rather than low risk of bias.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Although the article reports that “data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions,” the personnel responsible for implementing the BACE activity-schedule changes necessarily knew which participants were assigned to the intervention. More importantly for the effect of assignment, the reported analysis excluded participants on the basis of post-randomization intervention delivery.

   Specifically, the article states: “Twelve died, 10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention, and 2 were excluded for erroneous or missing data. The final sample was 78, with 36 receiving the experimental intervention and 42 in the control group.” It further states: “A participant had to have been given at least one of the prescribed changes in activity schedule … to be included in the analysis.” Thus, participants assigned to BACE who did not receive at least some intervention were excluded rather than analysed in their originally assigned group.

   This is not an intention-to-treat analysis of assignment to BACE versus control. Excluding 10 participants because the intervention was not delivered is a post-randomization, per-protocol-type exclusion and could have a substantial impact on the result, particularly in a trial with only 102 enrolled participants and 78 analysed. Therefore, risk of bias is high for the assignment effect.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data contributing to the Table 4 analysis were available for 78 of 102 participants who entered the study (approximately 76%). Thus, data were not available for nearly all randomized/enrolled participants. The report identifies 12 deaths and 2 exclusions for “erroneous or missing data.” The 10 participants who were “dropped” because they were not given the intervention chiefly raise a deviation-from-intended-intervention problem, but also contribute to the incomplete final analytic sample.

   No arm-specific accounting of deaths, missing agitation data, or exclusions is provided, and no sensitivity analysis or statistical approach addressing the potential consequences of missing outcome data is reported. Death or clinical deterioration in this frail population could plausibly be associated with participants’ health status and potentially with agitation. However, the article does not provide enough information to establish that missingness was likely related to the true agitation outcome, or that its reasons/proportions differed between groups. This supports a judgement of some concerns rather than high risk.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Agitation was measured in both groups using the same structured direct-observation procedures: research assistants observed participants for three minutes every 15 minutes between 8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. on pretest and posttest days. The outcome assessors were reported to be blinded: “Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions.”

   Although agitation is a subjective observer-rated outcome and therefore could be influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment, the reported blinding of research assistants substantially reduces this risk. The investigators also described extensive observer training and reliability monitoring: training continued until inter-rater reliability was at least .85, and post-training agitation reliabilities ranged from .95 to .984. The same measurement method and schedule were used across groups, with no indication that ascertainment differed by assigned intervention.

   The Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument was initially planned but replaced before data collection because sufficient reliability could not be achieved. The substitute visual analogue scale was operationalized using prespecified intensity parameters. This change does not, by itself, demonstrate an inappropriate measurement method or differential measurement between groups. Therefore, measurement bias is judged low.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report provides the numerical result in Table 4 and describes the principal analysis as a repeated-measures ANCOVA adjusted for pretest arousal imbalance and MMSE. It also reports additional analyses, including adding therapeutic activities as a covariate, within-group paired analyses, analyses by arousal-balance periods, and exploratory subgroup interaction analyses.

   However, there is no sufficiently detailed, dated pre-specified statistical analysis plan in the material provided to verify that the Table 4 analysis—including the selected covariates, outcome scale, analysis metric, and time-point—was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The paper also reports that the originally planned agitation instrument (ABMI) was replaced by a visual analogue scale because of inadequate reliability during training. This change may have been legitimate and apparently occurred before outcome measurement, but its timing relative to finalized analysis intentions cannot be fully verified.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported Table 4 result was selected from multiple outcome measurements or analyses on the basis of its statistical significance or direction. Nevertheless, in the absence of a detailed pre-specified analysis plan or sufficiently informative trial-registration details for this specific result, selective reporting cannot be excluded. Accordingly, there are some concerns.
