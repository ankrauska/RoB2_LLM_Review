# Risk of Bias Assessment: WRAP Trial (Outcome: Brief Symptom Inventory - Psychiatric Symptom Severity)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat)

---

## 1. Bias due to Randomization

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:**
- Randomization sequence generation: The article states "Randomization was performed by SRL staff at the end of each interview using a random allocation sequence programmed into CAPI administration software that allowed for complete allocation concealment up to the point of assignment." This indicates a random component was used (Y), and allocation was concealed (Y) via computer-generated, remote randomization at the point of assignment.
- Allocation concealment: The description strongly supports adequate concealment—interviewers and respondents "had no way of knowing each subject's study condition until after the assignment had occurred."
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows no statistically significant differences between groups across a large number of demographic, clinical, and service-use variables. The article explicitly states: "The success of randomization was confirmed by the absence of statistically significant differences by study condition at baseline on all characteristics."
- However, some concerns arise because: (1) the exact method of sequence generation (e.g., simple vs. blocked randomization) is not detailed beyond "random allocation sequence," and (2) there is a differential dropout pattern worth flagging—more experimental (25, 9.1%) than control (11, 3.9%) participants were lost to follow-up, which, while addressed under the missing data domain, raises a flag about whether the groups remained comparable at the point they contributed data. This alone does not constitute definitive evidence of a randomization *process* problem, but combined with limited procedural detail, "Some concerns" (rather than "Low") is a conservative, defensible judgement.

---

## 2. Deviations from Intended Intervention (Effect of Assignment to Intervention)

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- **2.1/2.2 (Awareness of assignment):** Participants and those delivering the WRAP intervention (peer facilitators) were necessarily aware of intervention assignment—this is an unblinded behavioral intervention (Y to 2.1 and 2.2). Notably, though, outcome *assessors* (interviewers) were blinded: "interviewers were blinded to respondents' study condition," and blinding integrity checks found only 4% of interviews had condition inadvertently revealed.
- **2.3 (Deviations due to trial context):** There is little evidence that non-protocol interventions or contamination arose specifically because of the trial context (e.g., recruitment/engagement effects) that would bias the assignment effect. Control participants continued "services as usual," and no significant differences were found in receipt of case management, medication management, individual therapy, or other services between groups. The main deviation—participation in peer support groups by control participants (41.9%–44.9%)—was measured, but this reflects real-world/community exposure available to both groups equally as part of "usual care" rather than a trial-context-driven deviation inconsistent with the protocol. The authors treat this as a naturally occurring co-intervention and control for it in sensitivity analyses, which did not change conclusions ("time by study condition remaining significant in all analyses").
- **2.4/2.5:** Given the above, these deviations are unlikely to represent a substantial, unbalanced influence driven by trial context that would bias the ITT estimate.
- **2.6 (Appropriate analysis):** The primary analysis was explicitly intent-to-treat: "Outcomes were assessed... using an intent-to-treat mixed-effects random regression analysis." The flow diagram (Figure 1) confirms all randomized participants with available data were retained in their assigned groups (251 experimental, 268 control), with no post-randomization exclusions for non-adherence (the 43 experimental subjects who did not receive the intervention were retained in the ITT analysis).
- **2.7:** Not applicable, as an appropriate ITT analysis was used.

Given blinded outcome assessment, no strong evidence of context-driven differential deviations affecting the outcome, and a genuine ITT analysis, this domain is judged **Low risk**.

## 3. Missing Outcome Data

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- Overall attrition was low: "Of the 519 subjects who completed T1 assessments, 458 subjects (88.2%) completed T2 interviews, and 448 (86.3%) completed T3 interviews, for a combined attrition rate of 6.6%."
- Rates of missingness were similar between groups and not statistically significantly different: at T2, "224 (89.2%) of the intervention group and 234 (87.3%) of the control group (χ²=0.49, P=.29)"; at T3, "220 (87.6%)... and 228 (85.1%)... (χ²=0.39, P=.23)."
- Reasons for missingness were documented and appear unrelated to the outcome's true value in a differential way: "reasons including death or ill health, moving away from the area, and formal withdrawal from the study." Notably, most dropouts among the intervention group (68%) had received no WRAP sessions, suggesting dropout was linked to non-engagement rather than to symptom trajectory per se.
- Given the small and statistically similar proportions of missing data between arms, and no clear evidence that missingness depended on the true (unobserved) outcome value in a way that differed by group, the risk of bias due to missing outcome data is low.

---

## 4. Measurement of the Outcome

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- The BSI is an appropriate, validated self-report instrument for psychiatric symptom severity ("high concordance with clinician symptom assessment," frequently used in randomized trials of mental health interventions) — not inappropriate (4.1 = No).
- Measurement method and timing were identical across groups (same instrument, same assessment schedule at T1, T2, T3) — no differential ascertainment (4.2 = No).
- The outcome assessor for this participant-reported outcome is technically the participant themselves; however, the *administration* of the interview was blinded: "Researchers employed by the UIC Survey Research Laboratory (SRL) administered structured telephone interviews, and interviewers were blinded to respondents' study condition." Blinding integrity was checked and confirmed largely successful (unblinding occurred in only 4% of T2/T3 interviews).
- Because the BSI is a self-report measure, participants themselves knew their treatment assignment (unblinded participants), which per RoB2 guidance means assessment of the outcome could potentially be influenced by knowledge of intervention received (some concern is warranted in principle for participant-reported outcomes). However:
  - There is no strong indication (e.g., a placebo-like intervention with high expectancy effects targeting this specific symptom scale) that participants' self-reports of general psychiatric symptoms (BSI covers anxiety, depression, somatization, etc.) were being consciously or strongly biased by demand characteristics beyond what is typical/unavoidable in behavioral trials of this kind.
  - The interview format with blinded interviewers reduces the likelihood of interviewer-induced bias, and the structured/standardized nature of the CAPI-administered BSI limits opportunity for differential interpretation.
- On balance, since the interviewers (who facilitate/administer the measure) were blinded and checks confirmed low unblinding rates, and there's no compelling evidence that assessment was likely influenced by knowledge of assignment in a way that would bias this particular outcome, this domain is judged **Low risk**, acknowledging this is a judgment call for a self-reported outcome in an unblinded behavioral trial.

---

## 5. Selective Reporting

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- The trial was prospectively registered (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01024569), and the article states: "all outcomes, hypotheses, and statistical analyses presented here were prespecified at the time the proposal was submitted to the federal government for funding consideration."
- Comparing the registry (which lists BSI Positive Symptom Score explicitly as a component of the primary outcome "Psychiatric Symptoms Recovery Using the Brief Symptoms Inventory (BSI)") with the published article, both the Global Severity Index and Positive Symptom Total from the BSI are reported in the article (Table 2, Table 3) with full descriptive statistics and MIXREG model results (estimates, SE, p-values) at all three time points.
- There is no evidence of selection from multiple eligible measurements or analyses: the article reports the outcome measurement and analysis method (ITT MIXREG) consistent with the pre-specified plan, and results (both significant and more modest findings, e.g., the "pathways" subscale of Hope showing no significant effect) are reported transparently, suggesting no selective suppression of unfavorable results.
- The registry and article match in analytic approach (MIXREG, intent-to-treat), and effect sizes/odds ratios are also reported for the BSI outcome, further supporting transparency.
- No discrepancies were identified between the registered outcome definition and the reported result for BSI Global Symptom Severity/Positive Symptom Total.
