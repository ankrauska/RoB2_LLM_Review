1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was computer generated, managed independently, and concealed until after induction of anaesthesia. This should have prevented surgeons, participants, and recruitment personnel from knowing the allocation before enrolment and surgery. Baseline characteristics were generally comparable; the isolated difference in baseline anxiety score is compatible with chance and does not indicate a failure of randomization.

   The publication states that participants were randomized “**using random number codes disclosed after anaesthesia induction**” and, more specifically, that randomization used “**computer-generated random number codes managed by an independent study member**,” with minimization by important prognostic factors (age, BMI, diabetes duration, insulin treatment, and ethnicity).

   The group sizes were close to the planned 1:1 ratio (SR-LRYGB 56; LSG 58). Table 1 shows no material imbalance in diabetes duration, insulin use, HbA1c, BMI category, age, or ethnicity. Although female sex was somewhat more frequent in the SR-LRYGB group (59% versus 45%), this alone does not demonstrate a problematic randomization process.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was designed as participant- and outcome-assessor-blinded, with efforts to standardize perioperative care and diabetes-medication management. Although the operating surgeons necessarily knew the procedure delivered, there is no evidence that trial-context deviations from the assigned procedures occurred differentially between groups in a manner likely to affect HbA1c-defined diabetes remission.

   The report describes a “**double-blind (patient and assessor) clinical trial**.” It also reports that the surgical port placements and methylene-blue leak testing were identical for both procedures. Diabetes medication management was standardized: “**All pharmacological agents for diabetes were discontinued following surgery subject to endocrinologist review prior to discharge**,” and medications were subsequently recommenced “**according to a standard medical review protocol, by endocrinologists blinded to treatment allocation**.”

   The remission results were analysed according to randomized groups: all 56 randomized SR-LRYGB participants and the 53 LSG participants with 1-year data were included in their respective allocated groups. There is no indication of an as-treated analysis, a naïve per-protocol analysis, or exclusion because participants did not receive their allocated surgery. The three LSG participants who underwent additional surgery included one revision to SR-LRYGB, but this occurred after the primary 1-year intervention assignment and does not appear to have led to reassignment in the reported analysis.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** HbA1c remission data at 1 year were missing for 5/58 participants (8.6%) randomized to LSG, whereas no SR-LRYGB participants were missing. This differential loss to follow-up could materially influence the remission comparison, particularly because remission was a dichotomous outcome and the between-group differences were small.

   The CONSORT information reports that “**5 [participants] were lost to follow up**” in the LSG group, with none lost in the SR-LRYGB group. Consequently, the remission analyses used denominators of 56 for SR-LRYGB and 53 for LSG, rather than all 114 randomized participants.

   The reported reason for missing data was “**failure to make contact and/or to attend appointments beyond 3 months post-operatively**.” Such non-attendance may plausibly be related to participants’ health, glycaemic status, engagement with postoperative care, medication use, or treatment experience. The loss of outcome data was also unbalanced between groups. No sensitivity analyses were reported to show that the remission findings were robust to plausible outcomes among the five missing LSG participants. For example, at the HbA1c ≤38 mmol/mol threshold, the observed remission difference was only 5 percentage points (43% LSG versus 38% SR-LRYGB); outcomes among five missing LSG participants could meaningfully change this comparison.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** T2D remission was defined using objective laboratory HbA1c thresholds together with absence of glucose-lowering medication. The same thresholds and measurement approach were used in both groups, at the same 1-year time point. In addition, the study states that outcome assessors and the endocrinologists responsible for medication decisions were blinded to treatment allocation.

   The outcome was explicitly defined as HbA1c values “**without the need for any glucose lowering therapy**,” using four specified thresholds: ≤38 mmol/mol, <42 mmol/mol, <48 mmol/mol, and <53 mmol/mol. HbA1c is an objective biochemical measurement with little scope for subjective outcome-assessor judgement. Medication information was obtained through “**clinical data … extracted by medical records review**,” and the relevant clinical management was undertaken by “**endocrinologists blinded to treatment allocation**.”

   There is no indication that HbA1c testing or ascertainment of diabetes-medication use differed systematically between intervention groups. Therefore, knowledge of the procedure received was unlikely to influence classification of HbA1c-defined remission.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The available trial-registration record is insufficiently specific to confirm that the four reported 1-year HbA1c remission thresholds, their timing, and the corresponding analyses were pre-specified before unblinded data became available. The registration identifies the primary outcome only broadly as “**Remission of type 2 diabetes mellitus/ glycaemic control**,” without specifying HbA1c threshold(s), medication-free status, time point, or analysis approach.

   The article describes the primary outcome differently: “**Primary outcome is the proportion of patients achieving HbA1c <42 mmol/mol (<6%) without glucose lowering therapy at 5 years**.” The present report instead presents four alternative remission definitions at 1 year: HbA1c ≤38, <42, <48, and <53 mmol/mol without diabetes medication. Reporting all four thresholds reduces concern that a single threshold was selected because it was favourable; indeed, all four results are shown. However, without a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized protocol or statistical analysis plan, it is not possible to verify that the 1-year analyses and all four definitions were prespecified.

   Thus, there is no clear evidence that the reported result was selected on the basis of its findings, but there is insufficient information to rule out selection among multiple potentially eligible HbA1c definitions, thresholds, time points, or analytical approaches.
