1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence was generated using computer random numbers, but allocation concealment was not described.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors state that patients “were allocated into one of the two groups based on a computer random numbers table,” which supports a genuinely random allocation sequence. The groups were equal in size (44 per group), and Table 1 shows no important baseline imbalances in measured prognostic characteristics, including age, ejection fraction, EuroSCORE, creatinine, cardiopulmonary-bypass time, and baseline NT-proBNP.

   However, there is no information on how the sequence was concealed before and during enrolment—for example, whether allocation was administered centrally, by an independent person, or through sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. Absence of information on allocation concealment leads to *some concerns* under RoB 2, despite apparently adequate sequence generation and balanced baseline characteristics.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to magnesium sulfate versus placebo at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial is described as a “double-blind clinical trial” and used a placebo: “The same volume of normal saline was administered as a placebo via a 50 ml syringe intravenously.” The investigators also report that, apart from magnesium sulfate or placebo, “the anesthesia methods and protocols for the two patient groups were exactly the same,” and that the same surgical, cardiopulmonary-bypass, and postoperative-care protocols were used.

   These features make important deviations caused by awareness of treatment assignment unlikely. The reported NT-proBNP comparison retained the randomized group structure, with Table 3 reporting results for 44 participants in each group. Although the authors mention that patients requiring triple inotropic therapy during CPB weaning would be excluded, the report states that 88 patients were enrolled and presents outcome data for all 44 participants in each randomized group; thus, there is no evidence that post-randomization exclusions affected the NT-proBNP analysis. The group comparison using Student’s *t* test appears to estimate the effect of assignment to the randomized intervention.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Eighty-eight patients were enrolled and randomized equally, and Table 3 reports both baseline and postoperative NT-proBNP results for the full stated group sizes: magnesium sulfate, *n* = 44; placebo, *n* = 44. Therefore, NT-proBNP data appear to have been available for all randomized participants.

   The article does not report losses to follow-up or missing postoperative NT-proBNP specimens. Given that NT-proBNP was measured at 24 hours after ICU arrival during the immediate postoperative admission, outcome ascertainment was feasible for essentially all participants. There is no indication of differential missingness, imputation, or exclusion of participants with missing NT-proBNP data.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** NT-proBNP is an objective laboratory biomarker. Blood samples were collected according to the same specified schedule in both groups—before anaesthesia and 24 hours after ICU arrival—and underwent standardized handling: centrifugation, freezing, transfer, and storage procedures were “kept consistent throughout the study.”

   NT-proBNP was measured with an electrochemical luminescence immunoassay using an Elecsys 2010 analyser. The authors report that this assay had a coefficient of variation of 2.2–3.2% and a detection range of 5–35,000 pg/mL. The outcome was therefore measured using an appropriate, objective, and comparable method across groups. Even if laboratory personnel were aware of allocation, an automated immunoassay leaves little or no scope for knowledge of treatment assignment to influence the measured NT-proBNP concentration.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies NT-proBNP as the “main study end point” and reports both baseline and 24-hour postoperative NT-proBNP values. However, the available registration information does not provide assurance that a detailed analysis plan—specifying the precise outcome time point, measurement approach, and statistical analysis—was finalized before unblinded outcome data became available.

   In particular, the study reportedly began in January 2010 and ran for 12 months, whereas the IRCT identifier is **IRCT201205069650N1**, suggesting that registration occurred after the study period. A retrospectively registered record cannot reliably demonstrate that the reported 24-hour NT-proBNP comparison was selected in accordance with prospectively finalized analysis intentions.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected from multiple NT-proBNP measures or analyses based on statistical significance. Nevertheless, because prospective, sufficiently detailed analysis intentions cannot be confirmed, RoB 2 indicates *some concerns* for selection of the reported result.
