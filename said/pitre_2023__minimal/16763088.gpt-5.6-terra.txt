1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The study used a randomized split-mouth, cross-quadrant allocation, with each patient contributing brackets treated using both systems. The allocation sequence was generated using block randomization, and allocation was concealed from operators until the patient had entered the trial. There is no indication of important baseline imbalance or manipulation of allocation.

   The report states: “*Randomization for allocation of either of the two etch and primer systems to the upper right quadrant was undertaken using a block randomization method*.” It further states that “*The operators were unaware of the assignment until the patient was entered into the trial*,” supporting concealment until enrolment. The within-patient split-mouth design also reduces the potential for confounding by patient-level factors such as age, sex, oral hygiene, and underlying propensity for bracket failure.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants were blinded to which system was used, although operators could not be blinded because the interventions had different application procedures: “*The patients were unaware which system had been used on each side of the mouth, but as the two systems had different modes of application, it was not possible to blind the operators*.”

   Operator awareness could potentially lead to differential implementation. However, the bonding procedures for both interventions were explicitly specified and standardized, including cleaning, isolation, curing time, adhesive, bracket type, and initial archwires. There is no reported evidence that knowledge of allocation resulted in deviations from protocol, differential co-interventions, or unequal treatment of quadrants that would plausibly affect bracket failure.

   The analysis appears consistent with the assigned interventions: brackets were analysed according to the etching/primer system allocated to their quadrant, and analyses accounted for clustering of brackets within mouths. There is no indication of post-randomization exclusion of brackets because of treatment received, adherence, or failure to implement the allocated system.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Follow-up was essentially complete. Of 39 participants, only one moved away after seven months; no bracket failures had occurred for that participant before follow-up ceased. The report states: “*All patients were observed for the entire 12-month period apart from one 13-year-old male who moved to another city after 7 months of observation, during which time no bracket failures occurred. His data was still included in the analysis*.”

   Thus, outcome information was available for nearly all randomized participants and brackets. The small amount of censoring—one participant out of 39—was handled using survival analysis, and is unlikely to have materially changed the estimated difference in failure rates between the self-etching and conventional systems. There is no evidence that missingness differed by intervention group or was related to unobserved bracket failure.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Bracket failure is a relatively objective clinical outcome: a bracket is either detached/failed or remains bonded. The same ascertainment procedures were used for brackets in both intervention groups during the same follow-up period. The study describes that, after bonding, “*Each patient was then monitored for 12 months. If a bond failed, the tooth on which the failure occurred [and] the date of failure … were recorded*.”

   Although operators were not blinded to the intervention, it is unlikely that knowledge of the etch/primer system could materially influence whether a bracket failure occurred or its recognition. There is no indication that brackets treated with one system were reviewed more frequently, assessed using different criteria, or subjected to different thresholds for defining failure. Therefore, differential measurement or ascertainment bias is unlikely.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication clearly reports the principal failure-rate comparison and associated survival analysis. It reports both the crude 12-month failure rates—11.2% for self-etching primer and 3.9% for conventional etch/primer—and the Cox regression result: “*the conventional etch and primer system [had] a 60 per cent reduced chance of bracket failure over a 12-month observation period*” (hazard ratio 0.4, 95% CI 0.2–0.8).

   However, there is no accessible prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan in the materials provided, and the reported trial-registration information does not establish that the precise outcome definition, 12-month time point, and analytic approach were pre-specified before unblinded outcome data were available. Several possible analyses were conducted, including failure rates, Kaplan–Meier curves, Cox regression, logistic regression, subgroup analyses by sex, age, operator, tooth location, and number of bracket manipulations.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported failure-rate result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable to a particular intervention. Nevertheless, without a sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan or registry outcome specification, it is not possible to exclude selective choice among eligible analyses or outcome presentations.
