# Risk of Bias Assessment: Nambour Sunscreen Trial (Outcome: Change in Photoaging)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), per instructions.

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise primarily because allocation concealment is not described, though sequence generation was adequately random and baseline characteristics appear generally well-balanced.

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: "one of our study investigators, who had no knowledge of the participants, randomly assigned them by using a computer-generated randomized list" — this supports a "Yes" to random sequence generation.
- Allocation concealment: No information is provided about concealment mechanisms (e.g., sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes; central allocation). Given that sunscreen could not be placebo-controlled ("placebo sunscreen was considered unethical"), and the investigator who generated the list had no contact with participants, this reduces some risk, but there is no explicit statement confirming that the sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants until assignment. This warrants "No information" on concealment.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows generally similar baseline characteristics between daily and discretionary sunscreen groups (phenotype, sun exposure, pretrial sunscreen use). However, the trial notes "slightly more people were randomly assigned to β-carotene than to placebo (P = 0.06)" — this pertains to the β-carotene factor, not sunscreen, and is only borderline significant, compatible with chance. No mention of significant imbalance for the sunscreen comparison specifically.
- Per the algorithm: random sequence = Yes/Probably yes, allocation concealment = No information, imbalance = No/compatible with chance → maps to "Some concerns" (per Table 4 row: Y/PY sequence, NI concealment, N/PN/NI imbalance → Some concerns).

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Participants and personnel could not be blinded (sunscreen use is inherently observable, and placebo sunscreen was deemed unethical), raising the possibility of trial-context-related deviations; however, there is no strong evidence that such deviations meaningfully biased the outcome, and appropriate analysis (as-randomized) was used.

**b. Rationale:**
- Blinding: "placebo sunscreen was considered unethical" — participants and those delivering the intervention (or self-administering it) were aware of allocation. This is an open-label comparison of daily vs. discretionary sunscreen use.
- Potential trial-context deviations: Because participants knew their allocation, participants in the discretionary group could theoretically have altered their behavior (e.g., increased personal sunscreen use due to awareness of the trial's purpose), diluting the intervention contrast. However, the article explicitly reports: "Reported sun exposure was similar between the daily and discretionary sunscreen groups during the trial... Use of sun-protection measures other than sunscreen was also similar" — suggesting no major imbalance in non-protocol behaviors that would bias the outcome.
- Adherence: While adherence differed ("77% of daily sunscreen users were applying sunscreen at least 3-4 days per week compared with 33% of discretionary users"), this is non-adherence relevant to the per-protocol effect, not the ITT effect. For the effect of assignment, this is expected and does not itself introduce bias, as it reflects real-world differences in behavior consistent with assignment.
- Analysis: "Data were analyzed according to treatment as randomly allocated" — this is consistent with an ITT approach, satisfying the appropriate analysis criterion.
- Given lack of blinding but no clear evidence that non-protocol interventions or trial-context-driven behavioral differences occurred and affected outcomes differentially, but also no explicit confirmation this was ruled out formally (e.g., no information on whether informed consent processes influenced control group behavior), a judgement of "Some concerns" is appropriate rather than "Low," since participants were aware of allocation and some behavioral influence cannot be fully excluded.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** A substantial proportion of participants (about one-third) had missing outcome data at one time point, and although the authors provide reassurance via sensitivity analyses, some concerns remain regarding the missingness mechanism.

**b. Rationale:**
- Missingness: "Good-quality replicas of the back of the hand were obtained from 817 participants at baseline and 673 in 1996... Of these, 604 contributed replicas in 1992 and 1996, 213 in 1992 only, and 69 in 1996 only." Thus of 886 eligible participants, only 604 (68%) had complete data — this is a substantial proportion of missing outcome data, not "nearly all."
- Reasons/associations with missingness: "Compared with those with 2 replicas, participants who contributed only 1 (n=282) were more likely to have severe photoaging of the neck and 2 to 5 total sunburns." This indicates that missingness was related to prognostic factors associated with photoaging severity, raising concern that missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome.
- However, the authors state: "Having a missing microtopography grade in 1996 was not associated with baseline microtopography grade," and "treatment allocation was not associated with missing grades." This suggests missingness was not differential by intervention group.
- The authors performed sensitivity analyses: complete-case analysis (n=604) and multiple imputation, both of which "replicated the results presented in Table 4," providing reassurance that the result is robust to missing data assumptions.
- Despite this reassurance, because missingness was substantial and related to some prognostic factors (baseline neck photoaging, sunburns) rather than clearly unrelated to the outcome, and because the missingness was not equal between the two follow-up time points, this is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low," even though sensitivity analyses were reassuring and balanced between arms.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome was assessed using a validated, appropriate method, ascertainment was identical across groups, and assessors were blinded to allocation with no plausible mechanism for differential influence.

**b. Rationale:**
- Appropriateness of measurement: The outcome was measured using "the Beagley and Gibson scale of microtopography grades," a previously validated instrument: "severity of dermal elastosis is predicted by standard grading of the microtopography of the skin surface, which provides a valid measure of skin photoaging." Intra- and inter-rater reliability was high: "weighted κ statistics of 0.81 and 0.86, respectively."
- Comparability of measurement across groups: Skin replicas were obtained identically for all participants ("trained personnel obtained skin surface replicas from the back of the left hand by using silicone-based impression material"), with no indication that ascertainment frequency or method differed by group.
- Blinding of outcome assessors: "Experienced assessors who were unaware of treatment allocation graded replicas" — explicit blinding of assessors.
- Since assessors were blinded and the outcome (objective microtopography grading of silicone replicas) is not a participant-reported or highly judgment-laden outcome likely to be influenced by unblinded participant self-report, risk of differential measurement bias is low. The article itself notes: "it seemed to be nondifferential with respect to treatment groups, particularly for microtopography, because assessors were blinded to allocations."

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The trial protocol was pre-specified and published prior to outcome ascertainment (registered trial with published design paper), and the outcome and analysis reported for photoaging match the pre-specified plan, with no evidence of selective outcome or analysis reporting.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial is registered (Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry: ACTRN12610000086066), and its design was published in advance: "Aims, methods, and results have been fully documented elsewhere" (Green et al., Control Clin Trials 1994), which pre-specified photoaging as an outcome and described the intended analytic approach.
- The primary outcome for this assessment, "change in photoaging" as measured by microtopography grading, is reported in full within the manuscript, with pre-specified statistical methods (GEE-based ordinal logistic regression) described in detail in the Methods section, matching what is reported in Results (Table 4).
- The results include both unadjusted and adjusted models (Model 1 and Model 2), but both are reported transparently and are consistent with each other, not selectively reported based on significance — the manuscript explicitly states "Results were not materially different from models without these covariates (model 1 in Table 4)."
- Sensitivity analyses (complete case and multiple imputation) are reported regardless of outcome, and results were consistent, indicating no evidence of selective reporting based on results.
- There is no indication of switching primary outcomes, selectively reporting only favorable time points or subscales, or omission of pre-specified analyses. Subgroup analyses were pre-specified based on baseline characteristics (age, sex, education, BMI, smoking, phenotype, sun exposure, skin cancer history) and are fully reported, including a non-significant sunscreen effect for some strata (Figure 2).
