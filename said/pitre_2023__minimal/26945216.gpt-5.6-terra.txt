1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study reports an appropriate method for sequence generation: “*computerized block randomization*.” Baseline pain intensity was broadly similar across groups (TENS 69.9, IFT 67.2, control 70.3 mm; *P* > 0.05), and there were no statistically significant differences in age or sex. These findings do not suggest an important failure of randomization.

   However, allocation concealment is insufficiently described. The report states that allocation was “*concealed in envelops*,” but does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed/tamper-proof, and opened only after irreversible enrolment. Under RoB 2, this does not provide enough assurance that allocation was adequately concealed. The significant imbalance in affected side (*P* = 0.015) is not, by itself, compelling evidence of a problem with randomization, particularly as the groups remained equal in size (35 each) and baseline pain was comparable. Nonetheless, incomplete reporting of concealment results in some concerns.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants were reportedly blinded to treatment allocation: “*The participants were blind to the allocation to treatment groups after randomization, and hence, the study was a single blind trial.*” The treating physiotherapist necessarily knew the allocated intervention because they delivered standard care plus either TENS, IFT, or no electrotherapy.

   Although the therapist was aware of allocation, the report provides no evidence that deviations from the protocol arose because of the trial context, such as differential administration of non-protocol co-interventions. The treatments were protocolized: all groups received the same standard-care package, and the TENS and IFT groups received their allocated electrical modality after standard care. All participants also received the same home programme.

   The analysis included all randomized participants: “*All completed the study and were entered into the analysis.*” The reported group analyses therefore appear consistent with assignment to the randomized groups. There is no indication of an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis. Consequently, there is low risk of bias for the assignment effect in this domain.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data appear to have been available for all randomized participants. The article states: “*There were 105 individuals who expressed interest in participating in the study. All completed the study and were entered into the analysis*,” and the CONSORT diagram shows 35 participants in each group through post-treatment measurement.

   Pain intensity results are presented for all three groups with *n* = 35 per group. There is no reported loss to follow-up, withdrawal, or exclusion from the pain analysis. Therefore, missing pain data could not plausibly have had an important effect on the estimated intervention effects.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Pain intensity was measured using a participant-reported 100-mm visual analogue scale: “*Pain intensity over the affected upper trapezius at rest was recorded using a 100-mm visual analog scale*.” Thus, the participant was the outcome assessor.

   Although the authors state that participants were blind to allocation, credible blinding is doubtful for this comparison. TENS, IFT, and control treatment were visibly and experientially different: TENS involved two electrodes, IFT involved four electrodes, and the control group received no electrical stimulation. In particular, participants allocated to standard care alone would be aware that they did not receive an electrical modality. The electrical stimulation procedures and sensations were also likely distinguishable between active groups.

   Pain is a subjective outcome, and reported pain—especially immediately after treatment—can readily be influenced by expectations and knowledge of the treatment received. The study itself acknowledges limitations from “*not using a placebo for the patients of the control group*.” In addition, the principal investigator both administered treatment and performed the study procedures, although this is more directly relevant to non-patient-reported outcomes. Because knowledge of treatment allocation was likely to influence self-reported pain, the risk of bias in measurement of pain is high.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article reports pain intensity at the stated measurement occasions—baseline, immediately after the first treatment, before the eighth treatment, and one week after the eighth treatment—and presents results for all three randomized groups. This is reassuring. The trial was reportedly registered with the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry.

   However, there is no published or provided statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the specific pain scale, all reported time points, calculation of change from baseline, and one-way ANOVA/Tukey analyses were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The available registration information as described in the article does not establish a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan for the reported numerical pain results. Since pain could have been assessed and analysed using multiple time points and approaches (for example, final values versus change scores), selective choice of a favourable result cannot be excluded. There is no direct evidence that selective reporting occurred, but the absence of a detailed pre-specified analysis plan leads to some concerns.
