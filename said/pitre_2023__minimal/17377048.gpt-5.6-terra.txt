1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The report states that “*patients were assigned to two groups at random, each with 20 patients*,” but gives no information on how the random allocation sequence was generated or whether allocation was concealed until enrolment. Thus, genuine random sequence generation and concealment cannot be verified. Baseline age was reported not to differ significantly between groups, and group sizes were initially equal (20 per group), which does not suggest a clear failure of randomization. However, absence of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns under RoB 2.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to the baseline imaging protocol (group A: 64 × 0.6-mm collimation; group B: 20 × 1.2-mm collimation). The analysis excluded four randomized participants because they had no detectable coronary calcium: “*Four patients had to be excluded from the study because no coronary calcium was detected on any of the reconstructions.*” Consequently, the reported analyses included 36 rather than all 40 randomized participants (18 per group rather than 20 per group).

   Excluding participants after randomization because their Agatston score was zero is not an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis for the effect of assignment. Zero is a valid outcome value, not an indication that the participant was ineligible or lacked outcome data. The exclusion represents 10% of randomized participants and may materially affect mean Agatston scores and comparisons between reconstruction strategies, particularly because scores were low or absent in these excluded individuals. The article does not present an analysis including all randomized participants. Therefore, there was potential for a substantial impact from failure to analyse participants in their assigned groups.

   Lack of blinding of personnel is not itself the principal concern for the assignment effect here, because the outcome was CT-derived and there is no evidence that trial-context deviations or co-interventions differentially affected coronary calcium. The high-risk judgement is driven by the inappropriate post-randomization exclusions.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Agatston scores appear to have been obtained for all participants who underwent the CT procedures. The four participants excluded from the reported analyses were excluded because their measured outcome was zero, not because their outcome data were unavailable: “*no coronary calcium was detected on any of the reconstructions.*” These are post-randomization analytic exclusions and are considered under deviations from intended intervention, rather than as missing outcome data in RoB 2.

   For the remaining participants, the study reports that five diastolic reconstructions and both reconstruction increments were created and scored for each patient. There is no indication of unmeasured scans, incomplete CT acquisition, loss to follow-up, or missing Agatston-score data. Thus, risk of bias specifically due to missing outcome data is low.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** Agatston scoring was based on a standard CT-derived definition: “*CAC was defined as the presence of more than two contiguous pixels with Hounsfield units greater than 130.*” The same scanner, reconstruction procedures, threshold, and workstation were used across groups, so there is no clear indication that outcome ascertainment systematically differed between assigned collimation groups.

   However, scoring was not wholly automated. After automated lesion identification, “*a radiologist with 4 years of experience … and a cardiologist with 11 years of experience … differentiated between calcium and noise by placing regions of interest around the CAC.*” The report does not state whether these assessors were blinded to the assigned group, reconstruction increment, or reconstruction interval. Because reconstruction characteristics may have been evident from the images and manual classification/ROI placement involves some judgement, awareness could potentially influence scores. There is no evidence that it did so, nor a clear reason to expect intentional differential assessment; therefore, some concerns rather than high risk of bias is appropriate.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** No protocol, statistical analysis plan, or sufficiently detailed prospectively registered analysis plan is available in the supplied materials to confirm that the Agatston-score analysis was specified before unblinded data were available. The trial assessed several potentially selectable results: Agatston and volumetric scores, two reconstruction increments, five reconstruction intervals (50%, 55%, 60%, 65%, and 70% of the R–R interval), coefficients of variation, and risk-category analyses.

   The article reports several of these analyses and provides results for both groups and both reconstruction increments. There is no direct evidence that the specific reported Agatston-score result was selected because of its direction, magnitude, or statistical significance. Nevertheless, because pre-specified outcome measurements, time points, and analytical methods cannot be verified against a dated analysis plan or usable registry information, selective reporting cannot be excluded. This results in some concerns rather than high risk of bias.
