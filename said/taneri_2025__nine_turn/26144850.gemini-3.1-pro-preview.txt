Outcome Name: cure rate at 2 years

**Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process**

**1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?**
* Response: Yes
* Reason: The authors state that participants were allocated "using random number tables." They generated 268 three-digit random numbers from a table to assign treatments based on whether the number was odd or even. 

**1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**
* Response: No
* Reason: The article explicitly states that the random numbers "were written in the patient registers in advance. Patients with even numbers were given PB-MDT and those with odd numbers were given ROM." Because the sequence was recorded in the register in advance and tied to a simple even/odd rule, the investigators enrolling the patients could foresee the next treatment allocation. Therefore, allocation was not concealed.

**1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**
* Response: Probably no
* Reason: Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 2 (age, sex, disease duration, number of patches, nerve involvement). The authors report that the groups were comparable, with no statistically significant differences in mean age (P = 0.059) or mean duration of disease (P = 0.29). While there are slight numeric variations, the baseline differences do not strongly suggest a failure of the randomization process itself, despite the lack of allocation concealment.

**Domain 1 Risk of Bias Judgment:** High risk of bias
* Reason: Although the sequence was generated randomly, the allocation sequence was written in advance in the patient register, meaning allocation concealment was completely absent. In an unblinded trial, this lack of concealment creates a high risk of selection bias, as recruiters could easily anticipate the allocation of the next participant.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 2 (Effect of assignment to intervention): High**

**3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**
* Response: Yes
* Reason: The reported reasons for dropping out explicitly included participants who "felt cured" (5 patients), providing direct evidence that their decision to leave the study depended on their perceived outcome status. Furthermore, when the authors later traced some of the defaulters, they found that while most were cured, a proportion still had active disease (e.g., 4 out of 23 traced in the PB-MDT group), confirming that the missing data directly contained meaningful variability regarding the true outcome (cure vs. no cure).

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: High**
* Reason: Nearly 24% of the randomized participants were missing from the 2-year cure rate analysis. No statistical corrections or sensitivity analyses were performed. Furthermore, the documented reasons for defaulting (such as feeling cured) make it highly likely that the missingness depended on the true value of the outcome, leading to a high risk of attrition bias.
