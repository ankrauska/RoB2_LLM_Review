1. **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?**

   • **Response: Yes**  
   • **Reason:** The article explicitly reports that women “were assigned randomly via random number tables” to supportive counselling, stress management, or alcohol-focused couples therapy. Random number tables constitute a random component in sequence generation.

2. **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**

   • **Response: No information**  
   • **Reason:** Although the study states that random number tables were used, it provides no information about who generated or held the allocation sequence, who enrolled participants, when assignment occurred in relation to enrolment, or any allocation-concealment mechanism (e.g., central randomization or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes). Therefore, concealment cannot be established from the report.

3. **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**

   • **Response: Probably no**  
   • **Reason:** The authors report no statistically significant pre-treatment differences across conditions in demographic characteristics (age, years married, and number of children), women’s GHQ and RSS scores, reported male drinking measures, or relationship measures (DAS, MSI, and CTS). For the outcome domain, baseline burden/distress measures (RSS and GHQ) were reported as not significantly different. However, the article does not provide baseline values by randomized group for all characteristics, and final analysed group sizes differed (supportive counselling *n* = 17, stress management *n* = 14, AFCT *n* = 13), apparently reflecting attrition/missing data rather than necessarily unequal random allocation. No clear baseline imbalance suggesting a flawed randomization process is reported.

**Domain 1 overall risk-of-bias judgement: Some concerns**

• **Justification:** Sequence generation was clearly random, but allocation concealment was not reported. As concealment is essential to prevent selection bias and there is insufficient information to determine whether enrolment personnel could foresee assignments, the domain is judged as having **some concerns**, despite no reported baseline imbalances indicating a randomization problem.

Outcome Name: women’s burden and distress

1. **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** Sixty-one women were randomized. Forty-seven completed treatment, and 44 provided assessment data at 6-month follow-up. Thus, outcome data at follow-up were available for approximately 72% (44/61) of randomized participants, meaning 28% had missing follow-up outcome data. This is substantially below the approximate 95% level generally regarded as “nearly all” for a continuous outcome. The article also indicates that not all participants provided every outcome measure at every assessment time.

2. **3.2 If No/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** The authors did not report an analysis method intended to address possible bias from missing outcome data, such as a model or weighting approach justified under appropriate missing-data assumptions. They also did not report sensitivity analyses examining whether results were robust to plausible assumptions about the missing burden/distress outcomes. The repeated-measures ANOVAs were based on participants with available data.

3. **3.3 If No/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** The article reports that most women who dropped out did so because they had left their partners soon after beginning the programme, and that these women had been “very unhappy” in their relationships. All women who dropped out declined further assessment. Leaving a severely alcohol-affected and distressed relationship, and declining subsequent assessment, could be related to women’s post-randomization burden and psychological distress—the outcomes assessed by the RSS and GHQ.

4. **3.4 If Yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** Missing outcome data were substantial, and the reported reason for dropout—leaving an extremely unhappy relationship—is closely connected to the women’s burden and psychological distress. The authors themselves note that it would have been helpful to know whether women who ended their relationships had better mental health and quality of life after doing so, but these data were unavailable because all dropouts declined assessment. Thus, the unobserved burden/distress outcomes may plausibly have differed importantly from those of women retained in follow-up.

**Domain 3 overall risk-of-bias judgement: High**

• **Justification:** Follow-up burden/distress data were missing for 17 of 61 randomized women (28%). No reported analysis or sensitivity analysis addressed potential bias arising from missing outcome data. Dropout was commonly related to relationship separation following severe relationship unhappiness, a circumstance likely related to the women’s burden and distress outcomes.

Outcome Name: women’s burden and distress

1. **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** Women’s burden was measured using the Relative Stress Scale (RSS), a 15-item scale assessing personal distress, life upset, and negative feelings. Psychological distress was measured using the 28-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ). The article reports satisfactory psychometric properties for both measures: RSS test–retest reliability of *r* = 0.85, and GHQ internal reliability of Cronbach’s α = 0.93 with established validity as a screening measure for common psychological disorders. Nothing reported indicates that these instruments were insensitive to plausible treatment effects or otherwise inappropriate for this outcome.

2. **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** The RSS and GHQ were administered to women in all three groups at the same scheduled time points: pre-treatment, post-treatment, and 6-month follow-up. The article does not report different measurement instruments, thresholds, assessment schedules, or outcome-ascertainment procedures by intervention group.

3. **4.3 If No/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**

   • **Response: Yes**  
   • **Reason:** The burden and distress outcomes were self-reported by the participating women; therefore, the participants were the outcome assessors. Because this was an open-label psychotherapy trial with visibly different intervention content, women were aware of their assigned treatment condition. No participant blinding is reported.

4. **4.4 If Yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

   • **Response: Yes**  
   • **Reason:** RSS and GHQ outcomes require subjective self-assessment of burden, distress, and psychological symptoms. Participants’ expectations, perceived credibility of the treatment, satisfaction with their therapist, or awareness that they received a structured behavioural intervention rather than supportive counselling could have influenced how they rated these outcomes.

5. **4.5 If Yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** The interventions were overtly different and the principal outcomes were subjective, participant-reported measures of stress and psychological symptoms. The study was specifically designed to compare structured behavioural treatments against a supportive-counselling contact control, making differential expectations or perceived treatment credibility plausible. The report does not describe blinding of outcome assessment or measures to minimize expectancy-related influence. However, it does not provide direct evidence that outcome ratings were influenced by knowledge of treatment, so “Probably yes,” rather than “Yes,” is appropriate.

**Domain 4 overall risk-of-bias judgement: High**

• **Justification:** Valid and comparable outcome instruments were used across randomized groups. Nevertheless, the outcomes were subjective self-reports completed by unblinded participants receiving clearly distinguishable psychological interventions. Knowledge of assigned treatment could, and probably did, influence assessment of women’s perceived burden and psychological distress.
