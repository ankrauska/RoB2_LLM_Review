### Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?**

- **Response: Yes**

No reported imbalance suggests a problem with randomization.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Low risk of bias**

- **Justification:** The allocation sequence was computer-generated, allocation was concealed until assignment, and the reported baseline data do not suggest failure of the randomization process.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The trial randomized 555 participants (276 intervention; 279 control), but the reported analysed sample comprised 519 participants (251 intervention; 268 control). Thus, 36/555 (6.5%) randomized participants were lost to follow-up and were not included in the analysed sample. More importantly, BSI outcome data at the follow-up assessments were available for fewer participants: at immediate post-intervention (T2), 224/276 (81.2%) intervention participants and 234/279 (83.9%) control participants had BSI Global Severity Index data; at 6-month follow-up (T3), 220/276 (79.7%) and 228/279 (81.7%), respectively, had data. These proportions are below the approximately 95% level generally regarded as “nearly all” for a continuous outcome.

**3.2. If no/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The authors used mixed-effects random regression, noting that this approach accommodates missing observations. However, the report does not present an analysis specifically correcting for potential bias due to missing outcome data, such as an analysis based on justified assumptions about missingness, inverse-probability weighting, or multiple imputation with appropriate auxiliary variables. No sensitivity analyses are reported to assess whether the findings were robust under plausible assumptions that missing BSI data were related to unobserved symptom severity. The mixed-model approach alone does not demonstrate that missingness did not bias the result.

**3.3. If no/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The article reports that loss to follow-up occurred for reasons including “death or ill health,” moving away, and formal study withdrawal. Ill health and withdrawal could be related to participants’ psychiatric symptom severity, the BSI outcome. In addition, this population had severe and persistent mental illness, and symptom status could plausibly influence continued participation in telephone follow-up assessments. Therefore, missing BSI outcome data could depend on the true underlying level of psychiatric symptoms.

**3.4. If yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** Missingness was appreciable at both post-randomization assessments (approximately 16–19% of randomized participants did not provide BSI data at T2 or T3). Attrition was also numerically higher in the intervention group: 25/276 (9.1%) intervention participants were lost to follow-up compared with 11/279 (3.9%) controls. The authors report “death or ill health” among reasons for missingness, and such reasons can be associated with worse health status and potentially greater psychiatric symptoms. The report does not provide outcome-related comparisons between those with versus without BSI follow-up data, nor sensitivity analyses addressing informative missingness.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: **High risk of bias**

- **Justification:** BSI data were unavailable for a substantial proportion of randomized participants at post-intervention and 6-month follow-up. Although mixed-effects modelling was used, no evidence or sensitivity analysis demonstrates that missing data did not bias the intervention-effect estimate. Given the reported reasons for loss to follow-up, including ill health, and the greater loss to follow-up in the intervention group, it is likely that missingness could have been related to true psychiatric symptom severity.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Psychiatric symptom severity was measured using the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), a 53-item self-report instrument assessing symptoms experienced during the previous week. The authors state that the BSI has high concordance with clinician symptom assessment and is frequently used to assess clinical improvement and treatment outcomes in mental-health randomized trials. No information suggests that the instrument lacked validity or sensitivity to detect plausible effects of WRAP on psychiatric symptoms.

**4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** BSI outcomes were collected using structured telephone interviews administered by University of Illinois at Chicago Survey Research Laboratory interviewers at the same scheduled study time points for both groups: baseline, 6 weeks after the intervention period, and 6-month follow-up. The same self-report BSI instrument was used in both groups. There is no report of differential outcome-measurement methods, thresholds, or timing between the intervention and control groups.

**4.3. If no/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** Although the telephone interviewers were intended to be blinded to condition, the outcome was participant-reported. For a self-reported BSI outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor. Participants knew whether they attended WRAP or were assigned to the waiting-list/usual-care condition. The authors also report that participant disclosure of study condition occurred in 4% of T2 and T3 interviewer assessments, but participant awareness applies to all unblinded participants.

**4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The BSI is a subjective self-report measure of how much participants were bothered by psychiatric symptoms. Knowledge of receiving an 8-week peer-led recovery and symptom self-management intervention, or knowledge of being allocated to a wait-list condition, could influence how participants perceived, recalled, or reported their symptoms.

**4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The study provides no direct evidence that participants intentionally or systematically reported BSI symptoms differently because of their assigned condition. Interviewers were blinded, and condition disclosure to them was reported in only 4% of T2 and T3 interviews. While the self-reported and unblinded nature of the outcome means influence is possible, the article does not provide evidence establishing that differential reporting due to knowledge of assignment was likely.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: **Some concerns**

- **Justification:** The BSI appears to be an appropriate outcome measure, and the procedures for administering it were the same in both groups. However, psychiatric symptom severity was self-reported by participants who necessarily knew their assigned intervention. Knowledge of allocation could influence subjective symptom reporting, although the report does not establish that such influence was likely.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1. Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available for analysis?**

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The article reports that the study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01024569) and states that “all outcomes, hypotheses, and statistical analyses presented here were prespecified at the time the proposal was submitted to the federal government for funding consideration.” The article specified psychiatric symptom reduction as the primary outcome and described use of intent-to-treat mixed-effects random-regression analysis.  
  Although the ClinicalTrials.gov record was first submitted and posted after study completion, the article provides a statement that the outcomes, hypotheses, and analyses were prespecified before the trial. The complete pre-specified statistical analysis plan is not available in the supplied material; therefore, “Probably yes,” rather than “Yes,” is appropriate.

**5.2. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?**

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The article identified psychiatric symptom severity as the primary outcome and reported results for both specified BSI indices: the Global Severity Index and the Positive Symptom Total. The authors described these measures in the Methods section before presenting results, and both were reported in the outcome table and in the mixed-effects regression table. There is no evidence that the BSI result was selected from a larger set of unreported eligible BSI symptom-severity measures on the basis of statistical significance or direction of findings.

**5.3. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?**

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The Methods section prespecified an intent-to-treat multivariable longitudinal mixed-effects random-intercepts regression model, including study site as a fixed effect and study condition, time, and condition-by-time interaction as model variables. The reported primary BSI findings were generated using this stated analysis. The authors also report a sensitivity reanalysis controlling for time-varying exposure to peer-led support groups and state that results did not differ substantially. There is no evidence that only a favourable analysis was selected for reporting from multiple analyses.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 5: **Low risk of bias**

- **Justification:** The article states that outcomes, hypotheses, and statistical analyses were prespecified, and the published report presents results for both named BSI symptom indices using the described intention-to-treat mixed-effects analysis. The available information does not indicate selective choice of either the BSI measurement or analysis based on the results.
