# Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

## 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

**Response: Yes**

**Reason:** The article explicitly states "using a computer-generated random sequence concealed in opaque envelopes, with stratification for operator and center." Further detail confirms: "subjects were allocated to either the study (SmartClip) group or control (Victory) group using a block randomization determined by a computer-generated random number table, with stratification for operator and center." This clearly demonstrates a random component was used in sequence generation (computer-generated random number table), meeting the criteria for "Yes."

## 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

**Response: Yes**

**Reason:** The article states: "The appliance type was placed in a sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelope that was opened after the patient was accepted onto the trial." This meets the described criteria for appropriate envelope use (opaque, sequentially numbered, sealed, opened only after irreversible assignment). Additionally, the article notes "The generator of the randomization did not participate in patient allocation," further supporting adequate concealment, as this separates the sequence generator from the enrollment/allocation process, reducing risk that enrolling investigators could foresee upcoming allocations.

## 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

**Response: Probably yes**

**Reason:** Table 2 reveals notable imbalances in malocclusion classification between groups, a key prognostic factor directly relevant to treatment duration (the primary outcome being assessed here). Specifically:
- Class I: 12 (SmartClip) vs 23 (Victory) — nearly double in Victory group
- Class II division 1: 37 (SmartClip) vs 31 (Victory)
- Class II division 2: 9 (SmartClip) vs 4 (Victory) — more than double in SmartClip group
- Class III: 8 (SmartClip) vs 13 (Victory)

Malocclusion severity/type is a well-established prognostic factor that influences treatment complexity and duration (e.g., Class II cases, particularly division 2, often require more complex/prolonged treatment than Class I). The imbalance is meaningful in magnitude relative to the group sizes (65-70 per group), and no statistical comparison or discussion of these baseline differences is provided by the authors to reassure readers this is compatible with chance. Additionally, age range differed markedly (12-29 years in SmartClip vs 10-56 years in Victory), suggesting some heterogeneity in case mix that was not explicitly addressed. Without formal testing or discussion acknowledging/adjusting for this imbalance, and given its direct relevance to the outcome of treatment duration, this raises reasonable concern that randomization may not have achieved adequate prognostic balance, though it stops short of definitive evidence of a flawed randomization *process* (e.g., no evidence of tampering or non-random allocation mechanics). Hence "Probably yes" rather than a definitive "Yes."

## Overall Domain 1 Judgement: **Some concerns**

**Justification:** While the randomization sequence generation (1.1) and allocation concealment (1.2) were both adequately described and judged as low risk ("Yes" for both), the observed baseline imbalance in malocclusion classification (1.3) — a factor plausibly linked to treatment duration — was not addressed statistically or narratively by the authors. Per the RoB 2 algorithm, when 1.1 and 1.2 are answered "Yes" but 1.3 is answered "Yes" or "Probably yes," the domain-level judgment is elevated from "Low risk" to "Some concerns," reflecting the possibility that baseline imbalance may have introduced bias into the estimate of the treatment duration outcome, even though the sequence generation and concealment mechanisms themselves were sound.

# Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

## 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

**Response: No**

**Reason:** The outcome (total treatment duration) was measured as "total duration of treatment (months) from fixed appliance placement to debond" — an objective, calendar-based measurement using documented clinical dates. This method is directly relevant and sensitive to detecting plausible intervention effects (differences in treatment time between bracket systems), and there is no indication of poor validity in this measurement approach.

## 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

**Response: No**

**Reason:** Both groups were treated according to a standardized protocol with the same measurement approach (recording of placement date to debond date) applied uniformly by the same two operators across both intervention arms. There is no evidence that measurement methods, timing, or thresholds for recording treatment duration differed systematically between the SmartClip and Victory groups.

## 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

**Response: Yes**

**Reason:** The article states: "it was not possible to blind the clinician or patient to the type of bracket system being used." Critically, the *treating clinicians* were the ones who determined when treatment was complete and debonding should occur — this decision inherently involves clinical judgment (e.g., assessing whether treatment goals/occlusal outcomes had been achieved) and was made by unblinded operators. While the article notes "data analysis was carried out at the end of the study with examiner blinding," this blinding applied only to the *statistical analysis* stage, not to the real-time clinical decision of when to debond, which is the actual determinant of the "treatment duration" outcome. Therefore, the relevant outcome assessors (treating clinicians deciding on debond timing) were aware of the intervention received.

## 4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

**Response: Yes**

**Reason:** The decision of when a patient's treatment is "complete" and ready for debond is not a purely objective, mechanical measurement (unlike, e.g., mortality) — it involves clinical judgment regarding occlusal outcome and case finish. This type of outcome assessment (an intervention-provider decision outcome) is susceptible to influence from the assessor's beliefs or expectations about the bracket system being used. Given that clinicians were unblinded and had received manufacturer-sponsored training specifically for the SmartClip system (raising potential expectation bias in either direction), there is plausible opportunity for knowledge of intervention to have influenced the timing/threshold used to determine treatment completion.

## 4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

**Response: Probably no**

**Reason:** While the *potential* for influence exists (per 4.4), there is no explicit evidence in the article suggesting strong directional beliefs (e.g., overt statements of expected superiority/inferiority of one system) that would have systematically biased debond-timing decisions in a particular direction. The trial appears to have been conducted with genuine clinical equipoise, and results showed no significant difference between groups, which is not suggestive of one-directional assessment bias. However, because clinicians were unblinded and the outcome involves subjective judgment, this cannot be fully ruled out, hence "Probably no" (indicating the outcome falls into "Some concerns" territory) rather than a confident "No."

---

## Overall Domain 4 Judgement: **Some concerns**

**Justification:** The measurement method itself was appropriate and applied consistently across groups (4.1 = No, 4.2 = No). However, the outcome assessors (treating clinicians who determined debond timing, thus defining "treatment duration") were unblinded to intervention assignment (4.3 = Yes), and this outcome involves an element of clinical judgment susceptible to influence by knowledge of the intervention (4.4 = Yes). Although there is no explicit evidence that assessment was actually influenced by this awareness in a directionally biased manner (4.5 = Probably no), the inherent judgment-based nature of determining "treatment completion" combined with unblinded assessment prevents a "Low risk" rating.
