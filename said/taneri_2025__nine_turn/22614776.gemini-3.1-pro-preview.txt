Based on the provided article and clinical trial protocol, here is a summary of the key information for your systematic review:

### **Study Overview**
*   **Title:** Brief Motivational Interviewing Intervention for Peer Violence and Alcohol Use in Teens: One-Year Follow-up
*   **ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier:** NCT00251212
*   **Study Design:** Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT)
*   **Setting:** Emergency Department (ED) at a level I trauma center (Hurley Medical Center, Flint, Michigan).
*   **Participants:** 726 adolescents (ages 14–18) presenting to the ED who screened positive for past-year aggressive behaviors and alcohol consumption.
*   **Interventions:** 
    *   *Therapist-administered brief intervention (TBI):* A 45-minute adapted motivational interviewing and skills training session delivered by a therapist with computer assistance.
    *   *Computer-based brief intervention (CBI):* A stand-alone interactive computerized motivational interviewing and skills training program.
    *   *Control:* A trifold brochure with community resources.

### **Key Findings at 12-Month Follow-Up**
*   **Violence Outcomes:**
    *   The TBI group showed significant reductions in severe peer aggression ($P < .01$) and peer victimization ($P < .05$) compared to the control group.
    *   The TBI group had a 43% reduction in severe peer aggression and a 23% reduction in peer victimization.
    *   No significant differences were observed between the CBI group and the control group for any violence variables at 12 months.
*   **Alcohol Outcomes:**
    *   Neither the TBI nor the CBI showed significant differences compared to the control group regarding alcohol-related variables (alcohol misuse, binge drinking, alcohol-related consequences) at 12 months.
    *   *Note:* Previous findings showed reductions in alcohol consequences at 6 months, but these effects were not sustained at the 1-year mark.

### **Conclusions**
A brief intervention delivered by a therapist (with computer assistance) in the ED setting effectively sustains reductions in peer aggression and peer victimization among at-risk adolescents up to 12 months post-visit. However, the intervention did not produce sustained reductions in alcohol use or consequences at the 12-month mark. The study highlights the feasibility and potential of standardizing ED-based violence prevention through computer-assisted therapist interventions.

1. 3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?
   • Response: Probably no
   • Reason: The article reports that 607 out of 726 (83.6%) participants completed the 12-month follow-up. A loss of 16.4% of the participants means data were not available for "nearly all" participants, particularly for a behavioral trial where missingness might relate to the outcome.

2. 3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?
   • Response: No
   • Reason: While the authors used Generalized Estimating Equations (GEEs) which use all available data, and conducted an intent-to-treat analysis, they explicitly acknowledge in their limitations that "it is always possible that those lost to follow-up may have biased results." There is no report of sensitivity analyses assessing whether the results are robust to plausible assumptions about the missing data.

3. 3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?
   • Response: Probably yes
   • Reason: The study evaluates severe aggressive behavior and violence consequences among urban adolescents. It is plausible that participants with higher ongoing rates of peer aggression or substance misuse might be harder to contact for follow-up or more likely to drop out of the study. 

4. 3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?
   • Response: Probably no
   • Reason: Although missingness *could* depend on the true value, there is no direct evidence provided (e.g., differential dropout rates between groups or reported reasons for attrition) indicating that it *likely* did in a way that disproportionately affected one group. The follow-up rate was relatively high (84%) and appears generally balanced across the three intervention arms based on the numbers in Table 1 (approximately 200 per group evaluated at 12 months from an initial allocation of roughly 242 per group).
