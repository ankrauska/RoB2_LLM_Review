1. 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?
   • Response: No information
   • Reason: The article states that "randomization was carried out confidentially by the clinic administration, and arranged so that 25 test persons would be treated with the active drug and 24 with a placebo." However, it provides no specific details on the method or random element used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, coin tossing). Following the guidelines, when the only information is a statement that the study is randomized, the response should be 'No information'.

2. 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?
   • Response: Probably yes
   • Reason: The authors report that the randomization was carried out "confidentially by the clinic administration," indicating that an external administrative unit held the sequence rather than the enrolling investigators. Additionally, the intervention was administered using "numbered boxes," which points toward an appropriate method for concealing the allocation sequence until irreversible assignment.

3. 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?
   • Response: No information
   • Reason: The study protocol randomized 49 participants (25 to topiramate, 24 to placebo). However, the article states that 6 subjects from the placebo group dropped out and their data were not analyzed. Table 1 ("Basic Data") reports the baseline characteristics solely for the 43 women who completed the study (n=25 for topiramate and n=18 for placebo). According to the evaluation guidelines, "No information" should be selected when studies report only the baseline characteristics of participants included in the final analysis, as the true baseline data for all randomized participants is missing.

   • Reason: The article states that 49 patients were initially randomized (25 to the topiramate group and 24 to the placebo group). This means outcome data were only available for 43/49 (87.8%) of participants overall, and missing for 25% (6/24) of the placebo group, which does not meet the threshold for "nearly all."

2. 3.2. Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?
   • Response: No
   • Reason: There is no mention of sensitivity analyses or advanced imputation methods to correct for bias due to missing data. The authors explicitly state that the data for the six dropouts "were not further analyzed."

3. 3.3. Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?
   • Response: Probably yes
   • Reason: The six participants dropped out because they "failed to appear for more than one evaluation." Without further documented, outcome-unrelated reasons for their absence (e.g., moving away), it is highly plausible that withdrawal from a weight-loss intervention study could be related to a lack of perceived efficacy (i.e., continuing to gain weight or failing to lose weight), which is directly tied to the true value of the outcome.

4. 3.4. Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?
   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: The guidelines state that a difference between intervention groups in the proportions of missing outcome data is a strong reason to answer 'Yes'. In this study, 100% of the missing data (6 dropouts) occurred in the placebo group, while the topiramate group had 0 dropouts. This severe imbalance strongly suggests that missingness is linked to the assigned intervention and likely to its true effect/outcome value.

    *(Reasoning: All missing data/dropouts occurred in the placebo group [6 out of 24]. Excluding these poor-performing individuals from the placebo group's final analysis artificially improves the average outcome of the placebo group. This makes the placebo group appear more effective than it truly is, thereby shrinking the observed difference between the topiramate group and the placebo group, biasing the effect estimate towards the null.)*
