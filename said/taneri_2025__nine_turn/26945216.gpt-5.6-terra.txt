**Outcome Name: Pain intensity**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** The article explicitly reports that participants were allocated using **“computerized block randomization.”** Computer-generated randomization includes a random component and is therefore considered a random allocation sequence.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The authors state that the **“allocation group [was] concealed in envelopes.”** This supports that an envelope-based allocation-concealment method was intended. However, the report does not specify whether envelopes were opaque, sequentially numbered, sealed/tamper-proof, or opened only after irreversible enrolment. Therefore, definite assurance of adequate concealment is not available, but the reported use of concealed envelopes supports a “Probably yes” response.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Group sizes were equal as intended (35 participants in each group). Baseline pain intensity did not differ statistically between groups (TENS 69.9, IFT 67.2, control 70.3 mm; *P* > 0.05), and baseline cervical range-of-motion measures were also reported as not significantly different. Age and sex distributions were not significantly different. Although affected side differed statistically between groups (*P* = 0.015), this was one statistically significant difference among several baseline comparisons and does not by itself indicate a randomization failure, particularly because baseline pain—the primary outcome—was comparable across groups.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

- **Judgement:** **Low risk of bias**
- **Reason:** The allocation sequence was generated using computerized block randomization; allocation concealment was reported through concealed envelopes, albeit without full procedural detail; and there is no convincing evidence from baseline group sizes or baseline pain intensity that the randomization process was compromised.

**Outcome Name: Pain intensity**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Pain intensity at rest over the affected upper trapezius was measured using a 100-mm visual analog scale (VAS), anchored by “no pain at all” and “worst pain imaginable.” This is an appropriate and commonly used method for measuring self-reported pain intensity. The article also cites evidence for VAS reliability. No information indicates that the measure lacked validity or sensitivity for detecting relevant changes in pain.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Pain was measured with the same 100-mm VAS in all three randomized groups, at the same planned time points: baseline, immediately after the first treatment, before the eighth treatment, and 1 week after the eighth treatment. The report provides no indication that differing measurement methods, thresholds, or assessment schedules were used across groups.

### 4.3. If no/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** Pain intensity is a participant-reported outcome; therefore, participants are the outcome assessors for this outcome. Although the authors state that participants were blinded to allocation after randomization, no sham TENS or sham IFT was used. Participants allocated to TENS or IFT received an additional 20-minute, perceptible electrical-stimulation treatment, whereas control participants did not. Thus, participants were likely aware of whether they received electrical stimulation in addition to standard care. In addition, the principal investigator, who administered the interventions and collected all measurements, was aware of group allocation.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** Pain intensity was self-reported on a VAS and is inherently subjective. Participants’ knowledge or likely recognition of receiving an additional active electrical-stimulation treatment, compared with standard care alone, could influence their pain ratings through expectations, placebo effects, reporting behaviour, or perceived treatment benefit.

### 4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The outcome was subjective and was assessed immediately after treatment as well as during short-term follow-up. The trial did not use sham stimulation for the control group, despite the additional treatment time and sensory experience of TENS or IFT. Further, the investigator both delivered the allocated treatments and collected outcome measurements. These features make influence of knowledge of intervention on self-reported pain ratings likely.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

- **Judgement:** **High risk of bias**  
- **Reason:** The pain measure itself was appropriate and used consistently across groups. However, pain was a subjective participant-reported outcome, participants were likely able to identify whether they received an electrical-stimulation treatment rather than standard care alone, no sham comparator was used, and the unblinded treating investigator collected outcome data. It is likely that knowledge of intervention received influenced pain assessment.
