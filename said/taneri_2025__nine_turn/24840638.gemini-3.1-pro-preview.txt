**Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process**

**1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?**
* Response: Probably yes
* Reason: The authors state that "participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups" and that "Random assignment... was accomplished by randomly assigning 2 participants to the percentile group, 2 to the fixed criterion group and 1 to the random incentives group from each group of 5 participants completing intake." The sequence was stratified by intent to use medication and order of study entry. While the specific mechanism of random sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated random numbers) is not explicitly detailed, the explicit claim of a random assignment process supports this judgment.

**1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**
* Response: No information
* Reason: The article does not provide any information regarding whether the allocation sequence was concealed (e.g., through centralized assignment, sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes) from the investigators or personnel enrolling the participants before the actual assignment took place.

**1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**
* Response: Probably no
* Reason: Table 1 and the text report that participants across the three groups were statistically similar on almost all intake measures. The only exception was a higher proportion of participants with Vocational/Technical/Community College education in the percentile group compared to the fixed criterion group. Given the large number of baseline characteristics tested, a single significant difference is statistically expected by chance and does not strongly suggest a systemic failure or manipulation of the randomization process.

Outcome Name: smoking abstinence by breath CO sample < 3 ppm

**Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data**

**3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**
• Response: Probably no
• Reason: The study protocol required participants to submit a breath CO sample every workday for 60 visits. The authors report that the average number of delivered breath CO samples was 42, 40, and 52 out of 60 for the percentile, fixed criterion, and random incentive groups, respectively. This indicates a substantial amount of missing outcome data (missed visits) across the 60-visit treatment phase for the participants. 

**3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**
• Response: No
• Reason: The authors do not report any sensitivity analyses or specific statistical methods designed to correct for potential bias introduced by the missing data (e.g., multiple imputation). Missed visits are simply treated as non-abstinent for incentive purposes, but the effect of missingness on the overall longitudinal analysis of abstinence is not formally evaluated for bias.

**3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**
• Response: Yes
• Reason: In smoking cessation and substance use trials, missing a clinic visit is strongly associated with relapse or continued substance use. A participant who has smoked and knows they will fail the breath CO test (and thus not receive an escalating monetary incentive) is highly likely to skip the visit. Therefore, the missingness of the CO sample heavily depends on its true value (smoking status).

**3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**
• Response: Probably yes
• Reason: As stated above, missing visits in contingency management trials are highly indicative of smoking. Furthermore, the study reports a statistically significant difference in the number of delivered breath CO samples between the groups (H = 8.57, df (2), p < 0.02). The combination of differential missingness (attendance) between groups and the inherent nature of substance use trials strongly suggests that missingness depended on the true smoking status of the participants.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3:** High
(Reason: There is a substantial amount of missing data (missed visits), no analyses were conducted to show results were not biased by this, missingness is highly likely to depend on the true outcome (smoking status), and the rate of missing data differed significantly between the intervention groups.)

**Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome**

**4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**
• Response: No
• Reason: The method of measuring smoking abstinence was an objective biological marker (breath carbon monoxide using a Vitalograph CO monitor) with a predefined threshold of < 3 ppm. This is a well-established and highly appropriate method for verifying smoking status in cessation trials, which the authors explicitly justified using prior literature.

**4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**
• Response: No
• Reason: All participants, regardless of their assigned group, followed the exact same daily procedure to measure their breath CO: they were expected to report each workday, hold their breath for 20 seconds, and exhale over 20 seconds into the same type of disposable mouthpiece on the CO monitor.

**4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**
• Response: Yes
• Reason: The trial was unblinded. The personnel collecting the breath CO samples had to be aware of the assigned group to immediately calculate and dispense the appropriate monetary incentive according to the specific schedule for each participant.

**4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**
• Response: Probably no
• Reason: The outcome is measured by an objective machine (a Vitalograph CO monitor) that provides a specific, quantifiable numeric reading (peak breath CO in parts per million). Because the outcome does not rely on observer judgment, participant self-report, or subjective interpretation, the assessors' knowledge of the intervention could not reasonably influence the objective reading provided by the monitor.

**4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**
• Response: Not applicable
• Reason: Answered "Probably no" to 4.4.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4:** Low
(Reason: Although the outcome assessors were aware of the assigned interventions, the outcome was an objective, machine-generated biological measurement (breath CO) that is highly unlikely to be influenced by the assessor's knowledge.)
