**Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome**

**4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**
- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The outcome was measured using the Y-BOCS, a validated, widely-used, standardized instrument for assessing OCD severity (Goodman et al., 1989, cited in the article's Introduction). Relapse was defined operationally as "an increase in the Y-BOCS total score of ≥5 points from the time of randomisation, or an unsatisfactory treatment effect (lack of efficacy) as judged by the investigator." This is an appropriate, clinically validated, and pre-specified method sensitive to detecting plausible intervention effects, consistent with methods used in comparable OCD relapse-prevention trials (e.g., Koran et al., 2002; Hollander et al., 2003b).

**4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**
- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** Both intervention groups followed the identical assessment schedule: "efficacy and tolerability parameters were assessed 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 weeks after randomisation and then every 4 weeks until their last dose of double-blind treatment (Week 24)." There is no evidence of differential visit frequency, differential thresholds, or differential ascertainment methods between the escitalopram and placebo arms.

**4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**
- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** Although the trial was described as double-blind with identical-appearing tablets, and "Patient ratings were assessed by the same investigator at each visit, whenever possible" (i.e., the treating clinician/investigator was also the outcome rater), the article reports a statistically significant excess of dizziness and nausea in the placebo group during the taper/early double-blind period. This differential, clinically recognizable adverse-event signature (consistent with SSRI discontinuation) could have provided cues to investigator-raters regarding treatment assignment, partially compromising blinding for at least some participants, consistent with the reasoning applied in Domain 2.

**4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**
- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The Y-BOCS is an observer/clinician-rated instrument that, despite being structured, involves clinical judgement in scoring symptom severity. Additionally, the relapse definition explicitly included a subjective component: "unsatisfactory treatment effect (lack of efficacy) as judged by the investigator." This judgement-based criterion could plausibly be influenced by an assessor's awareness or suspicion of treatment assignment.

**4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**
- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Several facts support that any such influence, if present, was unlikely to have materially affected the result: (1) The article explicitly states that "the Y-BOCS relapse criterion accounted for almost all of the relapses (116 out of 119 patients, 97%)," meaning the subjective "lack of efficacy" judgment alone accounted for only 3/119 relapses — a negligible proportion. (2) The authors conducted sensitivity analyses censoring relapses occurring in the first 7 and 14 days post-randomization specifically "to exclude potentially confounding discontinuation effects," and reported that "escitalopram was significantly superior to placebo" was maintained under these more conservative conditions. (3) Post-hoc analysis using only the objective Y-BOCS ≥5-point criterion (removing the subjective "lack of efficacy" component entirely) yielded nearly identical results (50% vs. 23% relapse, HR=2.73, p<0.001) compared to the primary combined criterion (52% vs. 23%, HR=2.74). This convergence across objective-only and combined criteria strongly suggests that subjective assessor judgement did not meaningfully drive the observed treatment effect.

**Justification:** While the outcome measure itself (Y-BOCS) was appropriate and applied identically across groups, there was a plausible mechanism for partial unblinding of outcome assessors (differential discontinuation-related adverse events in the placebo group), and the outcome assessment involved an element of clinical judgement (both in Y-BOCS scoring and explicitly in the "lack of efficacy" relapse criterion).
