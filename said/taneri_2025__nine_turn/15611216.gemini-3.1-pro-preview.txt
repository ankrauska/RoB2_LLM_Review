Outcome Name: Agitation Score (table 4)

1. 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?
   • Response: No information
   • Reason: The authors state that they "randomly assigned participants to study groups in time blocks of 1 week" and "randomized time blocks as treatment or control condition weeks," but they provide no specific information on the method or random component used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., random number generator, coin tossing).

2. 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?
   • Response: No information
   • Reason: There is no mention in the text of any methods used to conceal the allocation sequence (such as central randomization or sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes) before participants were enrolled and assigned to their respective time blocks.

3. 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?
   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: Table 1 and the text report a statistically significant baseline imbalance in a key prognostic factor, which is the exact target of the intervention: time spent in arousal imbalance (Experimental: 107.4 mins vs. Control: 84.6 mins; p = .012). The authors themselves note that "random assignment did not equalize the groups on arousal imbalance" and had to control for it in the analysis. Furthermore, participants were randomized by time blocks rather than individually, which increases the likelihood of baseline imbalances that can bias the intervention effect estimate.

Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: High
(The lack of specific information on randomization and allocation concealment, combined with significant baseline imbalances in a critical prognostic variable stemming from block-time randomization, raises substantial concerns for bias in the randomization process.)

Outcome Name: Agitation Score (table 4)

**Effect of interest chosen:** Effect of assignment to intervention

1. 2.1. Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?
   • Response: No
   • Reason: The authors explicitly state under the Design section that "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions." Furthermore, the participants had moderate to severe dementia (MMSE ≤ 15), making it highly unlikely they could deduce their assignment.

2. 2.2. Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants' assigned intervention during the trial?
   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: The intervention involved changing the daily activity schedule. The authors note that "The GNP worked with the staff nurse to configure a new daily activity schedule" and "the nursing staff implemented the new activity schedule." Therefore, the nursing staff (carers/deliverers) were inherently aware of the assigned intervention to implement it.

3. 2.3. Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?
   • Response: Probably no
   • Reason: Although there were deviations (the intervention was not given in 10 cases), the authors explain this was due to practical real-world factors: "Nurses stated the intervention was not given in 10 cases because of being short-staffed, being busy, or having more emergent needs." These are deviations consistent with what would occur outside the trial context rather than deviations arising specifically because of the trial context (e.g., control participants intentionally seeking out the intervention).

4. 2.4. Were these deviations likely to have affected the outcome?
   • Response: Not applicable
   • Reason: This question is skipped because the answer to 2.3 is 'Probably no'.

5. 2.5. Were these deviations from intended intervention balanced between groups?
   • Response: Not applicable
   • Reason: This question is skipped because the answer to 2.4 is not applicable.

6. 2.6. Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?
   • Response: No
   • Reason: The authors did not use an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis. They state that 10 participants "were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention." Excluding trial participants post-randomization simply because they did not receive their assigned intervention constitutes an inappropriate naive 'per-protocol' analysis when assessing the effect of assignment to intervention.

7. 2.7. Was there potential for a substantial impact (on the result) of the failure to analyse participants in the group to which they were randomized?
   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: Ten participants were excluded purely for non-adherence (not receiving the intervention), out of an initial pool of 102 (resulting in a final sample of 78). Excluding over 10% of the sample specifically because nursing staff were "short-staffed, being busy, or having more emergent needs" can systematically bias the results, as these factors may be strongly related to the participants' baseline agitation or prognosis on those specific days.

Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 2 (Effect of assignment to intervention): High

Outcome Name: Agitation Score (table 4)

1. 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?
   • Response: No
   • Reason: Out of the 102 participants who participated in the study during the allotted time, 24 participants (approximately 23.5%) were missing from the final analysis. Specifically, 12 died, 10 were dropped for not receiving the intervention, and 2 were excluded for missing or erroneous data, leaving a final sample of 78. This exceeds the general threshold of having data for 95% of participants for continuous outcomes.

2. 3.2. Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?
   • Response: No
   • Reason: The authors only analyzed the final sample of 78 participants. There is no mention in the text of any sensitivity analyses, multiple imputation, or other statistical methods used to assess or correct for the potential bias introduced by the missing outcome data.

3. 3.3. Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?
   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: The reasons for missing data include death (12 participants) and nurses being too busy or dealing with "emergent needs" to deliver the intervention (10 participants). Both a deteriorating health status leading to death and acute "emergent needs" in a dementia care setting could easily be related to a participant's level of agitation (the true value of the outcome).

4. 3.4. Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?
   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: Ten participants were dropped specifically for "not being given the intervention," a reason that applies uniquely to the experimental group. Because the control group was not intended to receive the intervention, this creates a difference between the intervention groups in the reported reasons for missing outcome data. Furthermore, the explicit reason that staff were dealing with "emergent needs" or were too busy is highly likely to correlate directly with severe episodes of agitation in the patients.

Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: High

Outcome Name: Agitation Score (table 4)

**Overall predicted direction of bias:** Favours experimental
*Reasoning:* Ten participants were dropped from the experimental group because staff were "short-staffed, being busy, or having more emergent needs." These "emergent needs" likely correspond to severe episodes of agitation or acute distress. Excluding these highly agitated participants from the experimental group's post-test analysis would artificially lower the mean agitation score for that group. Additionally, the baseline imbalance meant the experimental group had significantly more arousal imbalance initially, giving them more room for regression to the mean compared to the control group. Both of these methodological flaws would systematically bias the results to make the intervention appear more effective than it actually is.

**Overall Risk-of-bias judgement:** High
*Reasoning:* The evaluation identified a High risk of bias in Domain 1 (Randomization process), Domain 2 (Deviations from intended interventions), and Domain 3 (Missing outcome data), primarily driven by the lack of allocation concealment, significant baseline imbalances in key prognostic factors, and the inappropriate exclusion of a substantial number of participants post-randomization due to trial context factors. According to the Cochrane RoB 2 guidelines, if one or more domains are at High risk of bias, the overall judgement must be High.
