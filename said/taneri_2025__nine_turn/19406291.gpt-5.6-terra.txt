**Outcome Name: HAM-D score**

### Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

1. **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?**

   - **Response:** Yes  
   - **Reason:** The article explicitly states that random assignment to sertraline or placebo was balanced using a block design (blocks of eight) with **computer-generated random-number sequences**. This is a random allocation-sequence generation method.

2. **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**

   - **Response:** Yes  
   - **Reason:** After participants had completed eligibility and medical-clearance assessments, they were randomized by the pharmacy. The article reports that the pharmacy implemented masking and random assignment. It also states that the computer-generated allocation sequences were placed in individually presealed envelopes and that pharmacy-dispensed sertraline and placebo had identical markings. Study staff and participants were blinded to allocation. These procedures support that allocation was concealed from enrolment and study personnel until assignment.

3. **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**

   - **Response:** No  
   - **Reason:** The randomized groups were of broadly similar size at enrolment (27 sertraline; 25 placebo). The article reports no statistically significant between-group differences in sex, age, education, ethnicity, injury cause, time since injury, injury severity, or baseline depression, anxiety, and quality-of-life measures. Although the authors report a statistically significant difference in income, a single statistically significant baseline difference among numerous examined characteristics is compatible with chance. There was no reported substantial imbalance in baseline HAM-D score or another clearly key prognostic factor that indicated a failure of randomization.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Low risk of bias**

**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)**

   - **Response:** No  
   - **Reason:** This was described as a double-blind trial. The pharmacy dispensed sertraline and placebo with identical markings, and the article states that participants were blinded to whether they received sertraline or placebo. Although adverse events occurred, the reported events do not provide evidence that participants became aware of allocation: increased anxiety occurred in one participant in the sertraline arm, while increased depression and a mild allergic reaction occurred among placebo recipients.

**Outcome Name: HAM-D score**

### Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

1. **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**

   - **Response:** No  
   - **Reason:** Fifty-two participants were randomized (27 sertraline; 25 placebo), but only 41 completed the clinical trial and had reported end-of-treatment HAM-D data (22 sertraline; 19 placebo). Thus, end-of-treatment HAM-D data were unavailable for 11/52 randomized participants (21%). This is materially greater than the approximately 5% missingness often considered acceptable for a continuous outcome. The use of last-observation-carried-forward (LOCF) does not mean outcome data were available, because imputed observations are considered missing data for this assessment.

2. **3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**

   - **Response:** No  
   - **Reason:** The authors report an intent-to-treat analysis using LOCF for participants who did not complete treatment. However, LOCF does not reliably correct bias arising from missing outcome data. The report does not describe an analysis method designed to address missingness related to outcome values, nor does it report sensitivity analyses assessing whether the estimated intervention effect was robust under plausible assumptions about the missing HAM-D data.

3. **3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**

   - **Response:** Yes  
   - **Reason:** Missing outcome data resulted from withdrawal and noncompletion. Reasons included increased anxiety, increased depression after a family death, a mild allergic reaction, and protocol noncompliance (missed follow-up appointments, failure to collect medication, or failure to respond to contacts). Importantly, the authors’ intent-to-treat analysis found that participants who dropped out tended to have higher HAM-D scores at their last assessment than participants who remained in treatment (*F* = 4.0; *P* = .052). Therefore, missingness could be related to depression severity—the outcome being measured.

4. **3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**

   - **Response:** Yes  
   - **Reason:** There is direct reported evidence that those who discontinued treatment had higher HAM-D scores at their last observed assessment than those who remained in treatment. In addition, some withdrawals were explicitly related to worsening anxiety or depression. In a depression-treatment trial, continued or worsening depressive symptoms can plausibly contribute to noncompletion. Given that 21% of randomized participants lacked end-of-treatment data and dropout was associated with higher observed depression scores, it is likely that missingness depended on the unobserved true HAM-D outcome values.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: High risk of bias.**

### Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

1. **4.1 Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**

   - **Response:** No  
   - **Reason:** Depression severity was measured using the 17-item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-D), a clinician-rated scale. The article identifies the HAM-D as a widely used measure of depression severity and reports that it has high reliability and validity. The same pre-specified instrument was used throughout the study to measure the primary outcome.

2. **4.2 Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**

   - **Response:** No  
   - **Reason:** Participants in both groups underwent the same scheduled outcome-assessment procedures: baseline assessment, biweekly HAM-D assessments during the 10-week treatment period, and an end-of-treatment assessment. Assessments were conducted independently of physician medication-management visits. The report provides no indication that HAM-D measurement methods, timing, assessment thresholds, or assessment intensity differed between the sertraline and placebo groups.

3. **4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**

   - **Response:** No  
   - **Reason:** The report states that the pharmacy conducted masking and random assignment, and that both participants and study staff were blinded to the intervention received. HAM-D ratings were conducted by a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist independently of the physician. Therefore, the outcome assessors were reported to be blinded to whether participants received sertraline or placebo.

4. **4.4 Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

   - **Response:** No information  
   - **Reason:** This question is not applicable because outcome assessors were blinded to allocation (response to 4.3: “No”). The HAM-D is a clinician-rated measure involving judgement, so knowledge could theoretically influence ratings if blinding had failed; however, the article provides no evidence that assessors knew participants’ intervention assignments.

5. **4.5 Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

   - **Response:** No information  
   - **Reason:** This question is not applicable because assessors were reported to be unaware of intervention assignment. No evidence indicates compromised assessor blinding or differential outcome assessment.

### Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

1. **5.1 Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available for analysis?**

   - **Response:** Probably yes  
   - **Reason:** The trial was registered prospectively in ClinicalTrials.gov (**NCT00233103**), with first posting in October 2005, while recruitment/study conduct occurred from June 2003 to September 2008. The registry identifies HAM-D depression outcomes at baseline and end of treatment, and the article identifies the HAM-D as the primary outcome and bases its power calculation on this measure. A full statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data became available was not provided; therefore, “Yes” cannot be assigned. However, prospective registration and consistency of HAM-D as the stated primary outcome support “Probably yes.”

2. **5.2 Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?**

   - **Response:** Probably no  
   - **Reason:** The main depression outcome was the total 17-item HAM-D score, which was identified as the primary outcome in the article and corresponds to the registered depression outcome. Although the investigators also examined several HAM-D subscales and measured HAM-D repeatedly during treatment, they reported the total HAM-D outcome at baseline and end of treatment and reported the repeated-measures analysis of total HAM-D scores. The article also reports results for the additional HAM-D subscales, rather than only reporting a favourable subscale result. There is no reported evidence that the total HAM-D result was selected from multiple outcome measurements because it was statistically favourable.

3. **5.3 Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?**

   - **Response:** Probably no  
   - **Reason:** The investigators report several analyses of the HAM-D data, including an intent-to-treat approach using last observation carried forward, random-effects repeated-measures models across six assessments, and repeated-measures analyses comparing baseline with end-of-treatment scores. The principal random-effects analysis of the total HAM-D score showed no statistically significant group effect, and this null finding was reported. Although a full pre-specified statistical analysis plan is unavailable and multiple analytic approaches were possible, the report does not show evidence that only a statistically favourable analysis was selected for reporting.
