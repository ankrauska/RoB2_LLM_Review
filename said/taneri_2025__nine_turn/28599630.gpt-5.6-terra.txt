**Outcome Name: prevalence of depressive symptoms (EPDS score ≥ 11)**

### Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

1. **1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?**

   • **Response: No information**  
   • **Reason:** The article states that eligible women were “randomly assigned” and that “randomization was performed by a researcher not involved in the data collection” after stratification for EPDS score and prior history of depression. However, it does not report how the allocation sequence itself was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, block randomization with a random component, or minimization). Stratification alone does not establish that sequence generation included a random component.

2. **1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** Trial eligibility was determined after cohort baseline assessment, and eligible women were subsequently allocated by a researcher who was not involved in data collection. The report also states that participants, research assistants, and technicians were blinded to allocation, and that intervention and placebo capsules were identical in appearance and packaging. These features support that allocation was likely concealed from participants and study personnel involved in enrolment/data collection. However, the precise concealment mechanism—such as central randomization, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or sequentially numbered identical drug containers—is not described; therefore, “Yes” cannot be assigned with certainty.

3. **1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** The randomized group sizes were 32 in the fish-oil group and 28 in the control group, which is not a substantial imbalance. Baseline EPDS scores were similar: median 10.0 in the fish-oil group and 10.5 in the control group (*P* = 0.621). Previous depression history was also broadly balanced (50.0% versus 60.7%; *P* = 0.405). Although skin colour showed some imbalance (non-white: 59.4% versus 82.1%; *P* = 0.055), this isolated difference is compatible with chance in a small trial and does not by itself demonstrate a flawed randomization process. No excess of statistically significant baseline imbalances is evident.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Some concerns**

**Justification:** There is no reported information on the method used to generate the allocation sequence; thus, it cannot be confirmed that a random component was used. Although allocation concealment was probably adequate and baseline data do not indicate a problem with randomization, the absence of sequence-generation details results in **some concerns** for this domain.

## Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

1. **3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** Outcome data at the relevant endpoint, 4–6 weeks postpartum (T3), were not available for all or nearly all 60 randomized women. The participant flow indicates that only 32 women completed the trial (15 allocated fish oil and 17 allocated placebo). Thus, approximately 47% of randomized participants did not complete the trial. This level of missing postpartum outcome data is substantial, particularly for a dichotomous outcome with relatively few observed events. Although Table 3 labels results as ITT and presents denominators of 32 and 28, the report does not describe collection of postpartum EPDS data from all randomized participants, nor does it describe how outcome data for those who withdrew, were excluded, had pregnancy loss/stillbirth, or missed follow-up were handled.

2. **3.2. If no/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** No analysis method intended to address possible bias due to missing EPDS outcome data is reported. In particular, the article does not describe multiple imputation based on appropriate predictors, inverse-probability weighting, a model explicitly addressing missingness, or sensitivity analyses assessing plausible assumptions about the missing postpartum EPDS outcomes. Merely describing the analysis as ITT does not establish that missing outcome data were appropriately handled.

3. **3.3. If no/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** The outcome is depressive symptoms assessed using a self-reported EPDS at a postpartum study visit. It is plausible that a participant’s depressive symptoms could influence attendance at follow-up or withdrawal from the study. In addition, reasons for non-completion included missed visits and intervention dropout, for which outcome-independent reasons were not fully documented. While some reported reasons—such as transfer of prenatal care, pregnancy loss/stillbirth, and gestational-age reclassification—do not clearly indicate a relationship with depressive symptoms, the report does not establish that all missing postpartum EPDS data were unrelated to the true outcome value.

4. **3.4. If yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** Missing outcome data were extensive, and loss/non-completion was somewhat more frequent in the fish-oil group than in the placebo group: 15 of 32 fish-oil participants versus 17 of 28 placebo participants completed the trial. The report provides incomplete reasons for withdrawal and does not report outcome data or depressive-symptom status for participants who dropped out or missed postpartum assessment. Given the subjective mental-health outcome and substantial, differential non-completion between groups, the estimated treatment effect could be sensitive to missingness related to depressive symptoms.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: High risk of bias**

**Justification:** Postpartum EPDS outcome data were missing for a substantial proportion of randomized participants. The investigators did not report methods or sensitivity analyses that address bias from missing outcome data. Because depressive symptoms could influence follow-up participation, and completion differed between trial groups, it is likely that missingness may have depended on the unobserved outcome.

## Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

1. **4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** Depressive symptoms were measured using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), a widely used self-report screening instrument for perinatal depressive symptoms. The authors state that the Portuguese EPDS version had been validated in Brazilian mothers. They used the prespecified threshold of EPDS ≥11 for moderate/severe postpartum depressive symptoms, citing sensitivity of 83.8% and specificity of 74.7% against the relevant reference standard. The measure is appropriate for the reported outcome, which is depressive symptoms rather than a clinical diagnosis of depressive disorder.

2. **4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** EPDS was administered at the same scheduled assessment time points in both randomized groups: at 22–24 weeks’ gestation (T1), 30–32 weeks’ gestation (T2), and 4–6 weeks postpartum (T3). The same EPDS instrument and the same cut-off (≥11) were used for fish-oil and placebo participants. There is no report of differential outcome-ascertainment procedures, intervention-related clinical visits, or unequal opportunities for depressive symptoms to be detected between groups.

3. **4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** This was a double-blind placebo-controlled trial. The report explicitly states that participants and research assistants responsible for conducting the RCT were blinded to allocation. Since EPDS is participant reported, participants are the outcome assessors for this outcome. Fish-oil and placebo capsules were described as identical in colour, shape, size, and packaging, and both were deodorized. There was no reported side-effect pattern specific to fish oil that would likely have revealed allocation.

4. **4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

   • **Response: No information**  
   • **Reason:** This signalling question is not applicable because the outcome assessors/participants were not aware of assigned intervention (response to 4.3: No). If participants had known allocation, their reporting of depressive symptoms could potentially have been influenced; however, there is no evidence that blinding was broken.

5. **4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

   • **Response: No information**  
   • **Reason:** This signalling question is not applicable because there is no indication that participants knew their allocated intervention or that outcome assessment could have been influenced by such knowledge.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: Low risk of bias**

**Justification:** The outcome was measured using an appropriate, validated perinatal depressive-symptom instrument, using the same procedures, schedule, and threshold in both trial groups. Participants, who completed the self-reported EPDS, and study personnel were blinded to allocation. Therefore, there is no indication of differential or biased outcome measurement.

## Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

1. **5.1. Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available for analysis?**

   • **Response: No**

It does not specify the fish-oil versus placebo RCT, EPDS ≥11 as the RCT primary outcome, the relevant postpartum assessment, or the planned RCT statistical analyses. Furthermore, the registry record states that registration occurred retrospectively on 24 May 2012, whereas recruitment occurred from November 2009 to October 2011 and the final follow-up was in July 2012. Therefore, the available registry entry does not demonstrate that an analysis plan for this RCT outcome was finalized before unblinded outcome data became available.

2. **5.2. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?**

   • **Response: Probably no**  
   • **Reason:** The article explicitly identifies prevalence of EPDS score ≥11 as the primary outcome and reports this dichotomous outcome at all reported assessment points (T0, T1, T2, and T3), for both ITT and per-protocol populations, in Table 3. The primary endpoint is described as postpartum depressive symptoms at T3 (4–6 weeks postpartum). Although EPDS was also examined as a continuous score and at several time points, the primary dichotomous outcome definition (EPDS ≥11) and its results are transparently reported. There is no direct evidence that the reported prevalence result was selected from alternative EPDS thresholds, instruments, or time points because it produced a more favourable result. However, this cannot be confirmed against a prospectively finalized RCT analysis plan.

3. **5.3. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?**

   • **Response: No information**  
   • **Reason:** Multiple analyses were undertaken, including unadjusted comparisons at individual time points, ITT and per-protocol analyses, and random-intercept longitudinal logistic regression models with and without adjustment for covariates. The article reports the main models and both ITT and per-protocol results, which reduces concern that only a favourable analysis was reported. However, without a prospective RCT protocol or statistical analysis plan specifying the primary analysis, it cannot be determined whether other eligible analyses were conducted but not reported, or whether selection of the reported analysis was influenced by the results.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 5: Some concerns**

**Justification:** The available retrospective ClinicalTrials.gov record is for the parent observational cohort and does not provide a prospective pre-specified analysis plan for the nested RCT or for the EPDS ≥11 outcome. The report does transparently present several analyses and does not provide direct evidence of selective reporting of a favourable result. Nonetheless, the absence of a verifiable pre-unblinding RCT analysis plan results in **some concerns** regarding selection of the reported result.
