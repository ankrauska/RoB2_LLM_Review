### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** The authors reported that the random sequence was generated using a computer-generated list of random numbers in an Excel spreadsheet. They also used randomly permuted blocks, with randomized block sizes of 4 and 6, to maintain a 1:1 allocation ratio. These methods include a random component and are consistent with random sequence generation.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The authors stated that allocation concealment was achieved using “sequentially numbered and sealed opaque envelopes.” The envelopes were concealed from researchers and outcome assessors, and participants took an envelope only after informed consent had been signed. This supports concealment until enrolment. Although the report does not explicitly state that envelopes were tamper-proof or opened strictly sequentially, the reported approach is broadly consistent with adequate allocation concealment.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The groups had equal numbers of participants at randomization (16 per group), consistent with the intended 1:1 allocation ratio. Reported baseline characteristics—including age, skeletal measurements, overjet, and mandibular crowding—did not show a clear imbalance indicating a flawed randomization process. Although some numerical differences were present (e.g., MP/SN and MMP angles), the article reported that baseline characteristics were similar between groups, and no imbalance was clearly large enough to indicate that allocation had been compromised or to plausibly bias the outcome of patient acceptance.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: **Low risk of bias**

**Outcome Name: patient acceptance of the appliance**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** Patient acceptance was measured using a questionnaire completed by participants, which is an appropriate general method for assessing a subjective, patient-reported outcome. The questionnaire was based on and modified from a previously used FFRD patient-experience questionnaire, and used standardized Likert-scale response options. Although the article does not report formal validation of the modified and translated questionnaire, it provides no evidence that the instrument had poor validity or was unable to detect relevant differences in patient acceptance.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Both groups completed the same assessment questionnaire at the same trial stage: the day on which the FFRD was removed. The same questions and response scales were used for both groups. The questionnaire was explained to participants or their parents by the principal investigator in both groups. There is no reported difference in outcome-measurement method, timing, or threshold between intervention groups.

### 4.3. If No/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** For patient-reported acceptance, the participants themselves are the outcome assessors. Participants were aware of their assigned intervention because they either received FFRD alone or FFRD together with bilateral mini-implants. Although the authors reported blinding of the person extracting questionnaire data and the statistician, this does not blind the participants who completed the patient-acceptance questionnaire.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** Patient acceptance is a subjective patient-reported outcome, encompassing perceptions of appliance insertion, function, pain, appearance, and satisfaction. Participants’ knowledge of whether they received mini-implant anchorage in addition to FFRD could influence how they perceived and rated these experiences.

### 4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** The outcome could have been influenced by participants’ knowledge of intervention assignment, but the report provides no evidence that participants had strong expectations, preferences, or beliefs favoring either FFRD alone or mini-implant-anchored FFRD. There is also no evidence that the personnel explaining the questionnaires encouraged a particular response. Therefore, influence of knowledge of assignment is possible but not demonstrated as likely.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: **Some concerns**

- **Justification:** The patient-acceptance outcome was participant-reported and subjective. Participants necessarily knew whether mini-implants had been used, so their outcome assessment could have been influenced by treatment knowledge. The article does not provide evidence that such influence was likely; nevertheless, the absence of participant blinding for this subjective outcome raises some concerns.
