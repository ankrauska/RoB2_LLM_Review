I've thoroughly read through this article by the authors examining tutorial strategies and diagnostic reasoning accuracy in Italian nursing students. Here's my understanding of the key elements for systematic review purposes:

## Study Design Summary
- **Type**: Double pragmatic randomized trial (not a true RCT in the classical sense - described as "pragmatic")
- **Setting**: Two Italian nursing faculties (Trento/Verona and Udine)
- **Sample**: 144 first-year nursing students (62 Trento, 82 Udine) from 179 eligible
- **Duration**: November 2003–April 2004 (6 months)

## Key Methodological Observations Relevant to Critical Appraisal

**Strengths:**
- Randomization within each site (randomization table)
- Blind evaluation of diagnostic hypotheses by two independent researchers
- Standardized case scenario ("Valentina's case") used across all phases
- Pre-defined standard problems established by 15-nurse panel with Cohen's Kappa (K≥0.80)
- Multivariate analysis to control for confounders
- Clear operational definitions of tutorial strategies (Table 1)

**Notable Limitations/Risk of Bias Concerns:**
1. **Non-equivalent baseline contexts** - Trento and Udine had fundamentally different institutional histories/experience with tutorial strategies before the study began, threatening comparability for inter-trial analysis
2. **Confounded intervention during study** - Trento experienced funding cuts *during* the study, reducing tutor availability - a significant protocol deviation/contamination
3. **Repeated use of same case** (with modified data) across 3 phases - potential learning/testing effects acknowledged by authors as threatening internal validity
4. **Selection bias** - excluded students with limited Italian language proficiency, non-first-time enrollees, those with prior healthcare experience
5. **Small subgroup sizes** in Phase 2 (e.g., n=23 for Udine routine tutoring)
6. **Self-report perception data** collected via non-validated questionnaire (only pilot-tested on 10 students)
7. **No blinding of students/tutors** to group allocation (only outcome assessors were blinded)
8. **Short follow-up** (6 months only)
9. **Inter-trial comparisons** are explicitly cautioned by authors themselves due to contextual heterogeneity - this affects how the pooled OR (3.75) should be interpreted
10. **Multiple comparisons** without apparent correction, given numerous subgroup analyses

## Outcome Measures
- Primary: Number of diagnostic errors (dichotomized at median cut-off points that differ by phase - 3 vs 2)
- Secondary: Self-perceived competence/difficulty (NRS scale, open/closed questionnaire)

## Key Effect Estimates for Extraction
- Phase 1 (laboratory vs. no laboratory): OR 4.21 (95% CI 1.78–10.09) univariate; 3.15 (1.10–13.56) multivariate
- Phase 2 intensive tutoring: OR 3.35 (1.36–8.44) univariate; 2.65 (1.10–6.36) multivariate
- Phase 2 routine tutoring (Udine): OR 0.19 (0.06–0.56) univariate; 0.25 reported in multivariate (CI appears anomalous: 0.72–0.91, likely a reporting/typographical error since it doesn't cross 1 in expected direction relative to univariate OR)
- Inter-trial comprehensive OR: 3.75 (1.77–7.88) and 0.22 (0.07–0.65)

# Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**Outcome:** number of mistakes made in diagnosing problems (diagnostic hypotheses) identified by the students in Phases 1 and 2

---

## 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The article explicitly states: "the students enrolled in the research project were consequently randomised with a randomisation table into two different groups (an 'intervention' and a 'control' group)" (Section 3.3). This was applied at both campuses. Additionally, for Phase 2, the text states: "The two groups of students in both Faculties of Nursing (with 20–30 students in each) were randomly selected at each centre using a randomisation table." The explicit reference to a "randomisation table" constitutes a random component in sequence generation, satisfying the criteria for a "Yes" response.

## 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response: No information**
- **Reason:** The article provides no details on who generated the randomization sequence, who enrolled participants, or whether the sequence was concealed from those responsible for enrollment/assignment. There is no mention of sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, central/remote allocation systems, or any other concealment mechanism. Given the complete absence of any description of the allocation concealment procedure, "No information" is the appropriate response, and this cannot be assumed positively.

## 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response: No information**
- **Reason:** Table 2 reports baseline characteristics compared **between Trento and Udine faculties as a whole** (n=82 vs n=62), not between the specific randomized arms (intervention vs. control) within each faculty. Significant differences are reported for "average duration of previous work experience" (p<0.05), "voluntary work experience" (p<0.01), and "clinical training experience programmed" (p<0.01) — but these comparisons are at the **site/faculty level**, reflecting pre-existing contextual/institutional differences between the two campuses, not a comparison of the actual randomized groups (e.g., Trento intervention vs. Trento control, or Udine intervention vs. Udine control). The article does not report baseline characteristics disaggregated by the randomized arms themselves. Without this specific information for the actual comparison groups under evaluation, it is not possible to assess whether randomization within each site produced comparable groups. Therefore, "No information" is selected rather than assuming comparability.

---

## Domain-Level Risk-of-Bias Judgement for Domain 1:

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Justification:** While there is clear evidence that a random component (randomisation table) was used for sequence generation (1.1 = Yes), there is a complete absence of information regarding allocation concealment (1.2 = No information), meaning it cannot be ruled out that enrollment personnel or participants could have foreseen or influenced assignment. Furthermore, the baseline data provided (Table 2) does not allow verification of comparability between the actual randomized arms within each faculty — only between the two non-randomized faculty settings (1.3 = No information). This lack of transparency about concealment procedures and about arm-specific baseline balance introduces sufficient uncertainty to warrant a judgement of **"Some concerns"** rather than "Low risk," in line with the RoB 2 guidance to avoid defaulting to a favorable judgment when key information is missing.

# Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions

**Outcome:** number of mistakes made in diagnosing problems (diagnostic hypotheses) identified by the students in Phases 1 and 2

**Note on effect of interest:** Given that this study is explicitly described as a **pragmatic trial** (Section 3.1: "A double perspective randomised, pragmatic trial was adopted"), designed to evaluate tutorial strategies **as they are actually implemented** in real-world educational settings, I will assess this domain for the **effect of assignment to intervention** (rather than effect of adhering to intervention). This is consistent with the pragmatic design and the intra-trial/inter-trial "as randomized" comparisons reported by the authors.

---

## 2.1. Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The interventions (laboratory sessions, intensive clinical tutoring, weekly tutorials, routine tutoring) are behavioral/educational interventions that cannot be blinded. Students necessarily knew which type of tutorial strategy they were receiving (e.g., whether they attended a laboratory session, had daily debriefing with a tutor, or had only routine ward supervision). No blinding of participants was possible or reported.

## 2.2. Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants' assigned intervention during the trial?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** Tutors delivering the interventions were, by design, different personnel with different competencies for each arm (e.g., "pedagogic and clinical experts during intensive tutoring" vs. "clinical nursing professionals... during routine tutoring," Section 3.3). Tutors necessarily knew which strategy they were delivering; Table 1 also describes tutors meeting to standardize their specific method, confirming awareness of assignment.

## 2.3. Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The article reports one notable deviation: "While involved in the study the Trento Faculty of Nursing, experienced 'cut-backs' with reduced numbers of tutors allocated to supervising students doing practical clinical experience. Student exposure to intensive tutorials was reduced and routine tutorials by ward staff... were introduced" (Section 3.1). However, this deviation is explicitly attributed to **external institutional/budgetary cutbacks**, not to any effect of trial recruitment, informed consent procedures, or engagement activities associated with being a trial participant. Per RoB 2 guidance, "trial context" specifically refers to effects of recruitment/consent processes or trial personnel undermining protocol implementation in ways that would not occur outside the trial. Since this deviation would plausibly have occurred regardless of the trial (a real-world funding cut to nursing tutoring services), it does not meet the definition of a deviation "because of trial context."

## 2.6. Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The authors analyzed data by randomized group assignment ("intra-trial analysis" comparing trial vs. control groups within each faculty, and "inter-trial analysis" comparing across faculties), consistent with an intention-to-treat/as-randomized approach appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention. There is no evidence of per-protocol or as-treated analysis, and no participants appear to have been excluded post-randomization based on non-adherence.

---

## Domain-Level Risk-of-Bias Judgement for Domain 2 (Effect of Assignment to Intervention):

**Judgement: Low risk**

**Justification:** Although blinding of participants and intervention deliverers was not possible (2.1 = Yes, 2.2 = Yes), this is expected and unavoidable for this type of educational/behavioral intervention and does not by itself elevate risk of bias for the assignment-effect estimand. The one substantive deviation reported (reduced intensive tutoring in the Trento faculty due to funding cutbacks) is attributable to an external institutional cause rather than to the trial context itself (2.3 = No), and therefore does not trigger further concern under this domain's logic for the assignment-to-intervention estimand — pragmatic trials are specifically designed to capture the effects of such "real-world" implementation variability. The analysis method used (as-randomized, intra- and inter-trial comparisons) is appropriate for this estimand (2.6 = Yes). Based strictly on the signaling questions and the reported facts, Domain 2 is judged as **Low risk** for the effect of assignment to intervention.

*(Caveat for the reviewer's awareness, not used to alter the formal RoB 2 judgement per the tool's decision rules: the funding-cut-related reduction in intensive tutoring specifically affected the Trento control/routine tutoring arm, which is a genuine fidelity concern for interpreting the "intensive tutoring" contrast, even though it falls outside the strict definition of "trial-context" deviation under RoB 2.)*
