## Study Design Summary
- **Type**: Single-blinded, control-matched, prospective longitudinal study
- **Duration**: July 1998–June 2000; 12-month follow-up with bimonthly visits
- **Population**: 62 insulin-treated diabetic patients (Type 1 and Type 2 combined)
- **Intervention**: Randomization to free glucometer strips (100/month, NC group) vs. self-purchased strips (C group, initial 50-strip supply only)
- **Setting**: Winnipeg, Manitoba, Canada (Health Sciences Centre/Diabetes Education Centre)

## Key Methodological Points Worth Noting
1. **Randomization/matching**: Stratified by income tertile; groups well-matched at baseline (Table 1) for age, sex, diabetes type, duration, education, insurance status, baseline HbA1c, insulin regimen
2. **Blinding**: Patients blinded to study's primary purpose (told it was a "new glucometer" study); nurse did not give feedback on glucose values to avoid behavior modification bias
3. **Outcome measurement**: Objective SBGM frequency via glucometer memory download (not self-report) — a methodological strength
4. **Sample size**: Calculated need ~30/group; enrolled 62, but differential dropout occurred (NC: 19% vs. C: 48%)

## Notable Limitations for Critical Appraisal
- **High/differential attrition**: Substantial dropout, especially in control group (48%), which could introduce attrition bias — power dropped to 0.64 for primary outcomes (SBGM frequency, HbA1c)
- **Small sample size** after dropout, particularly limiting subgroup analysis by diabetes type (Type 1 vs Type 2 not analyzed separately)
- **Industry funding**: Partially funded by Bayer Inc. (glucometer/strip manufacturer) — potential conflict of interest
- **Generalizability**: Only insulin-treated patients; unclear applicability to oral-agent-treated Type 2 diabetics
- **Surrogate outcome**: HbA1c and glucose used rather than hard clinical endpoints (complications, mortality)
- **Short-medium term**: 12 months insufficient to assess durability or impact on complications

## Key Findings for Data Extraction
- SBGM frequency (first 4 months): NC 2.0±0.2 vs. C 1.4±0.1 tests/day (P<0.025)
- HbA1c: stable in NC group; increased over time in C group; significant group difference by 6 months (P<0.002)
- Insulin dose: stable in NC; increased 1.5-fold in C group
- HbA1c dichotomized by testing frequency: ≥2 tests/day = 8.8±0.2% vs. <2 tests/day = 9.6±0.2% (P<0.001)
- Barriers to SBGM: inconvenience (top-ranked both entry/exit); cost dropped from 31% (entry) to 10% (exit), plausibly due to differential dropout of cost-burdened C-group patients

**Reasoning**: The primary driver of bias in this study is the differential and outcome-dependent missingness identified in Domain 3. If patients with worse HbA1c/lower SBGM frequency preferentially dropped out of the C group, the *remaining* C-group completers would show artificially better (lower) HbA1c and higher SBGM frequency than the true underlying C-group population. This would **narrow the apparent gap** between the NC and C groups, causing the estimated intervention effect to be **attenuated (biased toward the null)** relative to the true effect. In other words, this specific bias mechanism would tend to make the free-strip intervention appear *less* beneficial than it truly is — working against, not in favor of, the study's positive findings.

Since the study still found statistically significant differences favoring the NC group despite this null-ward bias, the true effect size may be even larger than reported, though this cannot be confirmed without further data.

This would inflate the apparent glycemic control of the *remaining* C-group completers, artificially **narrowing** the observed gap between NC and C groups. This mechanism biases the effect estimate **toward the null**, suggesting the true beneficial effect of free strip access may be **underestimated**, not overestimated, in this study.

**Overall Risk-of-Bias — High**: Per the RoB 2 algorithm, an overall "High risk" judgment is triggered whenever **any single domain** is rated "High." Domain 3 (missing outcome data) met this threshold due to:
- Large, differential dropout (48% C vs. 19% NC)
- Author-acknowledged, outcome-related dropout mechanism (poor control/cost driving attrition)
- Inadequate compensatory analysis (LOCF is not considered bias-correcting under RoB 2)

This single domain is sufficient to elevate the overall judgment to **High risk of bias**, irrespective of the "Low" and "Some concerns" ratings observed in the remaining four domains.
