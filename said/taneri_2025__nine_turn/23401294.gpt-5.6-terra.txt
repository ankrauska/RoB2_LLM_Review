**Outcome Name: balance score**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The article explicitly reports that participants were “randomly allocated” and describes the procedure as picking the name of an orthosis from a bag containing the names of both orthoses. Drawing allocation names from a bag is a random process (drawing lots), assuming each orthosis name had an equal opportunity to be selected.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response: No information**
- **Reason:** The allocation procedure involved selecting an orthosis name from a bag, but the report provides no details indicating whether the draw was performed after irreversible enrolment, who conducted the draw, whether the allocation names were indistinguishable, or whether recruiters or participants could anticipate the next allocation. No central randomization, sealed opaque envelopes, or other concealment mechanism was reported.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The group sizes were slightly unequal (Spinomed: *n* = 10; posture-training support: *n* = 8), but this small discrepancy is compatible with chance in a small trial using a drawing-lots allocation procedure. The authors reported no statistically significant between-group differences at baseline in age, height, weight, thoracic kyphosis angle, or the balance outcomes measured without orthosis, including the primary outcome balance score (*p* = 0.443). The baseline balance-score means were also reasonably similar (Spinomed: 65.1; posture-training support: 62.38). There is no reported baseline imbalance sufficiently substantial to indicate a problematic randomization process.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

- **Judgement: Some concerns**
- **Reason:** The allocation sequence appears random because assignment was determined by drawing orthosis names from a bag. However, allocation concealment was insufficiently described. In particular, the article does not establish that allocation could not have been known or predicted by the person enrolling participants before assignment. Although baseline data do not indicate an apparent randomization failure, the lack of information about concealment results in **some concerns**.

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Balance score was measured using the Sensory Organization Test (SOT) on the NeuroCom EquiTest system, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The balance score is a computerized, force-platform-derived measure based on standardized sensory conditions. The authors also state that this measure has previously demonstrated acceptable test–retest reliability and predictive validity. No information in the report indicates that the measure was unsuitable or insufficiently sensitive to detect an immediate effect of the orthoses.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** Both intervention groups underwent the same SOT assessment using the same NeuroCom EquiTest system and the same standardized protocol. In each group, balance was tested both with and without the assigned orthosis, with randomized testing order and a 1-hour interval between testing conditions. The article does not report group-specific outcome-measurement procedures, assessment schedules, thresholds, or methods.

### 4.3. If no/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response: No information**
- **Reason:** The study is described as a “single blinded clinical trial,” but it does not specify who was blinded. In particular, it does not state whether the individual administering the SOT or recording/overseeing the balance assessment was blinded to whether a participant wore Spinomed or PTS. Since the devices were visibly different, assessor blinding would require specific procedures, which were not reported.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Although assessor blinding is not clearly reported, the balance score was generated using a computerized force-platform system during a standardized SOT. The score is based on recorded postural sway rather than subjective assessor judgement. The report does not indicate that assessors manually rated performance or had discretion in determining the balance-score result. Therefore, knowledge of the orthosis received was unlikely to materially influence outcome measurement.

### 4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Not applicable**
- **Reason:** Knowledge of intervention allocation was judged as unlikely to influence assessment of the computerized balance score (question 4.4: “Probably no”).

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

- **Judgement: Low risk of bias**
- **Reason:** Balance score was measured using the same standardized computerized force-platform procedure in both groups. Although the report does not clarify whether outcome assessors were blinded, the outcome was instrument-derived and did not depend on subjective assessor judgement. Consequently, any lack of assessor blinding was unlikely to have materially biased measurement of the balance-score outcome.
