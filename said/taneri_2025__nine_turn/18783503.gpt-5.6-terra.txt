**Outcome Name: severity of subjective withdrawal symptoms using the Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS)**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The authors state that participants “were assigned randomly” using “a randomization automated system on a one-to-one basis.” This explicitly indicates use of an automated random allocation process.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The article reports that “the randomization list was held securely, and released only after study completion.” In addition, gabapentin and placebo were supplied in identical-appearing capsules, and the trial was described as double-blind. Although the report does not explicitly state who held the allocation list, whether the automated system was centralized/independent, or the exact process by which allocation was communicated after enrolment, the secure withholding of the list until study completion supports probable allocation concealment.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The groups were of equal size at randomization (20 participants per group). The authors report no significant baseline differences in age, methadone dosage, or use/doses of symptomatic medications. Baseline SOWS scores were somewhat lower in the gabapentin group than in the placebo group (mean 12.6 versus 14.9), but the reported standard deviations were large (7.3 and 10.4, respectively), and the article does not identify this as statistically significant or as a clinically substantial prognostic imbalance. No baseline imbalance reported suggests a problem with randomization.

### Domain 1 risk-of-bias judgement: **Low risk of bias**

- **Justification:** The allocation sequence was generated using an automated randomization system; allocation was probably concealed, based on the secure randomization list and double-blind identical-capsule procedures; and there were no reported baseline differences suggesting a flawed randomization process.

### 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Outcome data were available for all 40 randomized participants at baseline, but three participants randomized to placebo were excluded during follow-up. At the final reported assessment, outcome data were available for 20/20 (100%) participants in the gabapentin group and 17/20 (85%) in the placebo group—37/40 (92.5%) overall. This is below the commonly used “nearly all” threshold of approximately 95% for continuous outcomes. In addition, missingness was different between groups: no participants were missing from the gabapentin group, while 15% were missing from the placebo group.

### 3.2. Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The article does not report analysis methods intended to address bias from missing outcome data, such as multiple imputation incorporating relevant prognostic variables, inverse-probability weighting, or sensitivity analyses under plausible assumptions about missing outcomes. The repeated-measures analysis appears to have used the available data after the three placebo participants were excluded.

### 3.3. Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The three placebo participants were excluded because of non-compliance with the treatment programme and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter drugs. In a detoxification trial, non-compliance and self-medication may be related to the severity of opioid withdrawal symptoms, the outcome being measured. Therefore, missingness could depend on the true underlying withdrawal-symptom severity.

### 3.4. Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** All missing outcome data occurred in the placebo group, with no missing participants in the gabapentin group. The missingness was due to non-compliance and self-medication during opioid detoxification, behaviours that are plausibly associated with more severe or inadequately controlled withdrawal symptoms. The authors themselves state that non-compliance and self-medication in the placebo group “may be due to the greater severity of withdrawal symptoms.” Although this does not prove the relationship, the differential missingness and reported reasons make it likely that missingness was related to true withdrawal severity.

### Domain 3 risk-of-bias judgement: **High risk of bias**

- **Justification:** Outcome data were missing for 7.5% of all randomized participants and 15% of the placebo group at the final assessment, with all missingness occurring in one intervention group. No analysis or sensitivity analysis was reported to address potential bias from missing data. The reasons for dropout—non-compliance and self-medication during withdrawal—are likely related to the true severity of withdrawal symptoms.
