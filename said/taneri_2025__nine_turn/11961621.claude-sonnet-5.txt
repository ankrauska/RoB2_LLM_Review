# Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

## Outcome Name: length of hospital stay

**4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Length of hospital stay (measured in days) is generally a straightforward, objective outcome that is sensitive to plausible intervention effects between LAVH and AH. However, the specific decision-making process for discharge is highly relevant here (addressed in 4.2-4.5), even though the metric itself is not invalid.

**4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The article explicitly states, "Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital." This means that the outcome (hospital stay duration) was not determined by an objective, protocol-driven clinical discharge criterion (e.g., specific vital signs, ambulation milestones, or physician-determined criteria) but rather by the patient's own subjective judgment. Since patients were aware of their surgical intervention (open-label trial) and outcomes could plausibly differ due to differing pain experiences, recovery expectations, or preconceived notions about the two procedures, ascertainment of this outcome could clearly differ between groups.

**4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** For this outcome, the effective "outcome assessor" is the patient herself, who decided when to be discharged. As established in Domain 2, participants were necessarily aware of their assigned surgical intervention (LAVH vs. AH), as blinding is not feasible for two distinct surgical approaches.

**4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** Since the patient (who is aware of her intervention) self-determines the discharge date, this is a judgment-based, participant-reported-type outcome rather than a purely objective one (e.g., unlike all-cause mortality). Patient expectations, beliefs about the invasiveness of AH vs. LAVH, and awareness of the "minimally invasive" nature of LAVH could plausibly influence when she felt "ready" to be discharged.

**4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The authors themselves acknowledge this concern directly in the Discussion: "the patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment; thus, their expectations may have influenced the duration of their recuperation or their satisfaction with the procedure." Although this statement is made specifically regarding "recuperation," the same subjective, patient-driven discharge process directly determined the length of hospital stay outcome. Given the strong a priori clinical and cultural expectation that laparoscopic/minimally invasive procedures result in faster recovery, and that this was the primary outcome the study was designed and powered to detect ("We hoped to reduce the median hospital stay... from 9 to 7 days"), it is likely that patients' knowledge of their intervention influenced their self-determined discharge timing, in the anticipated direction (favoring LAVH).

**Justification:** Although the metric itself (days to discharge) is not an inherently invalid measurement method, the *ascertainment* of this outcome was fundamentally subjective and patient-driven ("each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged"), rather than based on objective, pre-specified, protocol-driven clinical criteria applied consistently and blindly across groups.
