**Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data**

**3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**
- Response: **No**
- Reason: Of the 519 subjects who completed T1 baseline assessments, only 458 (88.2%) completed T2 and 448 (86.3%) completed T3 interviews. For the BSI specifically, at Postintervention 2 (T3), only 220/251 (87.6%) of the intervention group and 228/268 (85.1%) of the control group had available data. This falls below the ~95% threshold typically considered sufficient for continuous outcomes, so missing data cannot be assumed to have made no important difference to the estimated effect.

**3.2. Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**
- Response: **Probably no**
- Reason: The authors used MIXREG (mixed-effects random regression), which is a likelihood-based approach that can appropriately handle missing data under a missing-at-random (MAR) assumption, and the authors cite this as an advantage of the method ("missing observations due to the fact that not all subjects completed all assessments"). However, no formal sensitivity analyses (e.g., testing different assumptions about the missingness mechanism, or comparing MAR vs. non-MAR scenarios) were reported to directly demonstrate that the result was robust to the missing data. Reliance on the modeling approach alone is not sufficiently strong evidence to rule out bias.

**3.3. Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**
- Response: **Yes**
- Reason: Reported reasons for loss to follow-up included "death or ill health, moving away from the area, and formal withdrawal from the study." "Ill health" is plausibly related to psychiatric symptom severity (the true outcome value) in this population with severe and persistent mental illness, making it credible that missingness could be related to the outcome being measured.

**3.4. Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**
- Response: **Probably yes**
- Reason: There is a differential rate of loss to follow-up between the two arms: 25 of 276 (9.1%) randomized intervention participants were lost to follow-up compared to only 11 of 279 (3.9%) randomized control participants — more than double the attrition rate in the experimental arm. Per RoB2 guidance, a difference between intervention groups in the proportions of missing outcome data is one of the explicit criteria supporting a "likely" judgment. Additionally, the authors note that 68% of the dropped-out experimental participants received no WRAP sessions at all, suggesting early disengagement (potentially related to poorer initial functioning/symptom severity) rather than random administrative loss. No information is provided demonstrating that this imbalance was unrelated to symptom severity or health status.

- **Domain 2 (Deviations)**: Contamination in the control arm (peer-support group attendance) would tend to dilute the between-group difference, biasing results **toward the null** — this works in the opposite direction of Domains 3 and 4.

Since two of the "High risk" domains (missing data and outcome measurement) both plausibly operate in the same direction (inflating the apparent benefit of WRAP), while the "Some concerns" domain (deviations) works in the opposite (diluting) direction but was judged with lower severity, the dominant and more probable overall direction of bias is **toward overestimating the beneficial effect of the WRAP intervention — i.e., Favours experimental**.
