### Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

1. **1.1 Was the allocation sequence random?**

   • **Response: Yes**  
   • **Reason:** The article explicitly states that participants “were randomized by a computer-generated list.” A computer-generated allocation list contains a random component and is an acceptable random sequence-generation method.

2. **1.2 Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** The computer-generated randomization list was used for packaging the study tablets and was “maintained by a third party that was not involved in the conduction of the study.” Sodium bicarbonate and placebo tablets had identical appearance, packaging, labeling, and artificial mint flavour. The report also states that marked tablet packs were designated for each patient and that both patients and investigators were blinded. Although the report does not explicitly state that packs were sequentially numbered, dispensed only after irreversible enrolment, or otherwise protected from foreknowledge, the involvement of an independent third party in maintaining the allocation list and the use of identically packaged medication make adequate allocation concealment likely.

3. **1.3 Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** The groups were equal in size (30 participants per group), consistent with the intended 1:1 allocation. The authors report no statistically significant baseline differences. Demographic characteristics, dialysis duration, body size, dialysis prescription, inflammatory markers, dialysis adequacy, residual GFR, and Charlson Comorbidity Index were broadly comparable. There were numerical differences in some individual comorbidities—for example, congestive heart failure (12 versus 8) and cerebrovascular disease (1 versus 6) in treatment and placebo groups, respectively—but these isolated imbalances in a small trial are compatible with chance and do not by themselves demonstrate a flawed randomization process.

### Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

1. **3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**

   • **Response: Probably no**  
   • **Reason:** SGA was scheduled at baseline and at 12, 24, 36, and 52 weeks. The article reports that seven participants discontinued study medication but continued biochemical and nutritional assessments through 52 weeks. However, it does not report the number of participants with SGA data at each assessment time or provide a participant flow diagram. During the 12-month study, seven participants died (two in the bicarbonate group and five in the placebo group), two transferred to long-term haemodialysis, and one received a kidney transplant. SGA data after these events would not be available, and the report does not state how these missing data were handled. Thus, outcome data were not available for all randomized participants, and missingness could exceed the level generally considered “nearly all” for a continuous outcome.

2. **3.2 If No/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** The authors state that analyses were intention-to-treat, but do not describe a method that addresses bias from missing SGA measurements due to death, transfer to haemodialysis, or transplantation. No imputation method, model assumptions regarding missing data, sensitivity analysis, or analysis demonstrating robustness to plausible missing-data mechanisms is reported.

3. **3.3 If No/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** Missing SGA data may have resulted from death or transfer to long-term haemodialysis. These events may be associated with participants’ clinical and nutritional condition, which is directly related to the SGA outcome. Therefore, it is plausible that the probability of having missing SGA data depended on the underlying SGA score.

4. **3.4 If Yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** Seven deaths occurred during the 12-month follow-up, with more deaths in the placebo group than in the bicarbonate group (five versus two). Death is likely to be associated with poorer underlying health and potentially poorer nutritional status/SGA score. In addition, SGA measurements after death cannot be obtained. The article neither reports SGA data availability by group and time point nor addresses this potentially informative missingness. The differential number of deaths between groups further raises concern that missingness may be related to true outcome values and may differ between groups.
