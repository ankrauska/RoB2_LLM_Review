**Outcome Name: effects of supervision on patient care, measured by a team checklist score**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Patient care was measured using scenario-specific checklists of predefined clinical tasks, with a task counted as completed if performed by any team member. Such checklists are a plausible method for measuring care processes during standardized simulation scenarios. However, the authors acknowledge that the checklists were nonvalidated and that construct validity was not assessed. They report that the development process aimed to ensure face and content validity. There is no evidence that the checklist had demonstrated poor validity or was unable to detect plausible differences in patient-care processes; therefore, the method is not clearly inappropriate, although it has limitations.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The outcome was measured as the percentage of predefined checklist tasks completed during the supervised scenario. The same type of outcome measurement was used across the three supervision groups, and all scenarios were conducted in the same simulation centre using standardized scenario-specific checklists. Although three different scenario pairs were used, the article does not report that checklist assessment methods, thresholds for determining task completion, or outcome-collection procedures differed systematically by assigned supervision group.

### 4.3. If No/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The article does not state that assessors of the supervised-scenario team checklist score were blinded to assigned supervision level. Blinding would be difficult because the intervention was defined by the supervisor’s physical proximity: the fellow was present in the room for direct supervision, available by phone and potentially at the bedside for immediately available supervision, and available only by phone for distant supervision. Therefore, anyone observing or reviewing the scenario to determine whether tasks were completed would probably be able to identify the supervision group.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The team checklist score was based on judgments about whether predefined clinical tasks had been completed by any team member. Although task completion is more objective than a global performance judgment, determining whether a task was adequately completed can involve interpretation, particularly in a simulated clinical scenario. Knowledge that a fellow was directly present, immediately available, or distant could therefore influence borderline judgments about task completion.

### 4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: No information**
- **Reason:** The article does not report whether team checklist scoring was performed by blinded assessors, whether scoring was independently duplicated, whether assessors were trained or calibrated for this specific outcome, or whether procedures were used to minimize expectancy effects. However, it also provides no direct evidence that assessors actually scored the intervention groups differently because of their knowledge of supervision level. Thus, it cannot be established that influence of intervention knowledge was likely.

### Domain 4 overall risk-of-bias judgement: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** The scenario-specific team checklist was a reasonable but nonvalidated measure, and there is no indication that assessors of this outcome were blinded to supervision allocation. Because supervision level was visible during the simulation and assessment of checklist task completion may involve some judgment, outcome assessment could have been influenced by knowledge of intervention status. The report does not provide enough information to determine whether such influence was likely, resulting in some concerns.
