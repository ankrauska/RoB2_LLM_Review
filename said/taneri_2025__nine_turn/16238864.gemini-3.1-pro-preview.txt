Based on the article provided, I have carefully read and reviewed the study by Kellar and Abraham (2005) titled "Randomized controlled trial of a brief research-based intervention promoting fruit and vegetable consumption." 

The researchers conducted a controlled, randomized experimental study targeting university students to test the efficacy of a brief, leaflet-like intervention designed to increase the consumption of the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables (RDIFV). The intervention utilized persuasive messaging targeting self-efficacy and intention, and prompted participants to form implementation intentions (specific plans regarding where and when to buy and how to prepare fruit and vegetables).

The results indicated that the theory of planned behavior (TPB), augmented with anticipated regret, effectively modeled the cognitive antecedents of fruit and vegetable consumption. At a 1-week follow-up, the intervention group reported stronger intentions and greater anticipated regret if they failed to eat the RDIFV compared to the control group. Furthermore, the intervention group successfully ate more fruit and vegetables and achieved the RDIFV on more days. Notably, the change in behavior was not fully explained by changes in cognitions (intention and anticipated regret), suggesting that the implementation intention component (planning the actual behaviors) played a critical, independent role in bridging the intention-behavior gap.

I have taken note of the methodology, analyses, statistical findings in the tables, and the study's discussion regarding limitations and implications for future health promotion research. I am ready for any specific questions or tasks you have regarding this systematic review process.
