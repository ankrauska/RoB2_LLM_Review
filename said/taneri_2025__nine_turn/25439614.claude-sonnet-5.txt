# Domain 3: Risk of Bias due to Missing Outcome Data

1. **3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**

   - Response: **No**
   - Reason: The article reports that "the 5-year completion rate was 76% of the 163 original participants randomized to the ranibizumab + prompt laser group and 74% of the 150 original participants randomized to the ranibizumab + deferred laser group" (excluding deaths). Additionally, Table 1 shows that out of 180 eyes originally randomized to prompt laser, only 124 (69%) completed the 5-year visit (56 non-completers, 31%), and out of 181 eyes randomized to deferred laser, only 111 (61%) completed (70 non-completers, 39%). This represents substantial missing outcome data (approximately 24-31% depending on denominator used), well exceeding the 5% threshold suggested as generally acceptable for continuous outcomes like visual acuity. This is far from "nearly all" participants.

2. **3.2. Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**

   - Response: **Probably no**
   - Reason: The authors used a longitudinal mixed model approach ("using all available visit data") and additionally adjusted for potential confounders identified as differing between completers and non-completers (baseline CSF thickness, age, prior PRP, lens status, DR severity). While this is a reasonable analytic attempt, the authors themselves acknowledge in the Discussion: "it is not possible to know whether incomplete data were related to unobserved factors that could have affected the results in either direction." Adjusting for observed baseline covariates does not correct for bias if missingness is related to the unobserved/true outcome value itself (i.e., MNAR), which the authors do not rule out. No formal sensitivity analysis testing a range of assumptions about missingness-outcome relationships is reported. Therefore, the evidence provided is not sufficient to conclude the result was not biased.

3. **3.3. Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**

   - Response: **Probably yes**
   - Reason: The stated primary reason for missing data was that participants "chose not to continue in the extension phase of the study" — a voluntary, largely unexplained reason for withdrawal. The article does not report that missingness was due to purely administrative/unrelated reasons (e.g., site closure, equipment failure). Given that DME/visual acuity trajectory could plausibly influence a participant's willingness to continue in a demanding long-term follow-up (e.g., worse vision or perceived lack of benefit could reduce motivation to continue attending frequent visits), it is plausible that missingness relates to the true visual acuity outcome.

4. **3.4. Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**

   - Response: **Probably yes**
   - Reason: Several elements from the RoB2 guidance point toward this judgment:
     - **Differential proportions of missing data between groups**: 31% non-completion in the prompt laser group vs. 39% in the deferred laser group — a notable ~8 percentage point difference between arms.
     - **Documented baseline imbalances between completers and non-completers**: The article explicitly states, "Some imbalances between completers versus non-completers were noted (age, prior PRP, lens status, diabetic retinopathy severity level)" (Table 1). This indicates that non-completers were not missing at random with respect to prognostic factors known to influence visual/anatomic outcomes in DME (e.g., DR severity, prior PRP status), raising concern that those lost to follow-up systematically differed in ways that could correlate with visual acuity trajectory.
     - No clear evidence is presented that these baseline differences were adequately accounted for to eliminate the relationship between missingness and outcome (per the caveat in 3.2).

---

**Justification**: Following the RoB 2 algorithm: 3.1 = No (substantial missing data, ~24-39% depending on group and denominator, far exceeding the 5% benchmark for continuous outcomes) → 3.2 = Probably no (adjustment for observed baseline confounders in the longitudinal model is a partial mitigation but does not constitute robust evidence against bias, as acknowledged by the authors themselves) → 3.3 = Probably yes (plausible relationship between missingness and true VA outcome, given voluntary non-consent to extension as the primary driver) → 3.4 = Probably yes (supported by both differential attrition rates between intervention groups and documented systematic baseline differences between completers and non-completers on relevant prognostic factors). ### Overall Justification
Per RoB 2 methodology, the overall risk-of-bias judgment for an outcome is determined by the **highest (worst) domain-level rating**. Domain 3 (Missing Outcome Data) was rated **High risk** due to: (a) substantial attrition (~24–39% depending on group/denominator, far exceeding acceptable thresholds), (b) differential attrition rates between the prompt and deferred laser groups, and (c) explicitly documented baseline imbalances between completers and non-completers on outcome-relevant prognostic factors (age, prior PRP, lens status, DR severity), with no robust evidence ruling out that missingness was related to the true visual acuity outcome. This single High-risk domain is sufficient to drive the overall outcome-level judgment to **High risk of bias**, despite Low-risk ratings in Domains 2, 4, and 5, and Some Concerns in Domain 1.

The direction of this bias is judged **Unpredictable**, as the article does not provide sufficient information to determine whether the differential/systematic missingness would have inflated, deflated, or had a negligible net effect on the observed treatment comparison between prompt and deferred laser groups.
