**Outcome Name: Agitation Score (Table 4)**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The authors state that participants were “randomly assigned” and that time blocks were randomized as treatment or control weeks. However, they do not report how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, drawing lots, or other random mechanism). The statement of random assignment alone is insufficient to establish that the allocation sequence contained a random component.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The article does not describe allocation concealment procedures. In particular, it does not report central allocation, an independent allocation service, sealed sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, or another method that would prevent personnel enrolling participants from knowing the forthcoming assignment. Blinding of data collectors and participants does not demonstrate concealment of the allocation sequence at enrolment/assignment.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Group sizes were relatively similar (36 intervention participants and 42 control participants). The groups did not differ statistically significantly in gender, functional stage, age, cognition, length of stay, or baseline agitation score. The intervention group had significantly greater pretest arousal imbalance than the control group (107.4 versus 84.6 minutes per 12 hours; *p* = .012). However, this represents one statistically significant difference among several reported baseline comparisons and is compatible with a chance imbalance. There is no reported substantial imbalance in the baseline agitation outcome itself. The baseline arousal-imbalance difference is a concern and was adjusted for in the analysis, but the available baseline data do not by themselves demonstrate a clearly problematic randomization process.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

- **Judgement:** Some concerns  
- **Reason:** Random allocation is asserted, but neither the method used to generate the allocation sequence nor allocation concealment is reported. Consequently, it cannot be verified that the sequence was genuinely random or that assignments were protected from foreknowledge. Although one baseline imbalance in arousal imbalance was observed, baseline data do not clearly indicate failure of randomization. The lack of information on sequence generation and concealment results in **some concerns**.

**Outcome Name: Agitation Score (Table 4)**  
**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)**

### 2.1. Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The authors state that “data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions.” Although the intervention involved changes to the daily activity schedule, the report explicitly indicates that participants were blinded. No information is provided indicating that participants became aware of their allocation.

### 2.2. Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants’ assigned intervention during the trial?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** Nursing staff delivered the BACE intervention. In Phase 2, a geriatric nurse practitioner worked with the staff nurse to create an individualized revised activity schedule, and in Phase 3 nursing staff implemented that schedule. Therefore, personnel delivering the intervention necessarily knew which participants were assigned to BACE. Allocation concealment procedures were also not reported.

### 2.3. If yes/probably yes to 2.1 or 2.2: Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** Ten participants were dropped because nursing staff did not deliver the intervention, reportedly because of short staffing, being busy, or other emergent care needs on the scheduled intervention day. These implementation problems could occur in routine long-term-care practice and are not reported to have arisen from recruitment, consent, disappointment with assignment, trial procedures, or other features specific to the trial context. No evidence is reported of control participants seeking or receiving the BACE intervention because of trial participation.

### 2.4. If yes/probably yes to 2.3: Were these deviations likely to have affected the outcome?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** This question is not applicable because deviations arising specifically from the trial context were not identified. The report does not provide evidence that any trial-context deviations affected agitation outcomes.

### 2.5. If yes/probably yes to 2.4: Were these deviations from intended intervention balanced between groups?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** This question is not applicable because there was no evidence that deviations arose because of the trial context. The article does not report comparative information on trial-context deviations between groups.

### 2.6. Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The reported analysis was not conducted according to randomized assignment for all randomized participants. Of 102 participants who entered the study, 10 were excluded because they were not given the intervention, 12 died, and 2 were excluded because of erroneous or missing data; the final analysis included 78 participants (36 BACE and 42 control). In particular, the 10 participants who did not receive the assigned intervention were removed from analysis after randomization. The authors specified that a participant had to receive at least one prescribed schedule change to be included. This is inconsistent with an intention-to-treat analysis of the effect of assignment to BACE.

### 2.7. If no/probably no to 2.6: Was there potential for a substantial impact on the result of the failure to analyse participants in the group to which they were randomized?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** Ten of 102 enrolled/randomized participants (approximately 10%) were excluded specifically because they did not receive any intervention. These exclusions appear directly related to intervention implementation and are therefore likely to have occurred among participants assigned to BACE rather than comparators. Excluding participants who could not receive the intervention—owing to staffing, competing demands, or emergent needs—could select a subgroup with different prognosis or care circumstances. The intervention effect was statistically significant but modest (*F*(1,69) = 4.26; *p* = .043), so exclusion of this number of participants could plausibly have substantially affected the estimate.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 2: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions

- **Judgement:** High  
- **Reason:** Although the reported deviations do not appear to have arisen specifically from the trial context, the analysis excluded 10 participants post-randomization because they did not receive the intervention. This is not an appropriate analysis for the effect of assignment to intervention, and the size and intervention-related nature of the exclusion create potential for a substantial effect on the reported agitation outcome.

**Outcome Name: Agitation Score (Table 4)**

### 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The study reports that 102 people participated, but the final analysis included 78 participants (36 intervention and 42 control). Thus, outcome data were unavailable for 24 participants (approximately 24%): 12 died, 10 were excluded because they did not receive the intervention, and 2 were excluded because of erroneous or missing data. This is substantially below the approximately 95% availability that would generally be considered “nearly all” for a continuous outcome.

### 3.2. If No/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The authors do not report analyses that adjusted for missing outcome data, such as an appropriate multiple-imputation analysis, nor do they report sensitivity analyses examining how different plausible outcomes among excluded participants might have affected the result. The reported repeated-measures ANCOVA was based on the final sample of 78 participants only.

### 3.3. If No/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** Twelve participants died before inclusion in the final analysis. Death in this population of people with moderate or severe dementia may be associated with health status and clinical characteristics that could also be related to observed agitation. In addition, 10 participants were excluded because the intervention was not delivered owing to short staffing, staff being busy, or emergent care needs. Such circumstances could be associated with the participant’s care needs or clinical state and could therefore be related to agitation. The report does not provide sufficient information to rule out such associations.

### 3.4. If Yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** Although it is plausible that missingness could be related to agitation or related clinical status, the article does not report outcome data, baseline characteristics, or reasons for missingness separately by randomized group or by participant. It also does not state whether deaths or exclusions were associated with agitation severity. Therefore, the available reporting does not establish that dependence of missingness on the true agitation outcome was likely, rather than merely possible.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

- **Judgement:** Some concerns  
- **Reason:** Outcome data were missing for approximately 24% of the 102 participants who entered the study, and no analysis or sensitivity analysis was reported to address potential bias from these missing data. Missingness could be associated with true agitation scores, particularly for participants who died or whose intervention could not be delivered because of emergent care needs. However, the report does not provide enough evidence to conclude that such outcome-dependent missingness was likely.

**Outcome Name: Agitation Score (Table 4)**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The study initially intended to use the Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument but reported being unable to obtain sufficiently consistent interrater reliability, particularly for non-extreme behavior. The investigators then substituted a researcher-developed visual analog agitation scale scored from 0 to 100, using intensity parameters based on number, duration, and intensity of observed behaviors. The article reports high interrater reliability after training for this scale (range .95–.984), but does not report validation of this replacement measure or evidence that it has adequate construct validity, responsiveness, or measurement properties for this population. Given that the scale is not identified as a validated instrument and was adopted because the planned established instrument could not be reliably applied, the appropriateness of the method for measuring the agitation outcome is uncertain and there is a substantial concern that it may have poor validity.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Both groups were observed according to the same described procedure: research assistants observed participants for 3 minutes every 15 minutes between 8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. on both pretest and posttest days. The same visual analog scale and agitation parameters were used. There is no indication that outcome measurement procedures, thresholds, duration, or timing differed systematically between BACE and control participants.

### 4.3. If No/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Although this question is formally not reached because of the response to Question 4.1, the study states that data collectors were blinded to treatment and control conditions. The research assistants who conducted the repeated agitation observations were therefore reported to be unaware of group assignment.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** This question is not applicable because the outcome assessors were reported to be blinded. If assessors had known allocation, the observer-rated and judgement-based agitation scale could potentially have been susceptible to influence; however, this situation is not reported.

### 4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** This question is not applicable because outcome assessors were reported to be blinded to assigned intervention.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

- **Judgement:** Some concerns  
- **Reason:** Agitation was rated by blinded research assistants using the same procedures in both groups, which reduces the risk of differential outcome ascertainment and assessor-expectancy bias. However, the analyzed agitation measure was an apparently non-validated visual analog scale developed for the study after the planned established observational measure could not achieve acceptable reliability. The article demonstrates high post-training interrater reliability but does not establish validity or responsiveness of the substitute scale. This creates **some concerns** regarding measurement of the outcome, but the information does not establish that the method was definitively inappropriate.

### Overall predicted direction of bias

- **Judgement:** Favours experimental  
- **Reason:** The principal high-risk issue is exclusion after randomization of 10 participants because they did not receive the BACE intervention. These exclusions were linked to failure to implement the experimental intervention and therefore are likely to have occurred in the BACE group. Removing participants for whom BACE could not be delivered—because staff were short-staffed, busy, or managing emergent needs—may make the analyzed intervention group appear more favourable than the group as randomized. This could exaggerate the estimated reduction in agitation associated with assignment to BACE.

- **Judgement:** High

In addition, there are **some concerns** regarding the randomization process, missing outcome data, measurement of agitation, and selection of the reported result.
