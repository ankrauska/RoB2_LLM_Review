### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** The article states that, for each participant, one eye was assigned to the aspheric ablation profile (ASA) and the fellow eye to the non-aspheric profile (TSA) “at random using Microsoft Excel’s random number function.” This describes a computer-generated random component in sequence generation.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** Although the study reports use of Microsoft Excel’s random number function, it does not report who generated the allocation sequence, when it was generated, who had access to it, or any procedure for concealment from the surgeon or personnel enrolling/assigning participants. There is no description of centralized allocation, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another concealment method.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The intervention groups each included 15 eyes, consistent with the paired-eye design. The reported preoperative spherical equivalent refraction was similar between groups: −2.43 ± 1.53 D for ASA and −2.54 ± 1.47 D for TSA, with no statistically significant difference (*p* = 0.87). The mean between-eye spherical-equivalent difference was 0.208 D. No reported baseline imbalance suggests a problem with randomization. However, baseline visual-acuity data by allocated eye are not specifically reported.

### Domain 1 risk-of-bias judgement: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** The random sequence generation was adequately described, and the reported baseline refractive data do not indicate an imbalance. However, allocation concealment was not described. Therefore, it is not possible to determine whether the forthcoming eye-level allocation could have been known before intervention assignment.
