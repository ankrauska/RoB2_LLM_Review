**Outcome Name: all-cause death or nonfatal heart failure events**

### 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The primary outcome was analysed in all randomized participants according to the intention-to-treat principle, but complete follow-up for the time-to-event outcome was not available for every participant. A total of 44/1089 (4.0%) participants in the CRT–ICD group and 55/731 (7.5%) in the ICD-only group declined further participation, were withdrawn by a physician, or were lost to follow-up. Thus, 99/1820 (5.4%) randomized participants had incomplete outcome follow-up. The number with incomplete follow-up is not negligible in relation to the observed treatment difference, and the rates differed between groups.

### 3.2. If no/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The report does not describe statistical methods specifically intended to address possible bias due to missing outcome data, such as inverse-probability-of-censoring methods, multiple-imputation methods based on appropriate prognostic variables, or sensitivity analyses under plausible informative-missingness assumptions. The use of Kaplan–Meier and Cox proportional-hazards analyses does not itself demonstrate that censoring was non-informative.

### 3.3. If no/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The incomplete follow-up included participants who declined continued participation, were withdrawn by a physician, or were lost to follow-up. For a composite outcome of death or heart-failure event, these reasons could be related to health status, worsening heart failure, treatment experience, or prognosis. In addition, this is a time-to-event analysis, so participants with incomplete follow-up were censored before the planned common trial end date. The report does not establish that censoring was unrelated to future risk of death or heart failure.

### 3.4. If yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The proportion with incomplete follow-up differed between groups: 7.5% in the ICD-only group versus 4.0% in the CRT–ICD group. This differential censoring, combined with reasons that may plausibly relate to health status (physician withdrawal, participant discontinuation, or loss to follow-up), makes informative missingness plausible. The authors provide neither group-specific reasons for incomplete follow-up nor analyses demonstrating that the primary result was robust to different assumptions about outcomes among censored participants.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

- **Judgement: High**
- **Reason:** Outcome follow-up was incomplete for 5.4% of randomized participants and was more frequent in the ICD-only group. No analysis or sensitivity analysis was reported to address potential bias from missing time-to-event outcome data. Because the reasons for censoring could be related to the risk of death or heart failure, and censoring was differential between groups, missingness was probably related to the true outcome.

**Outcome Name: all-cause death or nonfatal heart failure events**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The composite outcome comprised all-cause death or a nonfatal heart-failure event. All-cause mortality is an objective and appropriate outcome measure. Heart-failure events were defined using prespecified clinical criteria: signs and symptoms consistent with congestive heart failure plus a need for intravenous decongestive therapy in an outpatient setting or augmented oral/parenteral decongestive treatment during an in-hospital stay. Events were adjudicated by an independent heart-failure committee that was unaware of treatment assignment. These methods are appropriate for measuring the specified outcome.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Participants in both groups were scheduled for clinical follow-up and device testing at 1 month after randomization and every 3 months thereafter. The same prespecified heart-failure-event definition was used in both groups, and the report does not describe different outcome-assessment schedules, thresholds, or systematic methods of event ascertainment by intervention group. Although the interventions and device follow-up differed clinically, there is no direct evidence that outcome ascertainment procedures differed between groups.

### 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The report explicitly states that heart-failure events were diagnosed by physicians who were aware of treatment assignment and that treating physicians were aware of study-group assignments. Although the independent mortality and heart-failure adjudication committees were unaware of treatment assignments, the initial clinical diagnosis and the treatment/admission decisions underlying the definition of a heart-failure event were made by unblinded treating physicians.

### 4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The nonfatal heart-failure component involved clinical judgement: determining whether symptoms and signs were consistent with congestive heart failure and whether escalation of decongestive treatment or hospital admission was warranted. Treating physicians who made these clinical decisions knew the assigned treatment. Such knowledge could influence diagnostic and management decisions relevant to whether a heart-failure event met the trial definition. All-cause death itself is unlikely to be affected by assessment knowledge, but heart-failure events accounted for most primary-outcome events.

### 4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The article acknowledges that knowledge of treatment assignment by investigators “contributed in some way” to the lower heart-failure-event frequency in the CRT–ICD group as a possibility. However, heart-failure events were subsequently adjudicated by an independent committee unaware of treatment assignment, using prespecified criteria. The report does not provide evidence showing that unblinded clinicians actually applied different diagnostic or treatment thresholds between groups. Therefore, influence is possible but not shown to be likely.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

- **Judgement: Some concerns**
- **Reason:** The outcome definitions and blinded adjudication process were appropriate, and data-collection schedules appear comparable. However, the primary composite was driven largely by heart-failure events, whose initial diagnosis and management-based qualification were determined by unblinded treating physicians. Knowledge of device assignment could have influenced those decisions. There is no direct evidence that this occurred, so the concern is not sufficient for a high-risk judgement.
