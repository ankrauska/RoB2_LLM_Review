Outcome Name: shared decision making score

1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?
• Response: Yes
• Reason: The authors state that "Randomization was performed by means of a computer-generated random sequence with stratification for age, gender, and positive family history for CHD using the Pocock and Simon method." 

1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?
• Response: Probably no
• Reason: The article states that "One of the investigators (HJ) generated the random allocation sequence and study arm assignment using the computer software described earlier." There is no mention of a centralized or remote randomization process, nor the use of sealed opaque envelopes or other concealment mechanisms to prevent the investigator or enrolling personnel from knowing the forthcoming allocation. Because a study investigator managed the sequence and assignments, concealment cannot be assured.

1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?
• Response: No
• Reason: Table 1 shows that baseline characteristics (including age, sex, smoking status, BMI, and other cardiovascular risk factors) were similar between the conventional risk score (n=103) and genetics-informed risk (n=104) groups. All p-values for baseline differences are >0.05, and group sizes are almost identical, indicating no apparent imbalances that would suggest a problem with the randomization process.

Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Some concerns
(Reason: While the allocation sequence was adequately generated and baseline characteristics were balanced, the lack of information on allocation concealment and the fact that a study investigator managed the allocation sequence raises some concerns about potential bias.)

Outcome Name: shared decision making score

4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?
• Response: No
• Reason: The shared decision-making score was measured using the Shared Decision-Making Questionnaire (SDM-Q), which the authors describe as "a validated 11-question survey that assesses patients' perception of the SDM process during clinical encounters." This is an appropriate and validated method for assessing this outcome.

4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?
• Response: No
• Reason: Both groups underwent the same study visit procedures (meeting with a genetic counselor for 30 minutes, followed by a physician visit) and were administered the same survey to measure the outcome at the same point in time. 

4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?
• Response: Yes
• Reason: For patient-reported outcomes like the SDM-Q, the outcome assessor is the study participant. The participants were aware of the intervention because they either received conventional risk information or a combination of conventional and genetic risk information during their counseling and physician visits.

4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?
• Response: Probably yes
• Reason: The shared decision making score is a subjective, self-reported measure. Participants' perceptions of their inclusion in decision-making and the quality of their consultation could theoretically be influenced by their knowledge that they were receiving novel, personalized genetic risk information compared to standard care.

4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?
• Response: Probably no
• Reason: While the participants were not blinded and the outcome is subjective, there is no strong reason to believe their knowledge systematically biased the assessment. The survey scores were very high and similar across both groups. Furthermore, a secondary quantitative analysis of a random subset of video-recorded encounters using an objective observer scale (OPTION5) yielded similar results (no significant difference between groups), suggesting the self-reported scores were representative of the actual encounters rather than heavily biased by intervention knowledge. 

Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: Some concerns
(Reason: Because the outcome is self-reported by participants who were aware of their assigned intervention, it is possible that this knowledge could have influenced their subjective assessment, which typically leads to "some concerns" in trials with patient-reported outcomes lacking blinding, even if there is no strong evidence that it actually did.)
