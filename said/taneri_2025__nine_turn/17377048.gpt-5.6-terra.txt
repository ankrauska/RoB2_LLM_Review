**Outcome Name: Agatston score**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Agatston scoring is an established method for quantifying coronary artery calcification. The study used the same predefined calcium criterion across reconstructions: more than two contiguous pixels with attenuation greater than 130 HU. The article does not provide evidence that the measurement method was invalid or incapable of detecting plausible effects of the reconstruction protocols.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Both randomized groups underwent coronary calcium scoring using the same scanner, reconstructed slice thickness (3 mm), field of view, matrix, pixel size, ECG-gated acquisition approach, reconstruction intervals, reconstruction increments, calcium definition (> 130 HU in more than two contiguous pixels), and workstation/software. Agatston scores were obtained for both groups using the same outcome-measurement procedure.

### 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The article states that a radiologist and cardiologist differentiated calcium from noise by placing regions of interest around coronary calcifications, but it does not state that these assessors were blinded to group assignment or to the reconstruction protocol. The intervention was a technical CT acquisition setting (64 × 0.6-mm versus 20 × 1.2-mm collimation), and the assessors evaluated the reconstructed CT datasets. In the absence of reported blinding, awareness of the reconstruction/acquisition protocol is plausible.

### 4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** Although the Agatston score was software-assisted and used a prespecified attenuation threshold, outcome assessment included human judgement: the radiologist and cardiologist differentiated true coronary calcium from image noise and placed regions of interest around calcified lesions. Knowledge of the acquisition protocol could potentially influence these subjective decisions, particularly for small, borderline, or artifact-affected lesions.

### 4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** The report does not provide evidence that assessors preferentially classified lesions, placed regions of interest differently, or otherwise made systematically different scoring decisions according to assigned collimation group. In addition, the scoring process used an objective attenuation criterion and automated lesion identification before manual review. Nevertheless, assessor blinding was not reported.

### Domain 4 overall risk-of-bias judgement: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** Outcome measurement procedures were comparable and based substantially on standardized, software-assisted Agatston scoring. However, manual differentiation of calcium from noise was required, and blinding of the radiologist and cardiologist assessing the images was not reported. Their knowledge of the acquisition protocol could potentially have influenced subjective elements of outcome assessment, although there is no evidence that such influence actually occurred.
