# Domain 2: Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions

**Outcome Name:** effects of supervision on patient care, measured by a team checklist score

**Effect of Interest Selected: Effect of Assignment to Intervention**

*Rationale for this choice:* This trial compares three levels of clinical supervision (direct, immediately available, distant) defined by physical proximity of the supervisor. By the very nature of this intervention, blinding of participants (residents) and intervention deliverers (fellows) is impossible—all parties must know whether the supervisor is physically present, on-call nearby, or remote, in order for the intervention to be implemented at all. This is analogous to an open-label trial. Per the RoB 2 guidance, the "effect of assignment to intervention" is the appropriate framework for this analysis, as it allows for such inherently unblindable interventions to be judged fairly.

---

## 2.1. Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** Residents were directly aware of their supervision condition, as the level of supervision was defined by physical proximity of the supervisor: "direct (supervisor in the simulation room with the resident from the beginning of the scenario), immediately available (supervisor initially available on the phone but able to be at the bedside within 1 min when needed), and distant (supervisor only available on the phone for the duration of the scenario)." - **Response: Yes**

- **Response: Probably no**

However, this variability appears to be an *inherent feature of the intervention design itself* (i.e., "immediately available" and "distant" supervision are explicitly defined by resident-initiated contact), rather than a deviation from the protocol arising from the trial context (e.g., participants seeking non-protocol care due to feeling disadvantaged by randomization). ## 2.6. Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The analysis appears to include all randomized participants analyzed according to their assigned group. Table 2 reports results for all 53 residents split by their assigned supervision condition (Direct n=18, Immediately Available n=16, Distant n=19), and the statistical method described ("we conducted a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the team checklist scores of the supervised scenario with the level of clinical supervision... as the independent variable") reflects an intention-to-treat-consistent approach, with no evidence of post-randomization exclusions or per-protocol/as-treated analysis strategies for this outcome.

---

### **Low**

**Justification:** Although both participants (residents) and intervention deliverers (fellows) were necessarily aware of the assigned supervision condition (2.1 = Yes, 2.2 = Yes), this is an unavoidable and expected feature of this type of intervention (open-label by design) rather than a source of bias in itself. Additionally, the analysis appropriately included all randomized participants according to their assigned group (2.6 = Yes), consistent with an intention-to-treat approach. Given the absence of problematic deviations and the use of an appropriate analytic strategy, this domain is judged to be at **low risk of bias**.

# Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**Outcome Name:** effects of supervision on patient care, measured by a team checklist score

## 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The team checklist score was based on predetermined, scenario-specific clinical tasks (e.g., specific interventions relevant to increased intracranial pressure, distributive shock, hypoxemic respiratory failure), with each item scored dichotomously as done/not done ("A checklist task was considered done if performed by any member of the team"). This method is objective and directly relevant to measuring clinical care processes, and is sensitive to detecting differences in care since it reflects concrete completion of relevant tasks. However, the authors themselves acknowledge in the Discussion that "we recognize the limitations associated with the use of a nonvalidated assessment tool, such as our scenario-specific checklists... The checklist development process ensured a high face and content validity, but we did not assess its construct validity." Despite this acknowledged limitation, there is no strong evidence that the tool was insensitive to plausible intervention effects — in fact, it detected a significant difference between groups (p=0.0013). Therefore "Probably no" is judged rather than "Yes/Probably yes."

## 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The same checklist and scoring method (task counted as complete if performed by any team member) was applied uniformly across all three supervision conditions. There is no indication that measurement thresholds or methods varied by group, and the outcome (team checklist score) was assessed using the same videotaped review process for all scenarios regardless of supervision level.

## 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The article states that "Two independent raters were trained to use the global rating scale and the checklists... The raters then independently reviewed and rated all the videotaped performance of the participants." Critically, the intervention itself (level of supervision) is defined by the physical proximity of the supervisor within the simulation room, which would be directly and unavoidably visible in the videotapes (e.g., a supervisor present in the room from the start versus supervisor absent). There is no mention of any blinding procedure for raters, nor is it plausible that raters could be blinded to group assignment given the visibility of supervisor presence/absence on video. Therefore, outcome assessors were aware of the intervention received.

## 4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** Although the checklist is largely objective (task done/not done), coding whether an action was adequately "performed" or met the checklist criteria involves some degree of rater judgment. Given that raters could clearly observe which supervision condition was in effect (based on physical presence of the supervisor), there is potential for their judgment of task completion to be subtly influenced by expectations regarding supervision level (e.g., expecting more complete care under direct supervision).

## 4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** While influence was possible (per 4.4), there is no direct evidence in the article suggesting that assessors' knowledge of the supervision condition actually biased their scoring. The checklist items were predetermined and based on specific, objective clinical actions relevant to each scenario, reducing subjectivity. Additionally, the study employed a structured rating process with "a two-step process including 1) the independent review of three videotapes... and 2) participation in a round of score calibration," and discrepancies were resolved by "a third independent assessor who reviewed the videotapes to confirm whether an item had been completed or not." This structured, calibrated approach provides some reassurance against strong assessor bias, though it does not eliminate the possibility entirely. There is no evidence of strong prior expectations or beliefs by the assessors that would clearly bias results in one direction.

---

## Domain-Level Risk-of-Bias Judgement for Domain 4:

### **Some concerns**

**Justification:** The outcome assessors (video raters) were not blinded to the intervention group, as the level of supervision (physical proximity of supervisor) was inherently visible in the videotaped scenarios (4.3 = Yes). This creates the potential for assessment of the checklist score—which involves some degree of judgment in coding task completion—to be influenced by knowledge of the supervision condition (4.4 = Probably yes). However, there is no direct evidence in the article that this knowledge actually influenced the results, and the use of a structured, calibrated checklist with a third-party adjudicator for discrepancies mitigates some of this concern (4.5 = Probably no). Per the RoB 2 algorithm, this pattern of responses (assessors aware, potential for influence, but no clear evidence of actual bias) corresponds to a judgment of **"Some concerns"** rather than "High risk," reflecting the unavoidable lack of blinding inherent to this type of intervention combined with the absence of confirmed biased assessment.
