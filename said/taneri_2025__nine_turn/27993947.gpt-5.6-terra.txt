### Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?**

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** The article explicitly states that “randomization was performed by means of a computer-generated random sequence,” using the Pocock and Simon method, with stratification for age, sex, and positive family history of CHD. Computer-generated allocation with minimization/stratification includes a random element and meets the criterion for a random allocation sequence.

**1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** Participants completed the first study visit and scheduled the second visit before randomization occurred. The article states that one investigator generated the random allocation sequence and study-arm assignment using computer software. Thus, allocation occurred after enrolment/baseline assessment, which reduces the likelihood that allocation could have influenced enrolment. However, the report does not explicitly describe allocation concealment procedures, such as a centralized randomization system inaccessible to recruiting personnel, password protection, or an independent allocation service. Therefore, “Yes” is not fully supported, but concealment was probably adequate based on the reported timing and computerized assignment.

**1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The randomized group sizes were nearly equal (103 assigned to conventional risk score [CRS] and 104 to CRS plus genetic risk score [CRS+GRS]). Baseline demographic and cardiovascular risk characteristics were similar, with no statistically significant differences reported: for example, age (p=0.91), sex (p=0.78), smoking history (p=0.19), family history of CHD (p=0.43), BMI (p=0.79), systolic blood pressure (p=0.81), lipid measures (p values 0.47–0.86), and college education or higher (p=0.25). There is no apparent imbalance suggesting a problem with the randomization process.

### Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

**4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Shared decision making was measured using the 11-item Shared Decision-Making Questionnaire (SDM-Q), which the article describes as a validated instrument for assessing patients’ perceptions of shared decision making during clinical encounters. The same questionnaire and scoring approach were used in both randomized groups. There is no reported evidence that the instrument lacked validity or sensitivity for this outcome.

**4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Both groups completed the same participant-reported SDM-Q after the risk-disclosure and physician encounters. The article does not report different survey instruments, outcome definitions, assessment procedures, or assessment time points between the CRS and CRS+GRS groups.

**4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** The SDM-Q is a participant-reported outcome; therefore, participants were the outcome assessors. Participants necessarily knew the information they received: the CRS+GRS group received genetic-risk information, whereas the CRS group received conventional-risk information without disclosure of the genetic risk score. The trial was unmasked.

**4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** The outcome was participants’ subjective perception of the shared decision-making process during the encounters. Such perceptions can be influenced by awareness that genetic information was, or was not, disclosed, including participants’ expectations of the perceived value, novelty, or complexity of genetic-risk information.

**4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The intervention was delivered openly and consisted of disclosure of additional genetic-risk information during the very clinical encounters assessed by the participant-reported SDM-Q. Since the outcome is subjective and directly concerns participants’ perceptions of the communication and decision-making process, knowledge of receiving genetic versus conventional risk information was likely to affect how participants evaluated the encounter. No participant blinding or other measure to mitigate this risk is reported.
