**2.7. Was there potential for a substantial impact (on the result) of the failure to analyze participants in the group to which they were randomized?**

**4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The same measurement protocols (qualitative scoring scales, standardized ROI placement rules, identical SNR/CNR/contour sharpness formulas) were applied consistently to images from both scanner groups. There is no indication that the measurement/ascertainment method itself (as opposed to true underlying image characteristics) was applied differently depending on group assignment.

**4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The article states explicitly: "At the time of the analysis the examiners were blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity." However, I am critically skeptical of the *effectiveness* of this blinding. The two scanners differ fundamentally in field strength, gradient strength, coil design (solenoid coil for open scanner vs. integrated phased-array coil for short-bore scanner), and field orientation (vertical vs. horizontal). These technical differences are highly likely to produce visually distinguishable image characteristics (e.g., differing noise textures, FOV geometry, coil-related signal patterns, and overall SNR appearance) that would allow trained examiners to infer which scanner produced a given image, even without being explicitly told. Given that the entire premise of the study is that images from these two scanners look measurably different, nominal blinding is unlikely to have been effectively maintained in practice for many/most images.

**4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The primary outcome includes qualitative grading (overall image quality, contrast, contour sharpness), which is a subjective, judgment-based assessment performed by human examiners. Subjective ratings of this nature are inherently susceptible to influence by assessor expectations if the scanner identity is inferred, unlike objective outcomes such as mortality.

**4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** There is a strong pre-existing expectation in the literature—explicitly acknowledged and cited by the authors themselves in the Discussion ("Several other studies have compared the image quality of conventional closed MR scanners and found superior image quality for higher field strengths")—that short-bore, higher-field scanners produce superior image quality compared to open, lower-field scanners. If examiners could infer scanner type from visual cues (as argued in 4.3), this well-established prior expectation could plausibly have influenced their subjective qualitative ratings, particularly for borderline cases between adjacent categories (e.g., "good" vs. "moderate"). This risk is compounded by the fact that qualitative assessment was performed by only two examiners in consensus (not a larger panel with formal inter-rater reliability reporting to detect systematic drift), and no strategy (e.g., anonymized/randomized image presentation order, use of images with matching artificial noise patterns to disguise scanner origin) is reported to mitigate this specific risk beyond a general blinding statement.

**Justification:** Although the measurement methods themselves were appropriate and consistently applied (4.1 = No, 4.2 = No), there are substantial concerns regarding the effectiveness of outcome assessor blinding. Despite the authors' stated intent to blind examiners, the inherent and substantial visual/technical differences between images produced by the two fundamentally different scanner platforms (differing field strength, coil type, and field orientation) make it likely that assessors could infer scanner identity (4.3 = Probably yes). Because part of the outcome relies on subjective qualitative judgment, and there is a well-documented pre-existing expectation in the field favoring short-bore/high-field scanners, knowledge of intervention could plausibly have influenced these subjective ratings (4.4 = Probably yes, 4.5 = Probably yes). ## Overall Predicted Direction of Bias: **Favours comparator**

**Reasoning:** Per the trial registration, the "open MRI scanner" is designated as the **Experimental** arm and the "short-bore/closed MRI scanner" is designated as the **Active Comparator**. The most influential source of bias identified—Domain 4 (measurement of the outcome)—has a clear, predictable direction. The authors themselves note in the Discussion that pre-existing literature consistently reports "superior image quality for higher field strengths" in closed/short-bore high-field scanners. Since:

1. Outcome assessors could plausibly infer scanner identity despite nominal blinding (due to fundamentally different image characteristics between open vertical-field and short-bore horizontal-field systems), and
2. There is a well-documented prior expectation in the field favoring short-bore/high-field scanners,

any influence of unblinding on the *subjective qualitative ratings* would systematically favor the short-bore scanner (the comparator), reinforcing rather than contradicting the direction of the observed result. This is a predictable, non-random direction of bias — not merely "away from null" in a generic sense, but specifically **toward the comparator (short-bore MRI)**, which happens to align with the study's actual reported findings (short-bore superior on all parameters). This raises concern that the magnitude of the observed superiority of short-bore imaging may be inflated by measurement bias, in addition to the compounding uncertainties introduced by Domains 2 and 3 (cross-referral/as-treated analysis and differential missingness related to claustrophobia/motion).

## Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement: **High**

**Justification:** Per the RoB 2 algorithm, an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" is warranted if **any single domain** is judged to be at high risk of bias, OR if multiple domains are judged to have "Some concerns" in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result. In this assessment, **three of the five domains** (Domain 2, Domain 3, and Domain 4) were independently judged to be at **High risk of bias**:

- **Domain 2 (High):** Substantial cross-referral (27% of participants) analyzed on an "as-treated" basis rather than by original random assignment, with plausible differential impact on outcome.
- **Domain 3 (High):** ~12% of the relevant randomized population had no outcome data at all, with a plausible mechanistic link (claustrophobia → motion → poor image quality) between the reason for missingness and the true value of the outcome, and no correction analysis performed.
- **Domain 4 (High):** Effectiveness of assessor blinding is doubtful given inherent technical/visual differences between the two scanner platforms, combined with a documented prior expectation in the literature that likely aligns with and could exaggerate the observed result.

Given that three domains independently trigger a "High" rating, and the predicted direction of bias systematically reinforces the study's main conclusion (short-bore superiority), the overall risk-of-bias judgement for the outcome of **image quality parameters** is:

# **High**
