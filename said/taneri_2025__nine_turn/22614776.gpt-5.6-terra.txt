**Outcome Name: peer aggression 12 months**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The article states that eligible participants “were randomly selected” and “assigned to 1 of the 3 study conditions,” with randomization stratified by gender and age group (14–15 versus 16–18 years). However, it does not report how the allocation sequence itself was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, block randomization, or another random mechanism). The trial registry likewise reports randomized allocation but provides no sequence-generation method.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The report does not describe allocation concealment procedures. In particular, it does not state whether assignment was conducted through a centralized/remote randomization system, by an independent party, or through sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. Therefore, it cannot be determined whether personnel enrolling participants could have anticipated or influenced the forthcoming assignment.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The authors report no significant differences between conditions in age, race, or gender. They report an initial between-condition difference in school dropout, but this is a single baseline difference and does not, by itself, constitute an excess of differences beyond that expected by chance. Baseline prevalence of severe peer aggression was somewhat higher in the therapist group (82.7%) than in the computer (75.5%) and control (77.9%) groups, but the reported data do not demonstrate a large or clearly implausible imbalance indicating failure of randomization. Group sizes also appear reasonably similar (total randomized sample: 726 participants across three groups).

### Domain 1 risk-of-bias judgement: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** Random assignment and stratification were reported, and baseline data do not clearly suggest a flawed randomization process. However, the methods used to generate the random allocation sequence and to conceal allocation until assignment were not reported. These omissions prevent confirmation that the randomization process adequately prevented selection bias.

**Outcome Name: peer aggression 12 months**

### 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Of 726 randomized participants, 607 completed the 12-month follow-up (83.6%); thus, outcome data were missing for 119 participants (16.4%). For this dichotomous outcome, this is not a sufficiently small number to conclude that missing outcomes, regardless of their values, could not have made an important difference to the intervention-effect estimate.

### 3.2. If no/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The authors used generalized estimating equations and state that these analyses used all available data, including data from participants lost to attrition. However, an available-case GEE analysis does not itself demonstrate that missingness did not bias the result. The report provides no sensitivity analysis under plausible missing-data assumptions and no analysis specifically designed to correct for potentially informative missing outcome data.

### 3.3. If no/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** Peer aggression was self-reported at follow-up. In a trial involving adolescents with alcohol use and aggressive behavior, loss to follow-up could plausibly be related to the participant’s ongoing behavioral risks, including peer aggression. The article does not report documented reasons for all missing 12-month assessments that would establish that missingness was unrelated to the outcome.

### 3.4. If yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** Although outcome-dependent missingness is plausible, the report does not provide evidence that it was likely. In particular, it does not report differential 12-month follow-up rates by randomized group, differential reasons for nonresponse by group, or characteristics of participants with missing versus observed peer-aggression data that would indicate that missingness was related to true peer aggression. The overall follow-up proportion was reasonably high (83.6%), but not sufficiently high to eliminate concern.

### Domain 3 risk-of-bias judgement: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** Outcome data were missing for 16.4% of randomized participants. The available-case GEE approach does not establish that the result was robust to missing data, and missing peer-aggression reports could plausibly be associated with participants’ true aggression status. However, there is insufficient evidence to conclude that such outcome-dependent missingness was likely.

**Outcome Name: peer aggression 12 months**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** Peer aggression was measured using items from the Conflict Tactics Scale, assessing severe past-year aggression toward peers (including hitting or punching, serious physical fighting, and use of a knife or gun). The outcome was assessed using the same computerized self-administered approach at baseline and 12 months. The report does not provide evidence that this measure lacked validity or sensitivity to detect plausible changes in peer aggression.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** The 12-month outcome was collected using a self-administered computerized survey, using the same outcome definition and assessment approach across randomized groups. Follow-up surveys were completed at the ED or another convenient location. The report does not indicate that intervention groups had different outcome-assessment schedules, instruments, thresholds, or opportunities for peer aggression to be identified.

### 4.3. If no/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** Peer aggression was a participant-reported outcome; therefore, the participant was the outcome assessor. Participants were aware whether they had received the therapist-assisted intervention, computer intervention, or control brochure. Although the trial registry indicates that outcome assessors were masked, this cannot blind participants reporting their own aggression.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** This was a self-reported behavioral outcome concerning aggressive acts over the previous year. Knowledge of having received an intervention specifically intended to reduce violence could influence how participants recall, interpret, or report aggressive behavior. This outcome is therefore susceptible to influence from awareness of intervention assignment.

### 4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** Participants in the therapist intervention received a motivational interviewing and skills-training intervention designed explicitly to reduce violence, whereas control participants received a community-resources brochure. Severe peer aggression is a socially undesirable, participant-reported behavior, and participants were not blinded to their intervention. These circumstances make it likely that knowledge of assignment could have influenced reporting at 12 months. The use of computerized self-administration and confidentiality protections may reduce, but do not eliminate, this concern.

### Domain 4 risk-of-bias judgement: **High**

- **Reason:** Although the peer-aggression measure and data-collection methods appear comparable across groups, the outcome was self-reported by unblinded participants. Given the behavioral, socially sensitive outcome and the clear contrast between a violence-focused therapist intervention and brochure control, reporting of peer aggression was likely influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

### 5.2. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** The article defines peer aggression in advance as a binary measure of any past-year severe peer aggression based on four Conflict Tactics Scale items. It reports all three stated peer-violence outcomes at 12 months: severe peer aggression, peer victimization, and violence consequences. The report notes that analyses focused on dichotomous outcomes because intervention effects at 3 and 6 months had been observed using dichotomous rather than continuous versions of outcomes. This provides a stated rationale for the reported form of the aggression outcome. Although the trial registry’s outcome description is broad and a full prespecified analysis plan is unavailable, there is no direct evidence that the severe-peer-aggression result was selected from multiple measures because it was favorable.

### 5.3. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** The article describes the analytical approach used for the 12-month outcomes: generalized estimating equation models testing group-by-time interactions, comparing each intervention group with control, using an intention-to-treat approach. It reports results for both intervention conditions against control. The authors acknowledge that outcomes could be examined continuously or dichotomously, but state that the 12-month analysis focused on dichotomous outcomes to assess maintenance of effects identified at 6 months. There is no direct evidence that alternative eligible analyses were conducted and that only a favorable analysis was reported.

### Domain 5 risk-of-bias judgement: **Low**

- **Reason:** The trial was prospectively registered and specifies violent behavior outcomes at 12 months. The article presents a stated a priori hypothesis, defines the severe peer-aggression outcome, and reports the planned intervention-versus-control GEE comparisons. Although a fully dated statistical analysis plan was not available, there is no indication that the peer-aggression result or its analysis was selectively reported on the basis of its findings.
