### Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

**1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?**

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The report states that patients were “randomly assigned” after stratification into three income categories. However, it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, envelopes, coin toss, or other random component). The statement of random assignment alone is insufficient to establish that the sequence itself was random.

**1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The article states that patients were assigned “in a patient-blinded fashion,” but this describes participant blinding rather than concealment from the person enrolling/assigning participants. There is no report of central randomization, telephone allocation, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or another procedure ensuring that the forthcoming allocation was concealed before enrolment and assignment.

**1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The groups had equal numbers at randomization (31 participants each) and baseline characteristics were closely comparable, including age, sex, education, income rank, insurance status, diabetes type, duration of diabetes, years of insulin treatment, baseline SBGM frequency, baseline HbA1c, random glucose, insulin dose, and number of daily insulin injections. The authors report that the groups were well matched. Although the control group had a numerically higher insulin dose at baseline, this difference was reported as not statistically significant and does not, on its own, suggest a failure of randomization.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1 (items 1.1–1.3): Some concerns**

- **Reason:** There is no reported method for generating the random allocation sequence and no information demonstrating allocation concealment. Baseline balance does not indicate a problem with randomization, but it cannot resolve the lack of information about sequence generation and concealment.

**Outcome Name: SBGM frequency and HbA1c**

### Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

**3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Sixty-two participants were randomized (31 per group). At the end of the 12-month study, 25 participants remained in the no-cost group and 16 remained in the control group. Therefore, outcome data at the end of follow-up were unavailable for 6/31 (19%) participants in the no-cost group and 15/31 (48%) participants in the control group, or 21/62 (34%) overall. This is not “nearly all” participants. The reported intention-to-treat analysis used last observation carried forward (LOCF), but imputed observations are considered missing outcome data for this signalling question.

**3.2. If No/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The authors used LOCF for the intention-to-treat analysis. LOCF does not adequately demonstrate that missing-data bias was avoided, because it assumes that each participant’s outcome would remain unchanged after withdrawal. The report does not describe sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-data assumptions, multiple imputation using relevant predictors, or another analytical approach that could address bias resulting from the substantial and differential missing outcome data.

**3.3. If No/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** Missingness could plausibly be related to the outcomes. The authors reported substantially more dropout in the control group (48%) than in the no-cost group (19%) and suggested that participants who regarded strip cost as problematic may have dropped out. Cost-related difficulty obtaining strips is directly related to the ability to perform SBGM, which was an outcome of the study, and may also be related to subsequent glycaemic control. Reasons for individual withdrawals and their relationship to SBGM frequency or HbA1c were not reported sufficiently to exclude outcome-related missingness.

**3.4. If Yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** There was a very large differential in dropout between randomized groups: 15/31 (48%) control participants versus 6/31 (19%) no-cost participants did not remain through the study. The article specifically links the high control-group dropout to problems with the cost of testing strips. Since inability or unwillingness to obtain strips is closely related to SBGM frequency, and SBGM frequency was associated with HbA1c in the study, it is likely that the missing outcomes were related to participants’ actual monitoring behaviour and potentially their glycaemic control. No sensitivity analysis was reported to establish that the findings were robust to this differential missingness.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3 (missing outcome data): High risk of bias**

- **Reason:** Missing outcome data were substantial overall (34%) and markedly differential between groups (19% no-cost versus 48% control). LOCF was used but does not adequately address potential bias. The reported pattern and suggested cost-related reason for control-group withdrawal make it likely that missingness was related to SBGM frequency and potentially HbA1c.

### Domain 5: Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

**5.1. Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available for analysis?**

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The article reports no trial registration, publicly available protocol, or statistical analysis plan finalized before outcome data were available. Although the authors state that no analysis occurred until participants completed the study, this does not establish that the analysis methods, outcomes, time points, and handling of missing data were pre-specified before unblinded outcome data were available.

**5.2. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?**

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The study measured the outcomes repeatedly at baseline and at 2-month intervals over 12 months. For HbA1c, the authors report an overall group difference, describe that the difference was “most apparent at the 6th month,” and display changes across multiple time points. Thus, there were several eligible measurement time points from which results could potentially have been selected. The report does not identify one prespecified primary time point for the between-group comparison, and no protocol or analysis plan is available to show that all intended time-point results and outcome measurements were reported irrespective of their findings. For SBGM frequency, the authors specifically report the mean frequency “during the first 4 months,” creating an additional concern that this time window may have been selected from several available follow-up periods.

**5.3. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?**

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The article reports several potentially eligible analyses: a General Linear Model incorporating baseline values and time as covariates; an intention-to-treat analysis using last observation carried forward; repeated-measures analysis of variance among completers; correlations between SBGM frequency and HbA1c; and analyses using particular follow-up periods or thresholds (e.g., SBGM at least twice daily versus less than twice daily). It is not clear which analysis generated each reported primary numerical result, nor is there a pre-specified analysis plan to establish that the reported analyses were selected in advance. The report provides no complete set of results from each stated analytical approach for SBGM frequency and HbA1c.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 5 (selection of the reported result): Some concerns**

- **Reason:** No pre-specified protocol or analysis plan is reported. The outcomes were measured repeatedly, and the report emphasizes selected time periods (first 4 months for SBGM and month 6 for HbA1c) while using multiple possible analytical approaches. This creates concern about selective reporting, but the available report does not provide direct evidence that results were selected because they were favourable; therefore, **Some concerns**, rather than high risk, is appropriate.

There are also **some concerns** regarding the randomization process (sequence generation and allocation concealment not described) and selection of the reported result (no available pre-specified protocol/analysis plan, multiple time points and analyses).
