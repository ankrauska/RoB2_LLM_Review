I've carefully read the article by Jouni et al. on the MI-GENES study examining shared decision-making (SDM) following disclosure of coronary heart disease genetic risk. Here's my understanding of the key elements for systematic review purposes:

**Study Design & Population**
- RCT (NCT01936675), Mayo Clinic Biobank-derived cohort
- 216 enrolled, 207 randomized and completed second visit (103 CRS-only vs. 104 CRS+GRS)
- Intermediate CHD risk patients, age 45-65, not on statins
- Nested within the larger MI-GENES trial (primary outcome: LDL-C, published separately in Circulation 2016)

**Intervention**
- Modified Statin Choice decision aid, EHR-linked (via GDMS/Synthesis), incorporating a 28-SNP genetic risk score (GRS) into Framingham-based conventional risk score (CRS)
- Two-step disclosure: genetic counselor (30 min) → physician visit for SDM regarding statin therapy

**Outcomes/Measures**
- SDM-Q (11-item, validated, self-report)
- OPTION5 (observer-rated, video analysis of 80/187 recorded encounters, blinded rater TSM)
- Physician visit satisfaction (6-item CAHPS-adapted survey)

**Key Findings**
- No significant between-group differences in SDM-Q total score (10.40 vs 10.61, p=0.09) or any individual item
- No difference in OPTION5 scores (70.1 vs 72.7, p=0.13)
- No difference in physician visit satisfaction (5.99 vs 5.97, p=0.32) or individual items
- High ceiling effects evident across most measures (>95% "yes" responses on many items)

**Methodological Notes Relevant to Risk-of-Bias Assessment**
- Randomization: computer-generated, stratified (Pocock-Simon) by age, sex, family history — adequate
- Allocation concealment: not explicitly detailed beyond investigator-generated sequence
- Blinding: not blinded (open-label; masking = NONE per registry) — appropriate given nature of intervention, but self-report outcomes vulnerable to social desirability/ceiling effects
- Outcome assessors for OPTION5: single rater (TSM), no inter-rater reliability reported — potential limitation
- Missing data handling: non-responders on any item excluded from SDM-Q analysis; missing individual items imputed as "0" (conservative, could bias toward null or against detecting SDM)
- Sample power: adequately powered a priori for LDL-C (primary outcome), but SDM/OPTION5/satisfaction were secondary outcomes with post-hoc calculated minimum detectable differences (0.69 points/11 for SDM-Q; 2.9/100 for OPTION5) — good transparency, though study not specifically powered for SDM as primary endpoint (risk of Type II error for subtler differences)
- Generalizability: single-center, Olmsted County MN, higher education/SES sample — limits external validity

**Points for Data Extraction Table**
- Trial registration and dates align (submitted Sept 2013, recruitment Oct 2013–May 2014)
- Video recording consent: 187/207 (90%) — subset of 80 analyzed for OPTION5 (40 matched pairs)
- No competing interests declared; Mayo IRB approved

**Potential Discussion Points for Synthesis**
- This is one of few (possibly first) trials assessing genetic risk disclosure's effect on SDM specifically (not just knowledge/anxiety/behavior)
- Findings support "no adverse effect" framing — relevant for reviews addressing whether genomic complexity undermines patient-centered care
- Ceiling effects in both SDM-Q and CAHPS-based satisfaction measures are a recurring limitation across decision-aid literature — worth flagging in any risk-of-bias/quality-of-evidence assessment (e.g., GRADE) as this limits ability to detect true differences
- Should be contrasted with other decision aid trials cited (Mann 2010, Weymiller 2007, Branda 2013) that also used Statin Choice but without genetic risk component, for comparative context in a review

Let me know what specific aspect you'd like me to focus on further — e.g., risk of bias (Cochrane RoB 2.0), GRADE assessment, data extraction into a specific template, comparison against your other included studies, or PRISMA-related synthesis language.

---

## 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

**Response: Probably No**

**Reason:** The article states: "One of the investigators (HJ) generated the random allocation sequence and study arm assignment using the computer software described earlier." Critically, the Methods section also states: "EMB and HJ recruited participants for the MI-GENES clinical trial." This indicates that the same investigator (HJ) who generated the allocation sequence was also directly involved in recruiting participants. There is no mention of any independent, centrally administered randomization service (e.g., pharmacy, telephone/web-based system), nor any use of sealed opaque envelopes or an equivalent concealment mechanism. Given that the individual generating the sequence had direct contact with recruitment, there is a reasonable basis to suspect that allocation concealment may have been compromised, even though the timing described (randomization occurring after completion of the first visit and scheduling of the second) provides some mitigation. Because the concealment mechanism is not explicitly described and the overlap in personnel roles raises legitimate concern, "Probably No" is the appropriate judgment rather than "No information" or "Probably Yes."

---

**Reason:** Table 1 reports baseline characteristics (age, gender, smoking status, family history of CHD, BMI, SBP, waist circumference, total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, education) for both groups, with all p-values ranging from 0.19 to 0.91 — none reaching statistical significance, and no pattern of excessive similarity or excessive imbalance. # RoB 2 Evaluation — Domain 4: Bias in measurement of the outcome

**Outcome Name: shared decision making score**

---

## 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

**Response: No**

**Reason:** The SDM score was measured using the SDM Questionnaire (SDM-Q), described in the article as "a validated 11-question survey that assesses patients' perception of the SDM process during clinical encounters," with a citation to its development and validation (Simon et al., 2006). This is a pre-specified, validated instrument appropriate for capturing patient-perceived SDM, and there is no indication that it is insensitive to plausible intervention effects or has demonstrated poor validity.

---

## 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

**Response: No**

**Reason:** The SDM-Q survey was administered identically to both groups, following the same sequence of events (genetic counselor visit followed by physician visit), at the same time point (immediately following the encounter, based on the study design), using the same 4-point Likert scale and scoring method (0-11) in both arms. There is no evidence of differential measurement procedures, timing, or thresholds between the CRS and CRS*GRS groups. Both groups underwent the same two-visit process with the only difference being the content of information disclosed (conventional risk only vs. conventional + genetic risk).

---

## 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

**Response: Yes**

**Reason:** The SDM score is a participant-reported outcome (self-report survey completed by the patient after the clinical encounter). Per RoB 2 guidance, "For participant-reported outcomes, the outcome assessor is the study participant." Since this is an open-label trial (masking = NONE) and participants necessarily knew which type of risk information they received (conventional risk factors alone vs. conventional risk factors plus genetic risk score, as explicitly disclosed to them during the counseling and physician visits), the outcome assessors (participants themselves) were unblinded and aware of their assigned intervention.

---

## 4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

**Response: Yes**

**Reason:** The SDM-Q is a subjective, self-reported measure of the patient's perception of the decision-making process, involving personal judgment (e.g., "My doctor and I weighed up the different treatment options thoroughly," "I felt included in the treatment decision"). Such subjective, judgment-based self-report outcomes are inherently susceptible to influence by participants' awareness of which study arm they were in — for example, patients receiving the novel genetic risk information might perceive the encounter as more thorough or personalized (or conversely, more confusing) simply because they knew they received additional/different information, independent of the actual quality of the SDM process.

---

## 4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

**Response: No**

**Reason:** While knowledge of intervention *could* have influenced participants' self-reported perceptions (as noted in 4.4), there is no specific evidence in the article suggesting strong prior beliefs, expectations, or motivations that would likely have systematically biased responses in one particular direction. The trial's own hypothesis was exploratory/comparative (whether genetic risk disclosure affects SDM), rather than testing an intervention participants would strongly expect to be superior or inferior. There is no described mechanism (e.g., strong patient expectation that genetic information would improve or worsen their decision-making experience) that would suggest a *likely* systematic influence, as opposed to a theoretical possibility. Additionally, the high ceiling effects observed across nearly all SDM-Q items (>90% "yes" responses in both arms) suggest a general response pattern (possibly acquiescence/social desirability bias) affecting both groups similarly, rather than a differential, intervention-knowledge-driven bias favoring one arm. Therefore, this is judged as a situation where knowledge of intervention *could* have influenced assessment, but there is insufficient evidence that it *did* systematically bias the outcome in a particular direction.

---

## Domain-Level Risk-of-Bias Judgement for Domain 4

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Justification:** The outcome measurement method itself was appropriate and validated (4.1 = No), and there was no differential ascertainment procedure between groups (4.2 = No). However, because the SDM score is a participant-reported, subjective/judgment-based outcome in an open-label trial, outcome assessors (participants) were necessarily aware of their intervention assignment (4.3 = Yes), and this awareness could plausibly have influenced their subjective self-report responses (4.4 = Yes). Since there is no strong evidence that this influence was *likely* to have systematically biased results in a particular direction (4.5 = No), but the theoretical potential for bias cannot be ruled out given the unblinded, self-report nature of the outcome, the domain-level judgment is "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk." This reflects the RoB 2 principle that self-reported outcomes in open-label trials involving subjective judgment are generally not eligible for a "Low risk" rating in this domain.
