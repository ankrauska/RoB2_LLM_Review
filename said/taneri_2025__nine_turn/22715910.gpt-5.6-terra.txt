**Outcome Name: Inhaler Use Technique**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The article states that eligible participants “were randomly assigned” to one of four groups. However, it does not report how the random allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, sealed-envelope draw, or other random mechanism). The trial registration also describes the study as randomized but does not provide sequence-generation methods. Therefore, random sequence generation cannot be verified.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** Neither the article nor the provided trial-registration record reports an allocation-concealment method. There is no description of central randomization, an independent allocation service, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another method that would prevent those recruiting/enrolling participants from knowing the forthcoming assignment. Therefore, concealment of allocation cannot be established.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The study groups were similar in size: 22, 21, 20, and 22 participants allocated to the four groups. The authors state that initial pretest analyses found “no statistically significant differences among the four study groups” regarding the outcomes of interest. For inhaler technique specifically, pretest mean scores ranged from 3.9 to 4.8 on the nine-step checklist, with substantial within-group variability (SD 2.0–2.3); the reported differences do not clearly indicate an implausible or important imbalance. Although a fully detailed baseline table by randomized group is not provided, the available data do not suggest that the randomization process produced problematic baseline imbalance.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

- **Judgement:** **Some concerns**
- **Reason:** The groups do not show evidence of problematic baseline imbalance, but the report provides insufficient information to determine whether the allocation sequence was genuinely random or whether allocation was concealed. These omissions raise some concerns regarding potential selection bias from the randomization process.

### 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** Of 92 participants assigned to the educational interventions, 85 completed the study; thus, outcome data at the reported follow-up were unavailable for 7/92 participants (7.6%). The outcome is continuous (a 0–9 inhaler-technique checklist score). Availability of approximately 92% is below the usual 95% threshold for considering outcome data available for “nearly all” randomized participants. The report does not demonstrate that the missing data, under any plausible values, could not have importantly affected the estimated intervention effect. In addition, it does not provide the number with inhaler-technique outcome data by each randomized group.

### 3.2. If no/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The authors do not report an analysis method intended to address potential bias due to missing inhaler-technique data, such as multiple imputation incorporating relevant predictors, inverse-probability weighting, or sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-data assumptions. No sensitivity analysis is presented to show that the findings were robust to the missing data. The analysis appears based on participants with available follow-up data.

### 3.3. If no/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The reasons for non-completion and the groups in which non-completion occurred are not reported. In a behavioural/educational intervention with follow-up assessment of observed inhaler technique, continued participation and completion of the outcome assessment could plausibly be associated with participants’ engagement, ability to perform inhaler technique, or perceived benefit from the intervention. The report does not document that missingness resulted from reasons unrelated to the inhaler-technique outcome.

### 3.4. If yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The study does not report reasons for the seven non-completions, baseline inhaler-technique scores among participants with missing follow-up data, outcome-data availability by randomized group, or differences in attrition between groups. Therefore, there is insufficient evidence to conclude that missingness was likely, rather than merely possibly, related to the true inhaler-technique outcome.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

- **Judgement:** **Some concerns**
- **Reason:** Follow-up inhaler-technique data were missing for 7.6% of randomized participants, which is not clearly “nearly all” for a continuous outcome. No analysis or sensitivity analysis was reported to address possible bias from missing data. Because the reasons and group distribution of missing data were not reported, missingness could be related to true inhaler-use technique, but it is not possible to determine that this relationship was likely.
