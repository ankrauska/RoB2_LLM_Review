**Outcome Name: Interhemispheric functional connectivity**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The report states that participants “were randomized into one of four study arms” and that “randomization was completed using blocks of four.” Block randomization ordinarily entails a random allocation component. However, the article does not explicitly describe how the order of assignments within each block was generated (e.g., computer-generated random sequence or random-number table). Therefore, there is evidence consistent with a random sequence, but insufficient methodological detail for “Yes.”

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response: No information**
- **Reason:** The study states that it was double-blinded and that both participants and the investigator assessing outcomes were blinded to the intervention. However, it provides no description of allocation concealment during enrolment and assignment. In particular, it does not report centralized/remote randomization, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, allocation performed by an independent party, or another procedure preventing enrolment personnel from foreseeing the next assignment. Blinding after allocation does not by itself establish adequate allocation concealment.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Group sizes were broadly similar: sham, *n* = 9; 1 Hz, *n* = 9; 100 Hz, *n* = 9; random-frequency, *n* = 11 initially (with one later excluded from EEG analysis because of excessive artefacts). The authors state that there were no significant differences in demographic characteristics between groups. Table 1 presents broadly comparable baseline age, sex distribution, MMSE, and behavioral-task values across groups. Although the sham group had a somewhat higher mean age (36 years) than the active groups (approximately 27–29 years), no reported imbalance clearly indicates a defective randomization process, and no baseline outcome measure of interhemispheric functional connectivity was reported by group. Thus, the available baseline information does not suggest a problem, while reporting is not sufficiently comprehensive to support a definitive “No.”

### Domain 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

- **Judgement: Some concerns**
- **Reason:** Block randomization provides some indication that a random allocation sequence was used, but its generation was not fully described. More importantly, allocation concealment was not reported. The reported baseline data do not indicate a clear randomization failure, but the absence of information about concealment means that selection bias before allocation cannot be excluded.

**Outcome Name: Interhemispheric functional connectivity**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Interhemispheric functional connectivity was operationalized as EEG interhemispheric coherence, measured using a 64-channel Electrical Geodesic Incorporated EEG system. The authors used magnitude-squared coherence, a quantitative pairwise connectivity measure derived from power spectral densities and cross-spectral density, and analysed predefined alpha/theta bands and specified sub-bands. EEG coherence is an objective neurophysiological measure that is capable of detecting pre-to-post changes in phase coupling. The article does not provide validation data for the specific analytic pipeline or electrode pairs, but it provides no evidence that the method was inappropriate or insensitive to plausible effects of tPCS.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** All groups underwent the same EEG recording procedure before and after their allocated sham or active stimulation condition: a 64-channel EEG recording for 12 minutes under eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions. The same preprocessing approach, including offline artefact detection/rejection and the same filtering procedures, was reported for EEG recordings. The intervention groups differed in stimulation condition/frequency, not in the reported EEG acquisition or outcome-assessment procedures.

### 4.3. If No/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The article explicitly states that “both the investigator assessing the outcomes and the subjects were blinded to the intervention.” Interhemispheric coherence was obtained from EEG recordings and analysed offline. Therefore, based on the reported blinding of the outcome assessor, outcome assessment was performed without knowledge of assigned intervention.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Not applicable**
- **Reason:** The outcome assessor was reported to be blinded to the assigned intervention.

### 4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Not applicable**
- **Reason:** This question is not applicable because outcome-assessor blinding was reported.

### Domain 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

- **Judgement: Low**
- **Reason:** The outcome was measured objectively using the same 64-channel EEG acquisition and coherence-analysis procedures across groups and at equivalent pre- and post-stimulation time points. The investigator assessing outcomes was reported to be blinded. There is no reported indication that the measurement method or its application differed by intervention group.

### 5.1. Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available for analysis?

- **Response: No information**
- **Reason:** The article describes planned statistical methods, including mixed-model ANOVA and stated “a-priori specifications” for selected post-hoc comparisons. However, it does not provide or cite a trial protocol, statistical analysis plan finalized before outcome-data analysis, trial registration number, or public prespecification of the functional-connectivity outcome measurements and analyses. Therefore, it cannot be verified that the reported analyses were pre-specified before unblinded outcome data became available.

### 5.2. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** There were multiple eligible measurements within the interhemispheric-functional-connectivity domain. Coherence was assessed across alpha and theta bands; four sub-bands (low-alpha, high-alpha, low-beta, high-beta); two electrode-pair configurations; and two recording conditions (eyes open and eyes closed). The article reports omnibus *p*-values for several bands/sub-bands, but emphasizes and graphically presents results for theta and low-alpha coherence, where effects of random-frequency stimulation appeared most favorable. It does not provide a prespecified plan demonstrating that all electrode-pair-specific and band-specific coherence results were fully reported. This creates a possibility that the highlighted numerical results were selected from multiple available outcome measurements based on their results.

### 5.3. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The report includes several possible analyses of the coherence data: pre-to-post change analyses; mixed-model analyses incorporating eyes-open/eyes-closed condition, intervention group, and their interaction; planned comparisons of each active group versus sham; and additional “further exploratory” comparisons between the random-frequency group and the 1 Hz and 100 Hz groups. The findings emphasized in the text include favorable exploratory comparisons for random-frequency stimulation, including random frequency versus 1 Hz and 100 Hz for theta coherence. Although some post-hoc comparisons were stated to be specified *a priori*, no protocol or finalized statistical analysis plan is available to determine whether all analyses, electrode-pair choices, frequency-band choices, and exploratory comparisons were pre-specified. Thus, selection of favorable analyses cannot be excluded.

### Domain 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result

- **Judgement: Some concerns**
- **Reason:** No prospectively finalized protocol or statistical analysis plan is available to verify the pre-specification of the reported functional-connectivity analyses. The study assessed multiple frequency bands/sub-bands, electrode-pair measures, recording conditions, and comparison/analysis approaches, while highlighting favorable theta and low-alpha findings and reporting additional exploratory comparisons. This creates concerns about selective selection of outcome measurements and/or analyses, but there is insufficient direct evidence to conclude that selective reporting definitely occurred.
