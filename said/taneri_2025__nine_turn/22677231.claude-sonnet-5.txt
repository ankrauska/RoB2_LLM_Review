## 2.3. Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The authors themselves raise this concern in the Discussion: "The unexpected improvement in the control group might be attributable to patients' use of treatments other than Deprexis, to spontaneous remission..., or to other, unknown factors." This directly mirrors the classic RoB2 example of participants who feel "unlucky" upon knowing they were assigned to a wait-list and therefore "seek the experimental intervention, or other interventions that improve their prognosis" because of trial context. The naturalistic design did not exclude participants for seeking concurrent treatment, and there is no data confirming whether treatment-seeking behavior changed during the 8-week trial period specifically in the wait-list arm. ## 2.4. Were these deviations likely to have affected the outcome?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The wait-list group showed a substantial and unexpected within-group improvement on the BDI (Cohen's d = .58), which the authors themselves find surprising ("unexpected improvement in the control group"). If this improvement is partly attributable to control group participants seeking alternative treatments due to awareness of their wait-list status, this would directly affect the primary outcome (BDI scores), diluting the between-group treatment effect.

## 2.5. Were these deviations from intended intervention balanced between groups?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** By definition, this type of context-driven deviation (control group participants seeking alternative treatment because they are aware they are waiting) is asymmetric — it would primarily or exclusively affect the wait-list group, not the immediate-treatment group. There is no reason to expect this specific deviation to occur symmetrically in the Deprexis group, since those participants already received their intervention. No data is presented on concurrent treatment-seeking behavior during the trial for either group, so this imbalance cannot be ruled out or confirmed as balanced.

## 2.6. Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The authors conducted both intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses using multiple imputation (SAS PROC MI, 100 imputed datasets, stratified by treatment) for missing outcome data, alongside per-protocol (PP) analyses. The ITT analysis is the appropriate method for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention, as it retains participants in their originally assigned group regardless of adherence or intervening events.

## 2.7. Was there potential for a substantial impact of the failure to analyze participants in the group to which they were randomized?

- **Response: Not applicable**
- **Reason:** Since 2.6 was answered "Yes" (appropriate ITT analysis was used), this question does not need to be answered per the RoB 2 algorithm.

# Domain 3: Risk of Bias due to Missing Outcome Data

## 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The overall completion rate was 81.9%, meaning approximately 18.1% of randomized participants had missing outcome data on the BDI at post-intervention. This falls well short of the ~95% threshold suggested as generally sufficient for continuous outcomes. Table 2 confirms this: of 105 randomized to wait-list, only 90 were included in the PP analysis; of 105 randomized to Deprexis, only 80 were included in the PP analysis. This is not "nearly all" participants.

## 3.2. Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The authors used intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis with multiple imputation via SAS PROC MI, stated as: "100 dataset[s] were imputed, stratified for treatment." Critically, the guidance explicitly states that "imputing the outcome variable... via multiple imputation based only on intervention group[i.e., treatment stratification], should not be assumed to correct for bias due to missing outcome data." The article does not report that the imputation model incorporated other predictors of missingness (e.g., baseline severity, QoL, other covariates found to differ between completers/non-completers) beyond simple treatment-group stratification. No sensitivity analyses testing a range of plausible assumptions about missingness mechanisms are reported. Therefore, this imputation approach cannot be assumed to adequately correct for potential bias.

## 3.3. Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The article reports: "Participants who did not complete the trial were indistinguishable from non-completers on any of the background, medical or baseline psychopathological and psychological variables **except for** the QoL subscales tapping social relationships, t(208) = 2.12, p = .03, and environment, t(208) = 2.12, p = .05, where completers achieved slightly higher baseline scores." This indicates that participants with poorer baseline quality of life (a variable closely related to depression severity, the outcome of interest) were less likely to complete the trial. This suggests a plausible link between missingness and the underlying construct being measured (depression-related functioning), raising concern that missingness could be related to participants' true depression outcome status.

## 3.4. Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** Multiple criteria from the guidance support this judgment:
  1. **Differential missingness across groups:** Completion was 85.7% in wait-list vs. 78.1% in Deprexis (Table 1), a numerical difference of ~7.6 percentage points, though not statistically significant (χ²(1) = 2.06, p > .1). This difference, while modest, is non-trivial and raises concern given the small sample.
  2. **Evidence linking missingness to outcome-related variables:** As noted in 3.3, non-completers had significantly lower baseline QoL scores (social relationships and environment domains), variables strongly associated with depressive symptomatology. This provides direct empirical evidence in the trial itself that dropout was non-random with respect to depression-related functioning.
  3. **Circumstantial plausibility:** In depression trials generally, it is clinically well-recognized that greater depression severity, hopelessness, or lack of motivation increases the likelihood of disengagement and dropout — this is analogous to the schizophrenia example provided in the RoB 2 guidance ("continuing symptoms make drop out more likely").
  4. No adjustment or correction was made in the analysis to specifically account for the baseline characteristics (QoL) that were shown to relate to missingness.

  Given the convergence of (a) meaningful (if non-significant) differential attrition by group, (b) documented association between missingness and outcome-relevant baseline variables within this very study, and (c) the general clinical plausibility of outcome-dependent dropout in depression research, I judge it as "Probably yes" that missingness depended on the true value of the outcome.

# Domain 4: Risk of Bias in Measurement of the Outcome

## 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) is a well-validated, widely used self-report measure of depression severity. The article notes extensive psychometric support: correlation with the clinician-administered Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) ranging r = .61–.81 across five studies, internal consistency >.8 in both psychiatric and non-psychiatric populations, and good re-test reliability (r = .72 in the current wait-list sample). The BDI is sensitive to changes in depressive symptomatology and is an appropriate, standard method for measuring this outcome.

## 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The BDI was administered identically to both groups via the same online survey platform (OPST®) at the same two time points (baseline and 8 weeks post-baseline), using the same reminder procedures (up to three reminders via email) and the same incentive structure (self-help material for completing post-assessment). There is no indication that measurement procedures, timing, or thresholds differed systematically between the Deprexis and wait-list groups.

## 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The BDI is a self-report instrument. Per the RoB 2 guidance: "For participant-reported outcomes, the outcome assessor is the study participant." As established in Domain 2, participants were explicitly informed of their allocation status (immediate Deprexis access vs. delayed wait-list access) via email. Therefore, the "outcome assessors" (i.e., the participants themselves) were fully aware of their intervention assignment when completing the post-treatment BDI.

## 4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The BDI is a self-report measure of subjective psychological symptoms (mood, self-attitudes, somatic complaints) requiring participant judgement — it is not an objective, error-free measure like all-cause mortality. Knowledge of group assignment plausibly could influence how participants rate their own symptoms, through mechanisms such as expectation effects, demand characteristics, hope/disappointment related to treatment access, or motivation to justify continued participation (e.g., Deprexis group may feel motivated to report improvement to validate effort invested; wait-list participants may feel demoralized, or alternatively minimize symptoms to avoid appearing to need help). The article's own data support the plausibility of expectancy effects: at baseline, "only 37.8% of participants expected to benefit from the program," and post-hoc "almost two-thirds of the participants in the immediate treatment group regarded the program as helpful in retrospect" — indicating expectancy and subjective appraisal processes were clearly active and measurable in this sample.

## 4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** This trial was entirely unblinded (open-label wait-list design), and the primary outcome relies exclusively on self-report with no corroborating clinician-rated or objective measure. The authors themselves acknowledge this limitation directly: "the primary outcome rested exclusively on self-report… clinician ratings could not be used in the current study." The RoB 2 guidance specifically flags "patient-reported symptoms" as a canonical example of outcomes likely influenced by knowledge of intervention receipt when there are "strong levels of belief in either beneficial or harmful effects of the intervention." Here, participants actively sought out and joined an online depression support/treatment study, suggesting motivation and hope for improvement; the Deprexis group received a discernible active intervention (a structured 10-module CBT-based program with substantial time investment, ~3.5 hours on average), while the wait-list group received nothing and had to wait 8 weeks knowing they were in the control condition. This asymmetry in perceived benefit is likely to generate differential reporting bias (e.g., Deprexis participants motivated to report improvement given effort invested; wait-list participants aware they are not receiving help). This is a highly plausible source of bias directly affecting the unblinded, subjective, self-reported primary outcome.

Given the fully unblinded design, the subjective/self-reported nature of the BDI as the sole primary outcome measure, and documented evidence within the study itself of active expectancy effects and differential motivational context between groups (immediate structured intervention vs. untreated waiting), it is likely — not merely possible — that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received. | 3.1 Data available for nearly all participants? | **No** | Completion rate = 81.9%; ~18% missing outcome data, below the ~95% threshold. |
| 3.2 Evidence result not biased by missing data? | **Probably no** | Multiple imputation was stratified only by treatment group; no adjustment for other covariates or sensitivity analyses reported. |
| 3.3 Could missingness depend on true value? | **Probably yes** | Non-completers had significantly lower baseline QoL (social relationships p=.03; environment p=.05) — a construct closely linked to depression severity. |
| 3.4 Likely that missingness depended on true value? | **Probably yes** | Differential completion by group (85.7% wait-list vs. 78.1% Deprexis), documented association between missingness and outcome-related baseline variables, and general clinical plausibility of symptom-driven dropout in depression trials. |

**Domain 3 Judgement: High**
*Rationale: Substantial missing data (>5%), no correction demonstrated to be adequate, and clear within-study evidence linking missingness to outcome-related variables.*

## Domain 4: Bias in Measurement of the Outcome

| Signaling Question | Judgement | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| 4.1 Was measurement method inappropriate? | **No** | BDI is well-validated (r=.61–.81 with HDRS; internal consistency >.8; re-test reliability r=.72 in this study). |
| 4.2 Could measurement differ between groups? | **No** | Identical administration procedure, platform, and timing for both groups. |
| 4.3 Were outcome assessors aware of intervention? | **Yes** | Self-report outcome = participant is assessor; participants were aware of their allocation. |
| 4.4 Could assessment be influenced by this knowledge? | **Yes** | BDI is a subjective, judgement-based self-report measure susceptible to expectancy/demand effects. |
| 4.5 Likely that assessment was influenced? | **Yes** | Fully unblinded design; sole reliance on self-report (author-acknowledged limitation); documented expectancy effects in the sample (37.8% expected benefit at baseline; 64.8% reported benefit met/exceeded post-hoc); asymmetric effort/investment between active treatment and no-treatment arms. |

**Domain 4 Judgement: High**
*Rationale: Unblinded trial design combined with an entirely subjective self-reported primary outcome creates strong, plausible potential for differential reporting bias favoring the treatment group.*
