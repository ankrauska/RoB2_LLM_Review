**Outcome Name: response rate**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The article describes the study as using a random sample and states that pharmacists were “randomized” to postal-mail, email, or hybrid groups. However, it does not report how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, or other random mechanism). Stratification by delivery method, questionnaire length, item placement, and recorded gender is reported, but the method used to randomize pharmacists within or across these strata is not described.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The article provides no information on allocation concealment. It does not state who generated the allocation sequence, who assigned pharmacists to delivery methods, whether assignment was centrally administered, or whether personnel selecting/enrolling pharmacists could foresee upcoming allocations.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** Baseline demographic characteristics are reported by delivery method only among survey respondents, rather than among all pharmacists randomized/allocated to the three delivery methods. Because response was the outcome and differed substantially by delivery method, respondent characteristics cannot validly demonstrate baseline balance or imbalance in the randomized groups. In addition, the email group was sampled from a different commercial database than the postal-mail and hybrid groups. Thus, the reported differences—for example, in ethnic-group distribution and practice site—cannot be used to determine whether the randomization process itself caused imbalance. No baseline characteristics for all allocated pharmacists are provided.

### Domain 1 risk-of-bias judgement: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** Although the study is described as randomized, there is no reported method for generating the allocation sequence and no information demonstrating allocation concealment. Furthermore, available demographic comparisons are restricted to respondents and therefore do not permit a meaningful assessment of baseline comparability of the randomized groups.

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The outcome was clearly operationalized as the percentage of eligible recipients who returned a questionnaire completed with no missing data. ### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** The delivery modes differed, but the outcome criterion was the same across groups: return/submission of a questionnaire with no missing data. Postal questionnaires were returned physically, whereas online questionnaires were stored electronically after submission. The article does not indicate different thresholds, definitions, or follow-up periods for determining a complete response across delivery groups. Although data-capture mechanisms differed by mode, there is no indication that these differences caused differential ascertainment of whether a complete survey was returned.

### 4.3. If No/probably no to 4.1 and 4.2: Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** Personnel recording and processing responses would necessarily have known the delivery method because postal-mail responses were received as paper questionnaires, whereas email and hybrid responses were submitted through the online survey system. In addition, participants—the relevant assessors of their own decision to return a questionnaire—were aware of the survey delivery method they received.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Response rate was determined from the objective occurrence of a completed questionnaire being returned/submitted, rather than from a subjective judgement by an assessor. Knowledge of delivery method may influence a pharmacist’s decision to respond, but that is the intended effect of the delivery-method intervention on the outcome, not bias in ascertainment or measurement of whether a completed response was received.

### 4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Not applicable  
- **Reason:** Assessment of the outcome was judged not to be influenceable by knowledge of delivery method.

### Domain 4 risk-of-bias judgement: **Low**

- **Reason:** Completed response was measured using an objective and consistently defined criterion across the delivery-method groups. Although delivery mode could not be blinded, awareness of mode was not likely to bias ascertainment of whether a complete questionnaire had been returned.
