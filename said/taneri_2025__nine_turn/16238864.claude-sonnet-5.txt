I've reviewed:

- **The theoretical framework**: The study integrates the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), self-efficacy/PBC distinctions, anticipated regret (AR), and implementation intentions (Gollwitzer's work) as the basis for intervention design.

- **Methodology**: A controlled, randomized pre-post design with 218 psychology undergraduates (218 baseline, 146 at 1-week follow-up, 33% attrition), comparing a persuasive communication + implementation intention leaflet against a control task of equivalent length.

- **Measures**: 7-point bipolar scales for attitude, norms, PBC, self-efficacy, intention, and anticipated regret, plus self-reported behavioral frequency (days eating fruit/veg/RDIFV) over the prior week.

- **Key results**:
  - Hypothesis 1 supported: TPB + AR model explained 34% of variance in follow-up RDIFV behavior and 71% of variance in intention (self-efficacy was the strongest correlate of intention; AR added 10%).

  - Hypothesis 3 supported: intervention group ate more fruit/vegetables and RDIFV more frequently at follow-up (small-moderate effect, d=.34).
  - Hypothesis 4 not supported: cognition changes (intention, AR) did not fully account for behavior change, suggesting implementation intentions had an independent effect.

- **Discussion/limitations**: Self-report reliance, predominantly female/university sample, short 1-week follow-up, no biochemical validation, no control for vegetarian/vegan diet status, and a motivated-responder bias among the follow-up completers.

- **Response: Yes**

The control group, in contrast, only answered neutral questions about the questionnaire experience, with no comparable prompt to report elevated intentions. This asymmetry in explicit priming toward the outcome construct makes it likely — not merely possible — that the observed group difference in reported intention was influenced by participants' awareness of, and responsiveness to, the intervention content itself, rather than reflecting a stable underlying cognitive shift.
