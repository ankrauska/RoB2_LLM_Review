Outcome Name: time to relapse from the start date of double-blind treatment

3.1 Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?
• Response: Probably no
• Reason: The study reports that during the 24-week double-blind period, the overall withdrawal rate (excluding relapses, which are the event of interest) was 7.9% for escitalopram (13/163 patients) and 8.9% for placebo (14/157 patients). In a time-to-event analysis, these participants are censored and considered to have missing outcome data for their remaining follow-up. While the missingness is relatively low (under 10%), it cannot strictly be considered "nearly all" since the number of missing participants is not insignificant.

3.2 Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?
• Response: Probably no
• Reason: The authors analyzed the intention-to-treat (ITT) population using Cox proportional hazards and Kaplan-Meier models, but there is no specific mention of sensitivity analyses (such as multiple imputation or specific bounds) designed to assess the impact of informative censoring/missing data under a range of plausible assumptions.

3.3 Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?
• Response: Probably yes
• Reason: In psychiatric trials (such as for OCD), participants may withdraw consent, be lost to follow-up, or drop out due to adverse events, which could be tied to their underlying health status and symptom severity. Therefore, it is possible that missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome.

3.4 Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?
• Response: Probably no
• Reason: While it is possible, it is not *likely* to have biased the outcome. The proportions of missing outcome data (censoring due to withdrawal) were very similar between the two groups (7.9% in the escitalopram group and 8.9% in the placebo group). Additionally, the trial protocol defined "lack of efficacy as judged by the investigator" as a relapse (the event), meaning patients who dropped out because the drug wasn't working were counted as having the event rather than as missing data. This drastically reduces the likelihood of severe informative censoring driving the remaining missing data.
