### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The report states that, after the 5-visit abstinence trial, hard-to-treat participants were “randomly assigned” to percentile, fixed criterion, or random-incentive groups. It further specifies that, from each group of five participants completing intake, **two were randomly assigned to percentile, two to fixed criterion, and one to random incentives**. Allocation was also stratified by intended smoking-cessation medication use and study-entry order. Although the report does not state the exact random mechanism (e.g., random-number generator, random-number table, or drawing lots), its description supports use of a random allocation process rather than an evidently predictable assignment method.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response: No information**
- **Reason:** The article states that randomization occurred after participants completed intake and the 5-visit abstinence trial and had been classified as hard to treat. However, it provides no information about allocation concealment procedures. There is no description of centralized randomization, an independent allocator, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, password-protected allocation, or whether staff enrolling/classifying participants could foresee upcoming group assignments. Therefore, concealment until assignment cannot be established.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** Group sizes were consistent with the planned approximate 2:2:1 allocation ratio: percentile 36, fixed criterion 40, and random 18 participants. The article reports that groups were statistically similar for intake characteristics except education: a higher proportion of percentile participants had vocational/technical school/community-college education than fixed-criterion participants (**χ² = 8.46, p < 0.01**). A single statistically significant baseline difference among numerous characteristics is compatible with chance and does not by itself indicate a flawed randomization process. No substantial imbalance in reported smoking-related baseline characteristics or intake breath CO was apparent.

### Domain 1 overall risk-of-bias judgement: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** The allocation sequence was described in a manner consistent with random assignment, and baseline data do not indicate a problem with randomization. However, allocation concealment was not reported. Because it is not possible to determine whether personnel enrolling or classifying participants had foreknowledge of forthcoming assignments, there are **some concerns** about bias arising from the randomization process.

**Outcome Name: smoking abstinence by breath CO sample < 3 ppm**

### 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Breath CO was measured only when participants attended a scheduled visit. Attendance was incomplete: participants submitted, on average, **42/60** CO samples in the percentile group, **40/60** in the fixed-criterion group, and **52/60** in the random-incentive group. Thus, a substantial proportion of scheduled outcome measurements were missing, particularly in the percentile and fixed-criterion groups. Although the report used all randomized participants as denominators when reporting the proportion who ever submitted an abstinent sample, it does not state that participants with missed visits were assessed for smoking status through another method or that missing CO outcomes were handled as smoking/non-abstinence.

### 3.2. If No/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The authors did not report an analysis method designed to address possible bias from missing breath-CO data. No sensitivity analyses were reported that tested the robustness of abstinence results under plausible assumptions regarding missing CO samples. The report also does not provide a missing-data strategy, such as justified imputation or an analysis accounting for predictors of missingness.

### 3.3. If No/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** Outcome data were missing when participants did not attend scheduled breath-CO visits. In a smoking-cessation contingency-management trial, attendance and provision of a CO sample could plausibly be related to smoking status and the likelihood of meeting the abstinence criterion, because abstinent samples could earn incentives and non-abstinent samples could result in no incentive in the fixed-criterion condition. Therefore, whether a participant attended and provided a measurable CO sample could depend on their true smoking/abstinence status.

### 3.4. If Yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** The trial involved repeated, incentive-linked outcome assessments, and missed visits resulted in unavailable CO outcomes. Participants in the fixed-criterion group could earn incentives only for CO <3 ppm, whereas participants in the percentile group could also earn incentives for lower non-abstinent CO levels. Consequently, non-attendance may plausibly be linked to whether participants expected to meet the relevant CO criterion and receive an incentive. Moreover, attendance differed across the three randomized groups (mean submitted samples: 42 percentile, 40 fixed criterion, and 52 random), demonstrating that the extent of unavailable CO data was not uniform across all study conditions. The authors did not report reasons for missed visits or analyses addressing the likely relationship between missingness and smoking status.

## Domain 3 overall risk-of-bias judgement: **High**

- **Reason:** A substantial amount of repeated breath-CO outcome data was unavailable because participants did not attend all scheduled visits. The study does not report an analysis or sensitivity analysis addressing bias caused by missing outcome data. In this incentive-based smoking-cessation context, missing attendance/CO samples is likely related to smoking-abstinence status, creating a material risk that missing data biased the estimated intervention effect.
