   • Reason: The article states that "Identical assessment tools were used in the three different interviews" across all four groups. There is no evidence to suggest that the method or timing of outcome assessment differed between the experimental and comparison groups.

3. 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?
   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: The outcome was assessed via in-person observation by the community facilitators and the research coordinator. Since these facilitators delivered the interventions (showing the videos or providing the pamphlet) and the study was conducted face-to-face, they were aware of the assigned interventions. While the article notes that facilitators "were not aware of the study hypothesis," this does not equate to being blinded to the intervention received.

4. 4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?
   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: Evaluating a patient's physical inhaler technique using a 9-step checklist is an observer-reported outcome that involves some degree of subjective judgment (e.g., determining if the inhalation was deep enough or if breath was held for the correct amount of time). Therefore, knowledge of the intervention could theoretically influence the assessment.

5. 4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?
   • Response: Probably no
   • Reason: Although the assessors knew the intervention groups, the assessment was based on a "standardized nine-step observational checklist" and was "judged by two observers (the interviewer and the research coordinator)." The use of a standardized checklist and dual independent observers provides a safeguard that makes it unlikely that knowledge of the intervention heavily biased the scoring, even though it theoretically could have.

*Reason:* The most significant source of potential bias in this study is the unblinded assessment of a subjective outcome (Domain 4). Because the community facilitators who delivered the experimental educational interventions also evaluated the participants' inhaler techniques, there is a risk that their subjective scoring could consciously or unconsciously favour the experimental groups they were actively involved with. Additionally, undocumented missing data (Domain 3) could skew results if those who failed to grasp the inhaler technique dropped out.

In this study, there are "Some concerns" in three different domains: Domain 1 (complete lack of information on randomization sequence generation and allocation concealment), Domain 3 (unexplained exclusions and dropouts), and Domain 4 (unblinded assessors measuring an observer-reported outcome involving subjective judgment). The accumulation of these methodological issues warrants an overall judgement of High risk of bias.
