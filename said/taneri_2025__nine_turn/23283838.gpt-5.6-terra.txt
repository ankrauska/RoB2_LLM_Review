**Outcome Name: between-session habituation**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The article states that participants “were randomly assigned” to 250 mg D-cycloserine or matching placebo. However, it does not describe how the random allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, block randomization, or another method containing a random component). Therefore, random sequence generation cannot be verified from the report.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The report states that participants were assigned by “a data manager who had no interaction with participants,” and that DCS and placebo were administered in “identical-appearing capsules.” This supports that allocation was managed independently from personnel interacting with/enrolling participants and that assignment was masked at the point of administration. Although the article does not provide full operational details of concealment (e.g., sequentially numbered medication containers or a central allocation procedure), the reported independent data manager and identical capsules make concealment likely.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The allocated group sizes were 18 participants in the DCS group and 16 in the placebo group, which is compatible with chance variation around an apparent 1:1 allocation. The groups did not differ significantly at baseline in gender, ethnicity, medication use, antidepressant use, baseline LSAS score, average speech SUDS during Visit 1, or Social Phobia Scale score at Visit 1 (all *p* > .16). The groups differed in age (*p* = .019), with an average age of approximately 49 years in the DCS group and 38 years in the placebo group. A single statistically significant difference among multiple baseline comparisons is compatible with chance and does not, by itself, indicate a flawed randomization process. Baseline outcome-related measures were reported as similar between groups.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Bias arising from the randomization process

- **Judgement:** **Some concerns**
- **Reason:** Allocation concealment was likely adequate, and the reported baseline data do not suggest a problematic randomization process. However, the method used to generate the allocation sequence was not reported beyond the statement that participants were randomly assigned. This leaves uncertainty about whether the sequence itself contained a random component.

**Outcome Name: between-session habituation**

### 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Of 34 randomized participants (18 DCS; 16 placebo), 4 participants did not return for Visit 2 and were excluded from all analyses (2/18 [11.1%] DCS; 2/16 [12.5%] placebo). Thus, between-session habituation data were available for 30/34 participants (88.2%), below the commonly adequate 95% level for continuous outcomes. Moreover, the primary outcome was dichotomous (response defined as at least a 5-point reduction in average SUDS); four missing outcomes could potentially affect the observed response comparison.

### 3.2. Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The authors performed a completer-only analysis, explicitly stating that only participants completing Visit 2 were analysed. No analysis correcting for missing outcome data, no imputation approach, and no sensitivity analysis under plausible assumptions about missing outcomes were reported.

### 3.3. Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** Two participants became unreachable following loss or change of telephone number, while two reported that they preferred not to continue in the study. For those who chose not to continue, withdrawal could be related to their experience of the speech exposure, anxiety, or anticipated outcome at Visit 2. The report does not establish that missingness was unrelated to the true between-session habituation outcome.

### 3.4. Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** Missing outcome data were balanced numerically between groups (2 DCS and 2 placebo participants). The stated reasons were also similarly distributed: one participant in each group became unreachable and one participant in each group preferred not to continue. There is no reported evidence that withdrawal was related to outcome severity, that reasons for missingness differed by group, or that missingness was more common in one group. Nevertheless, the reasons provided do not rule out outcome-related missingness.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: Bias due to missing outcome data

- **Judgement:** **Some concerns**
- **Reason:** Outcome data were unavailable for 11.8% of randomized participants, and the analysis was restricted to participants completing Visit 2 without sensitivity analyses or other methods to assess the impact of missing data. Missingness could be related to the true outcome, particularly for participants who chose not to continue, but balanced attrition and similar reported reasons across groups mean that it is not established as likely that missingness depended on outcome.

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** Between-session habituation was operationalized as a decrease of at least 5 points in the participant’s average Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS) score across the speech exposure from Visit 1 to Visit 2. SUDS is described as a standard scale in behavioural research and was administered repeatedly at prespecified points before and during each speech. The authors report excellent internal consistency for combined SUDS ratings in this sample (Cronbach’s α > .85). Although the report does not provide validation evidence for the specific dichotomous 5-point threshold, the available information does not indicate that the measurement method was inappropriate or unable to detect a plausible effect.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Both randomized groups underwent the same tailored speech-exposure procedure and SUDS ratings were collected at the same specified time points before, during, and at the end of each speech, at both visits. The same outcome definition—a ≥5-point decline in average SUDS from Visit 1 to Visit 2—was applied to both groups. No group-specific assessment method, schedule, threshold, or follow-up procedure is reported.

### 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Reason:** This was a participant-reported outcome, so participants themselves were the outcome assessors. Participants received matching, identical-appearing DCS or placebo capsules. No DCS-specific side effects that could unblind participants were reported.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?
