**Outcome Name: image quality parameters**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** The article explicitly states that eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using a **“computer-generated sequence.”** This constitutes a random component in allocation-sequence generation.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** The authors report that **“allocation was concealed (using sealed envelopes).”** This supports allocation concealment. However, the report does not specify whether envelopes were opaque, sequentially numbered, tamper-proof, and opened only after irreversible enrolment. Therefore, full assurance of adequate envelope procedures is unavailable, but the explicit statement that allocation was concealed supports a judgment of probably yes.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Baseline characteristics of the 93 participants who underwent spinal MR imaging were reported for the short-bore and open-MR groups. The groups were similar in sex, age, body size measures, anatomical region imaged, claustrophobia score, and pre-scan state anxiety. All reported between-group baseline comparisons had non-significant P values (range reported: P = 0.1 to 1.0). There was no apparent clinically important imbalance, excess of statistically significant differences, or marked disparity in group size that would suggest a problem with randomization.

### Risk-of-bias judgment for Domain 1: **Low risk of bias**

- **Reason:** The allocation sequence was computer generated, allocation concealment was explicitly reported although envelope details were incomplete, and baseline characteristics did not indicate a failure of the randomization process.

**Outcome Name: image quality parameters**

**Effect of interest assessed: effect of assignment to intervention** (assignment to initial short-bore versus open MR scanner).

### 2.1. Were participants aware of their assigned intervention during the trial?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** Participants could not be blinded to the assigned scanner because the scanner configurations were visibly and physically different. The authors explicitly state: **“Patients could not be blinded to the assigned study group due to the MR imaging setting.”**

### 2.2. Were carers and people delivering the interventions aware of participants' assigned intervention during the trial?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** The personnel performing MR imaging necessarily knew which scanner was used. The study involved two distinct scanner configurations, manufacturers, field strengths, coils, and imaging protocols. The article does not report blinding of radiographers or other intervention-delivery personnel.

### 2.3. If yes/probably yes to 2.1 or 2.2: Were there deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Some participants did not complete imaging on their initially assigned scanner because of claustrophobia and were cross-referred to the other scanner; some ultimately received sedation. However, this management was explicitly prespecified in the study protocol: participants unable to tolerate their allocated scanner were cross-referred to the alternative scanner to avoid sedation, and sedation was used only if neither scanner could be tolerated. These changes were therefore consistent with the trial protocol rather than deviations arising because of the trial context.

### 2.4. If yes/probably yes to 2.3: Were these deviations likely to have affected the outcome?

- **Response:** Not applicable  
- **Reason:** The answer to Question 2.3 is No.

### 2.5. If yes/probably yes to 2.4: Were these deviations from intended intervention balanced between groups?

- **Response:** Not applicable  
- **Reason:** The answer to Question 2.3 is No.

### 2.6. Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The primary image-quality analysis was not conducted according to randomized assignment. The report states that examinations conducted after cross-referral, with or without sedation, were **included in the primary analysis**. Results are presented according to the scanner on which imaging was ultimately performed: 48 short-bore and 55 open-scanner examinations, whereas 44 and 49 patients, respectively, were initially allocated to these groups in the analysed spinal-imaging population. Thus, participants who crossed over were analysed according to the scanner received rather than the scanner to which they were randomized. This is an as-treated analysis and is not appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention.

### 2.7. If no/probably no to 2.6: Was there potential for a substantial impact on the result of the failure to analyse participants in the group to which they were randomized?

- **Response:** Yes  
- **Reason:** Cross-referral was not rare: 25 examinations were performed after cross-referral, and 16 involved conscious sedation. These post-randomization changes are plausibly associated with claustrophobia and the ability to undergo the allocated scan; they may also be associated with image quality through motion, scan completion, and sedation. Analysing such participants by scanner received instead of randomized allocation therefore has potential to substantially alter the estimated effect of assignment.

### Risk-of-bias judgment for Domain 2: **High risk of bias**

- **Reason:** Although cross-referral and sedation were allowed by the protocol and do not constitute trial-context deviations, the authors analysed image-quality outcomes according to the scanner actually used after cross-referral rather than according to original randomized assignment. Given the sizeable number of cross-referrals and sedated examinations, this as-treated approach could substantially affect the estimated effect of assignment to scanner type.

**Outcome Name: image quality parameters**

### 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Of 106 randomized participants with a clinical indication for spinal MR imaging, 93 underwent MR imaging and were included in the image-quality analysis. Therefore, image-quality data were unavailable for 13/106 participants (12.3%). This is greater than the level generally regarded as “nearly all” for a continuous outcome. The analysed population also excluded participants who did not undergo MR imaging.

### 3.2. If No/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The article does not report an analysis method intended to address bias from missing image-quality data, such as an appropriate missing-data analysis or sensitivity analyses under plausible assumptions about the missing outcomes. The reported subgroup analyses excluding cross-referred patients and considering sedated patients do not address the missing outcomes of participants who did not undergo spinal MR imaging.

### 3.3. If No/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response:** Probably yes  
- **Reason:** Image-quality data were missing because some participants could not undergo MR imaging, principally owing to claustrophobia. Claustrophobia, intolerance of scanning, and inability to complete an examination could plausibly be associated with factors relevant to image quality, particularly patient movement and scan completion. The study also reports that motion was the principal source of relevant artifacts. Therefore, it is possible that missingness was related to the image quality that would have been obtained.

### 3.4. If Yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Although missingness could be related to image quality, the report does not provide evidence that participants with missing image-quality data would systematically have had poorer or better image quality than those included. Most claustrophobic events occurred before imaging started, rather than following observation of poor image quality. The article does not report outcome-dependent reasons for missingness separately by scanner group or demonstrate a differential proportion of missing image-quality data between groups.

### Risk-of-bias judgment for Domain 3: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** Image-quality data were unavailable for 13 of 106 randomized spinal-imaging participants, exceeding a negligible amount of missing data for this continuous outcome. No analysis or sensitivity analysis was reported to address possible bias from this missingness. Missingness could plausibly be associated with factors affecting image quality, although the available evidence does not establish that outcome-dependent missingness was likely.

**Outcome Name: image quality parameters**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** Image quality was evaluated using both qualitative and quantitative methods. Qualitative outcomes included standardized 5-point ratings of overall image quality, contrast, contour sharpness, noise, and artifacts. Quantitative measures included signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), and contour-sharpness measurements. These are relevant and sensitive measures for comparing spinal MR image quality between scanners. No information indicates that these measurement approaches lacked validity or were unable to detect plausible differences between scanners.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The same outcome-assessment approach was applied to images from both scanner groups. Qualitative ratings used the same defined rating scales, and quantitative analyses used the same ROI-based SNR/CNR calculations and the same ImageJ-based contour-sharpness procedure. The analysis was conducted after image acquisition, rather than through different clinical follow-up or diagnostic pathways in the two groups.

### 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response:** No  
- **Reason:** The article explicitly states that, at the time of image analysis, the examiners were **“blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity.”** Qualitative assessment was performed by two examiners in consensus, and quantitative assessment was performed by one examiner. Thus, according to the report, the assessors did not know whether images had been acquired on the short-bore or open scanner.

### 4.4. If yes/probably yes to 4.3: Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Not applicable  
- **Reason:** Outcome assessors were reported to be blinded to the imaging technique.

### 4.5. If yes/probably yes to 4.4: Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response:** Not applicable  
- **Reason:** Outcome assessors were reported to be blinded to the imaging technique.

### Risk-of-bias judgment for Domain 4: **Low risk of bias**

- **Reason:** The outcome was measured using appropriate qualitative and quantitative image-quality measures, with the same assessment methods used for both groups. Outcome assessors were explicitly reported to have been blinded to imaging technique and patient identity.

**Outcome Name: image quality parameters**

### 5.1. Were the data that produced this result analysed in accordance with a pre-specified analysis plan that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available for analysis?

- **Response:** No information  
- **Reason:** The trial was registered prospectively (NCT00715806), and a protocol describing the parent CLAUSTRO trial was published. However, the registered protocol identifies claustrophobic reactions as the primary outcome and subsequent clinical management as a secondary outcome; it does not specify image-quality parameters as an outcome or provide a pre-specified statistical analysis plan for the image-quality comparison. The article describes image-analysis methods and statistical tests, but it does not establish that these plans were finalized before unblinded image-quality data were available.

### 5.2. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements within the outcome domain?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** The authors reported results for a broad range of image-quality measures, including qualitative overall image quality, contrast, contour sharpness, noise, artifacts, SNR, CNR, and quantitative contour sharpness. Results were also presented separately by spinal region and imaging sequence in Tables 3 and 4. Optional sequences were excluded because they were acquired in only 15 examinations, which is a stated non-result-based reason. Although the available protocol does not pre-specify the image-quality outcome domain, there is no clear evidence that only favourable image-quality measures were selected for reporting.

### 5.3. Is the numerical result being assessed likely to have been selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses of the data?

- **Response:** Probably no  
- **Reason:** The article reports the primary analysis including examinations after cross-referral, with or without sedation, and also describes subgroup analyses excluding cross-referred participants and analyses among sedated participants. The main statistical methods are stated: Mann–Whitney rank-sum testing for qualitative ratings and unpaired t-tests for quantitative outcomes. Although it is not possible to verify pre-specification of the analysis approach, there is no direct evidence that the reported primary analysis or statistical approach was selected because it produced favourable results.

### Risk-of-bias judgment for Domain 5: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** A prospectively registered protocol exists, but image-quality outcomes and their analysis plan were not specified in the provided trial registration/protocol information. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that the reported image-quality analyses were pre-specified before unblinded outcome data became available. There is no direct evidence of selective reporting from multiple outcome measurements or analyses, but the absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan raises some concerns.
