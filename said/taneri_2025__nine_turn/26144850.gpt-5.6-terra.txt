**Outcome Name: cure rate at 2 years**

### 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The article explicitly states that patients were “allocated randomly” using **random number tables**. Specifically, 268 three-digit random numbers from the table were written in advance in the patient registers; patients assigned an even number received PB-MDT and those assigned an odd number received ROM-6. Use of a random-number table constitutes a random component in sequence generation.

### 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The random numbers were written in the patient registers **in advance**, and intervention assignment was directly determined by whether the pre-written number was even or odd. The report does not describe any allocation-concealment procedure, such as central randomization, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or identically numbered drug containers. Therefore, the personnel enrolling and confirming eligibility of patients could potentially determine the forthcoming allocation from the register before assigning the participant. This provides reason to suspect that allocation was not concealed.

### 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** Both groups contained 134 participants, consistent with the intended 1:1 allocation ratio. Reported baseline characteristics appear broadly comparable: mean age was 36.4 years in PB-MDT and 40.2 years in ROM-6 (*P* = 0.059); mean disease duration was 35.8 versus 32.0 months (*P* = 0.29); the distributions of number of lesions and nerve thickening were also similar. Although the ROM-6 group had somewhat more participants with disease duration below 12 months (40.6% versus 31.3%), the article provides no evidence that this imbalance was implausible by chance or sufficiently large to indicate a defective randomization process.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** The allocation sequence was random, and no meaningful baseline imbalance indicates a problem with randomization. However, allocation concealment was not adequately implemented or reported: pre-written allocation numbers in patient registers, with treatment determined by number parity, could have allowed those enrolling participants to foresee assignments. This raises concern about possible selection bias.

- **Reason:** Although the reported treatment deviations were not shown to arise specifically from the trial context, the effect-of-assignment analysis was not appropriately conducted. These exclusions could plausibly have materially influenced the small observed difference between groups.

**Outcome Name: cure rate at 2 years**

### 3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** Two-year outcome data were reported for 100/134 (74.6%) participants randomized to PB-MDT and 103/134 (76.9%) participants randomized to ROM-6. Thus, outcome data were unavailable for 34 participants in the PB-MDT group and 31 participants in the ROM-6 group, or 65/268 (24.3%) randomized participants overall. This is a substantial amount of missing outcome data and cannot be considered “nearly all” participants, especially for a dichotomous outcome with very few observed non-cure events.

### 3.2. Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?

- **Response: No**
- **Reason:** The authors did not report an analysis method that addressed potential bias due to missing outcome data, such as an appropriate missing-data adjustment. They also did not report sensitivity analyses assessing whether the estimated cure-rate difference was robust to plausible assumptions about the outcomes of participants missing at 2 years. The reported analysis was based on the participants available for follow-up.

### 3.3. Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** Participants were missing from the 2-year outcome assessment partly because they defaulted from treatment or were lost to follow-up. Default was reported for 27/134 (20.1%) participants assigned to PB-MDT and 19/134 (14.2%) assigned to ROM-6. Reported reasons included feeling cured, drug intolerance/weakness, vomiting/diarrhoea, swelling of feet, pregnancy, stigma, and loss to treatment. Participants’ actual disease status, treatment response, or treatment tolerability could plausibly be associated with whether they completed treatment and/or remained available for outcome assessment. Therefore, missingness could depend on the true cure outcome.

### 3.4. Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?

- **Response: Probably yes**
- **Reason:** There is direct indication that outcome-related factors contributed to missingness: some participants discontinued because they “felt cured,” while other reasons included drug intolerance and adverse symptoms that could affect treatment completion and subsequent cure. Further, among defaulters who were traced at the end of the study, some remained clinically active (4/23 traced PB-MDT defaulters and 1/9 traced ROM-6 defaulters), demonstrating that defaulting participants did not uniformly have the favourable cure outcome observed in the analysed 2-year cohort. Although these later observations do not establish each participant’s 2-year cure status, they support the likelihood that missingness was related to clinical outcome.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: **High risk of bias**

- **Reason:** Nearly one-quarter of randomized participants lacked 2-year cure data. No analysis or sensitivity analysis addressed potential bias from this missingness. Missingness was likely related to cure status and/or factors associated with cure, including perceived clinical recovery, treatment intolerance, and treatment discontinuation. Given the very high observed cure proportions and small between-group difference, plausible outcomes among the substantial number of missing participants could materially alter the estimated intervention effect.

**Outcome Name: cure rate at 2 years**

### 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** Cure was assessed clinically using stated criteria: complete lesion resolution, or a lesion becoming flat and hypopigmented with reduced size and/or regained sensation. Participants were formally assessed every 6 months after treatment completion. These clinical features are relevant to the study’s definition of cure in paucibacillary leprosy. The authors acknowledge that diagnosis and assessment were purely clinical and that histology could not be conducted in the field; however, the absence of histology does not by itself establish that the clinical outcome-measurement method was inappropriate for assessing clinical cure.

### 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The article describes the same clinical cure definition and the same formal follow-up schedule—every 6 months—for both randomized groups. There is no reported indication that the frequency, timing, clinical criteria, or method of outcome assessment differed between the PB-MDT and ROM-6 groups.

### 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** The trial report explicitly states that “blinding to treatment was not undertaken.” Outcome assessments were performed by trained paramedical workers and/or doctors during follow-up. The report does not describe any separate blinded outcome-assessment process. Therefore, outcome assessors were aware, or should be regarded as aware, of the assigned treatment.

### 4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Yes**
- **Reason:** Cure was based on clinical assessments involving observer judgement, including lesion resolution, flattening, hypopigmentation, reduction in lesion size, and recovery of sensation. Although these criteria were specified, their assessment is not entirely objective. Knowledge of treatment allocation could therefore have influenced the classification of a participant as cured or not cured.

### 4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?

- **Response: Probably no**
- **Reason:** The outcome was assessed using defined clinical criteria and apparently the same follow-up procedures in both groups. The article does not report evidence that assessors applied these criteria differently according to treatment allocation, nor does it report strong expectations favouring one regimen that demonstrably affected assessment. Nevertheless, because assessment was unblinded and clinically judgement-based, the possibility of influence cannot be excluded.

### Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: **Some concerns**

- **Reason:** Outcome measurement methods and follow-up procedures appear comparable between intervention groups, and the clinical cure criteria were stated. However, treatment blinding was not undertaken, the cure outcome involved clinical judgement, and there was no blinded outcome assessment. While there is no direct evidence that unblinded assessors actually assessed groups differently, the outcome could have been influenced by knowledge of assigned treatment.
