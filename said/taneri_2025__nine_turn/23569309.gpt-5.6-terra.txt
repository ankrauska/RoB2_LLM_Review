**Outcome Name: progression free survival**

### Domain 1: Risk of bias arising from the randomization process

1.1. **Was the allocation sequence random?**

   • **Response: Yes**  
   • **Reason:** The article explicitly reports that “the random permutation method was used for random assignment.” This method includes a random component in allocation-sequence generation. Participants with disease control after induction chemotherapy were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to maintenance paclitaxel/gemcitabine or observation.

2. **1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?**

   • **Response: Probably yes**  
   • **Reason:** The report states that “registration and random assignment were coordinated centrally at the KCSG data center.” Central coordination across 10 participating institutions provides reasonable evidence that allocation was administered centrally rather than determined by site investigators. However, the report does not specify the operational mechanism of concealment (e.g., telephone/web system, access restrictions, or when the allocation sequence became available); therefore, “Probably yes,” rather than “Yes,” is appropriate.

3. **1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** Randomized group sizes were virtually equal (maintenance, 116; observation, 115), consistent with the intended 1:1 allocation. Baseline characteristics were generally comparable, including age, performance status, menopausal status, hormone-receptor status, visceral metastases, number of metastatic sites, and prior systemic treatments. Although hematogenous lung metastases were more common in the maintenance group (31.9% versus 16.5%; P = .029), this isolated nominally statistically significant difference among numerous baseline comparisons is compatible with chance and does not by itself indicate a flawed randomization process. No substantial imbalance in a key prognostic factor clearly suggests a problem with randomization.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Low risk of bias**

### Domain 3: Risk of bias due to missing outcome data

1. **3.1. Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?**

   • **Response: Probably no**  
   • **Reason:** The PFS analysis was stated to include all randomized participants under the intention-to-treat principle, but this does not establish that PFS-event data were observed for all or nearly all participants. PFS was censored at the last clinical contact for participants lost to follow-up or at the analysis cutoff. The report documents patient withdrawal in 14/116 (12.1%) participants in the maintenance group and 13/115 (11.3%) in the observation group. It does not report the number censored for PFS, the numbers at risk at the cutoff, or the number of participants lost to follow-up. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that missing PFS data were sufficiently few to have no important effect on the estimated intervention effect.

2. **3.2. If no/probably no to 3.1: Is there evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** The article does not report an analysis designed to address potential bias from missing PFS data, such as an analysis accounting for informative censoring or sensitivity analyses applying plausible assumptions about outcomes among participants censored or lost to follow-up. Censoring at last clinical contact alone does not demonstrate that missingness was unrelated to the true PFS outcome.

3. **3.3. If no/probably no to 3.2: Could missingness in the outcome depend on its true value?**

   • **Response: Yes**  
   • **Reason:** PFS is a time-to-event outcome, and participants were censored at their last clinical contact if follow-up was incomplete. Withdrawal from the study or loss of clinical follow-up could be related to health status, treatment tolerability, clinical deterioration, or undetected progression. Thus, missingness in PFS could depend on the participant’s true progression status or time to progression.

4. **3.4. If yes/probably yes to 3.3: Is it likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value?**

   • **Response: Probably no**  
   • **Reason:** Although missing PFS data could be related to true progression status, the article does not provide evidence that this was likely. Withdrawal numbers were similar in the maintenance and observation groups (14 versus 13). The report does not provide the number or causes of PFS censoring, censoring distributions by treatment group, or evidence that censored participants had a different prognosis from participants with observed progression. Therefore, there is insufficient evidence to conclude that missingness was likely to depend on the true outcome.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 3: Some concerns**  

The article does not provide sufficient detail to confirm that PFS data were available for nearly all randomized participants or that censoring was non-informative. There is no reported sensitivity analysis addressing missing PFS data. However, the available report does not establish that informative missingness was likely or that it differed materially between trial groups.

### Domain 4: Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome

1. **4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?**

   • **Response: No**  
   • **Reason:** Progression-free survival was measured from randomization to documented disease progression or last clinical visit. Tumour response and progression were assessed using RECIST version 1.0, with radiographic evaluations scheduled every 6 weeks. RECIST-based radiologic assessment is an appropriate method for measuring disease progression in metastatic breast cancer.

2. **4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?**

   • **Response: Probably no**  
   • **Reason:** The article states that radiographic studies were performed every 6 weeks, without indicating that the imaging schedule or progression-assessment method differed between maintenance and observation groups. Both groups were followed until progression, and RECIST version 1.0 was used for disease assessment. Although the maintenance group had treatment-cycle visits for chemotherapy and toxicity monitoring, the prespecified radiographic assessment schedule appears to have been the same in both groups.

3. **4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?**

   • **Response: Yes**  
   • **Reason:** The study was open label: one group received continuing intravenous paclitaxel/gemcitabine whereas the comparator group received observation without anticancer treatment. The article states that PFS and response duration were assessed by investigators and does not report blinded independent central radiology review or blinding of investigator outcome assessors. Therefore, the investigators assessing radiographic progression were aware, or very likely aware, of treatment assignment.

4. **4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

   • **Response: Yes**  
   • **Reason:** Although radiographic progression was assessed using RECIST criteria, investigator determination of progression can involve judgment, including interpretation of radiologic findings, attribution of new lesions, and the timing of documentation of progression. In an open-label comparison of active maintenance chemotherapy versus observation, knowledge of allocation could therefore influence outcome assessment.

5. **4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?**

   • **Response: Probably no**  
   • **Reason:** The report does not provide evidence that investigator awareness actually influenced progression assessment. Radiographic assessments were scheduled at regular 6-week intervals and RECIST criteria were used in both groups, which limit—but do not eliminate—the scope for differential assessment. The absence of blinded independent review means that influence remains possible, but the available information does not establish that such influence was likely.

**Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: Some concerns**  

PFS was measured with an appropriate, prespecified RECIST-based radiographic method and apparently at comparable scheduled intervals in both groups. However, the trial was open label and PFS was investigator assessed without reported blinded independent central review. Knowledge of treatment assignment could have influenced progression assessment, although the report does not establish that this was likely.
