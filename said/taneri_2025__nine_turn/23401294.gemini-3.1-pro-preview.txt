Outcome Name: balance score

1. 1.1. Was the allocation sequence random?

   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: The authors explicitly state the method used to generate the sequence, reporting, "The randomization procedure was to pick the name of the orthosis from a bag including the names of both orthoses." Drawing lots (or names from a bag) incorporates a random component.

2. 1.2. Was the allocation sequence concealed until participants were enrolled and assigned to interventions?

   • Response: No information
   • Reason: The article mentions drawing names from a bag but provides no details on whether this process was concealed from the personnel enrolling the participants. There is no mention of centralized allocation, opaque/sealed envelopes, or any other mechanism to ensure the investigator could not foresee the allocation before enrolling a participant. 

3. 1.3. Did baseline differences between intervention groups suggest a problem with the randomization process?

   • Response: No
   • Reason: The article reports baseline characteristics (age, weight, height, angle of kyphosis, baseline balance score) and explicitly states that statistical analysis "revealed no significant difference" between the two groups. While there is a slight imbalance in group sizes (8 in the PTS group and 10 in the Spinomed group), the authors correctly note this inequality "was related to the group allocation method" (simple randomization via drawing from a bag), which is entirely compatible with chance in a small sample size of 18 participants.

Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 1: Some concerns
Reason: Although the allocation sequence was generated using a random component (drawing names from a bag) and there were no problematic baseline imbalances, there is no information provided regarding allocation concealment. The lack of documented allocation concealment raises some concerns about the randomization process.

Outcome Name: balance score

1. 4.1. Was the method of measuring the outcome inappropriate?
   • Response: No
   • Reason: The article states that the balance score was measured using the NeuroCom EquiTest system, and notes that it "is a common and recommended criterion to evaluate the risk of falling, demonstrating an acceptable test–retest reliability and predictive validity." The method is appropriate.

2. 4.2. Could measurement or ascertainment of the outcome have differed between intervention groups?
   • Response: No
   • Reason: The same computerized system (NeuroCom EquiTest) and standardized protocols were used to assess the balance score for both groups under identical conditions (randomized sequence of testing with and without the orthosis, with standardized rest intervals).

3. 4.3. Were outcome assessors aware of the intervention received by study participants?
   • Response: Yes
   • Reason: Although the article is described as a "single blinded clinical trial," participants could not be blinded to the physical orthotic devices they were actively wearing during the balance tests. Since the outcome depends on the participant's physical performance on the force plates, the participant acts partially as an assessor. Furthermore, there is no mention that the personnel operating the testing equipment were blinded (e.g., by covering the orthoses). 

4. 4.4. Could assessment of the outcome have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received?
   • Response: Probably no
   • Reason: The balance score is computed directly and objectively by the computerized NeuroCom EquiTest system, which uses load cells to transmit force information to the computer 100 times per second. Because the outcome is generated by an automated machine measuring involuntary postural sway across six sensory configurations, it does not involve human judgment in the assessment, making it highly unlikely that knowledge of the intervention meaningfully influenced the scoring.

5. 4.5. Is it likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received?
   • Response: Not applicable
   • Reason: This question is not applicable because the answer to 4.4 is 'Probably no'.

Risk-of-bias judgement for Domain 4: Low
Reason: The outcome was measured using an appropriate, objective, computerized system. Although participants and likely the operators were aware of the assigned physical intervention, the automated nature of the EquiTest system does not involve subjective human judgment, making it very unlikely that the outcome assessment was influenced by this knowledge.
