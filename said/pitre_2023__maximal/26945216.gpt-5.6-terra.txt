**Outcome assessed:** Pain intensity at rest measured using a 0–100 mm visual analogue scale (VAS), including the reported immediate and short-term (up to 1 week after treatment) between-group results. The deviations-from-intervention assessment addresses the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The authors report a random sequence-generation method: participants were to be allocated “using **computerized block randomization**,” which supports that the allocation sequence was random. However, the description of concealment is inadequate to establish that allocation was concealed until enrolment and assignment. The report states only that “**allocation group [was] concealed in envelops**.” It does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, tamper-proof, or opened only after irrevocable enrolment. Thus, allocation concealment cannot confidently be judged adequate.

   Baseline pain was reasonably comparable: mean VAS pain was 69.9, 67.2, and 70.3 mm in the TENS, IFT, and control groups, respectively, with no statistically significant baseline difference (*P* > 0.05). There was an imbalance in affected side (right-sided pain: 20/35 TENS, 25/35 IFT, and 20/35 control; *P* = 0.015), but the report provides no basis to conclude that this imbalance was sufficiently large or prognostically important for pain outcomes to demonstrate a failure of randomization. Therefore, the principal concern is insufficiently described allocation concealment, rather than clear evidence of a flawed randomization process.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The study states that participants were blinded to allocated group: “**The participants were blind to the allocation to treatment groups after randomization**.” In practice, blinding is questionable because TENS and IFT have perceptibly different electrode arrangements and the control intervention did not include a sham electrical-treatment procedure. Moreover, the principal investigator delivered all interventions and therefore was aware of treatment assignment.

   Nevertheless, for the effect of **assignment** to intervention, lack of blinding alone does not establish bias. RoB 2 requires evidence or a strong reason to believe that deviations from intended intervention arose because of the trial context, affected pain, and were imbalanced between groups. The report describes a standardized treatment schedule: all groups received standard care and a home programme, while TENS and IFT groups received their allocated electrical modality. It reports that “**all patients were provided with a home program**,” and home-exercise diaries were checked at each treatment visit. No differential cointerventions, differential implementation failures, treatment switching, or other deviations attributable to the trial context are reported.

   All 105 randomized participants completed the study and were analysed in their allocated groups: “**All completed the study and were entered into the analysis**.” Thus, the reported analysis appears consistent with assignment to the randomized groups. On the information available, there is no evidence that trial-context deviations biased the pain comparison.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data appear available for all randomized participants. The CONSORT flow diagram shows 105 participants randomized, with 35 allocated to each group, and the Results section states: “**All completed the study and were entered into the analysis**.” Pain-intensity results are reported for all three groups with *n* = 35 per group at baseline and for the subsequent change scores.

   Since there is no apparent loss to follow-up or missing pain outcome data, missingness cannot plausibly have materially affected the pain result.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The pain measurement itself was appropriate. Pain at rest was measured using a standard 100-mm VAS with anchors “**no pain at all**” and “**worst pain imaginable**,” and pain was assessed at the same scheduled time points across groups.

   However, pain intensity is a participant-reported, subjective outcome. The participant is the outcome assessor for a VAS pain score. Although the authors characterize the study as single blind and state that participants were blind to allocation, this is unlikely to have been maintained convincingly. Participants in the TENS and IFT groups received distinguishable electrical interventions, whereas control participants received standard care without a sham electrical treatment. The principal investigator was also both the therapist and the outcome assessor: “**The principal investigator acted as both outcome assessor, taking all study measurements, and as therapist administering all study treatment.**”

   Knowledge or inference of the received intervention could plausibly influence participants’ pain ratings, especially for immediate post-treatment pain measured “within 5 mins” of the first session. The authors themselves acknowledge that “**not using a placebo for the patients of the control group could be considered as limitations**.” Given subjective self-reported pain, no credible sham for control, and the therapist-assessor’s awareness of allocation, it is likely that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of treatment received. This creates a high risk of bias, likely favouring the electrical-treatment groups, particularly TENS if expectations differed between modalities.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The publication reports pain intensity at the stated measurement occasions—baseline, immediately after the first treatment, before the eighth treatment, and one week after the eighth treatment—and Table 2 reports the corresponding pain-change results. This reduces concern that only a single favourable time point was selectively reported.

   However, the report does not provide a dated, sufficiently detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available. It reports clinical-trial registration with the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry, but the article does not provide the registry identifier or sufficient registration details to verify whether the VAS pain scale, exact time points, change-score analysis, one-way ANOVA approach, and Tukey post-hoc comparisons were all prespecified. The analysis used change from baseline scores, while other eligible approaches could have included analysis of final values, analysis of covariance adjusting for baseline pain, or repeated-measures modelling. No prespecified hierarchy or rationale for selecting among these analyses is reported.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported pain result was selected because it was favourable; therefore high risk is not justified. However, without a pre-specified, date-stamped analysis plan or sufficiently detailed accessible registration information, there remain some concerns regarding selection from multiple possible outcome measurements and analyses.
