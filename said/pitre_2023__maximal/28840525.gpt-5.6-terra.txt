**Outcome assessed:** Type 2 diabetes remission at 1 year, defined as achievement of each of four HbA1c thresholds without glucose-lowering medication: ≤38 mmol/mol (5.6%), <42 mmol/mol (6.0%), <48 mmol/mol (6.5%), and <53 mmol/mol (7.0%).  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to SR-LRYGB versus LSG at baseline.

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The allocation sequence was random, allocation was concealed until participants had been enrolled and anaesthetised, and the reported baseline characteristics do not indicate a material failure of randomization.

* The report states: “**Randomisation was by computer-generated random number codes managed by an independent study member**,” with minimization for important prognostic variables: age, BMI, diabetes duration, insulin treatment, and ethnicity. This is an appropriate random sequence-generation method.
* Allocation concealment appears adequate. The abstract states that patients were randomized using “**random number codes disclosed after anaesthesia induction**.” Thus, treatment assignment was not revealed before enrolment and surgery, reducing the opportunity for selection or manipulation of allocation.
* The groups were close to the intended 1:1 allocation ratio (SR-LRYGB 56; LSG 58).
* Baseline characteristics were generally comparable for key prognostic factors for remission, including age, BMI, duration of diabetes, insulin use, and HbA1c. For example, baseline HbA1c was 64.5 ± 18.1 mmol/mol in SR-LRYGB and 61.9 ± 12.8 mmol/mol in LSG; insulin use was 30% versus 28%; and diabetes duration >10 years was 17/56 versus 15/58.
* Although baseline anxiety was somewhat higher in the LSG group, this is not a key prognostic factor for the objectively measured diabetes-remission outcome and does not suggest a systemic problem with randomization.

### 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

**b. Rationale:**  
Participants and outcome assessors were blinded, postoperative diabetes-medication management was standardized and undertaken by endocrinologists blinded to allocation, and the analysis retained participants in their randomized groups except for those with missing 1-year outcome data. There is no evidence of deviations arising from the trial context that were unbalanced between groups and likely to affect remission.

* This was described as a “**double-blind (patient and assessor) clinical trial**.” Allocation was disclosed after induction of anaesthesia, supporting patient blinding at surgery. Identical port placements and use of methylene-blue leak testing “irrespective of procedure” were also intended to support masking.
* Surgeons necessarily knew the procedure being delivered. However, lack of blinding of the surgical team does not itself establish bias for the assignment effect. The relevant concern would be trial-context deviations—for example, systematically different non-protocol diabetes management—that were caused by knowledge of allocation and affected HbA1c remission.
* Such deviations are not reported. Instead, the investigators state: “**All pharmacological agents for diabetes were discontinued following surgery subject to endocrinologist review prior to discharge. Metabolic medications were commenced post-operatively according to a standard medical review protocol, by endocrinologists blinded to treatment allocation.**”
* This standardized, allocation-blinded medication-management process is particularly relevant because remission required both HbA1c below the threshold and no glucose-lowering medication.
* The remission results were calculated in the randomized surgical groups: 56 SR-LRYGB participants and 53 LSG participants were analysed at 1 year. This is effectively a modified intention-to-treat/available-case analysis, with exclusions due to missing outcomes rather than reassignment according to treatment received. Any bias from the five missing LSG outcomes is considered under the missing-data domain, rather than this domain.
* Some surgical complications and reoperations occurred, but the report provides no evidence that these represented trial-context deviations from the intended intervention or that they caused differential post-randomization management relevant to diabetes-remission ascertainment.

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
Outcome data were unavailable for 5 of 58 participants (8.6%) assigned to LSG, while all 56 SR-LRYGB participants had 1-year data. This differential missingness could affect the dichotomous remission results, but the article does not provide sufficient evidence that missingness was likely related to the unobserved HbA1c/remission status.

* The CONSORT flow diagram indicates that 114 participants were randomized: 58 to LSG and 56 to SR-LRYGB.
* At 1 year, the remission analyses used 53 LSG participants and 56 SR-LRYGB participants. Thus, data were missing for 5 participants, all from the LSG group.
* The article states: “**Loss to follow-up of 5 participants occurred from the LSG group due to failure to make contact and/or to attend appointments beyond 3 months post-operatively.**”
* Therefore, data were not available for all or nearly all participants in the strict RoB 2 sense. For the more stringent HbA1c remission thresholds, the number of missing participants is not trivial relative to the number achieving remission; plausible outcomes among the five missing LSG participants could alter the estimated difference.
* Missingness was differential between groups: 0/56 in SR-LRYGB and 5/58 in LSG. This raises concern because failure to attend follow-up or loss of contact could be associated with health behaviours, engagement in postoperative care, medication use, or glycaemic status.
* However, the report does not state that loss to follow-up was caused by poor glycaemic control, treatment failure, complications, or medication use; nor does it provide evidence that the true remission status of missing participants was systematically different. No sensitivity analysis addressing the potential effect of the missing outcome data was reported.

Accordingly, missingness **could** depend on the true value of remission status, but it is not established as **likely** to do so. This supports **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Diabetes remission was measured using objective HbA1c thresholds together with documented absence of glucose-lowering medication. The same measurement approach was used in both groups, and outcome assessors were blinded to surgical allocation.

* The outcome was explicitly defined using laboratory HbA1c thresholds and medication status: “**proportions achieving HbA1c thresholds … without the need for any glucose lowering therapy**.”
* HbA1c is an appropriate and objective method for assessing glycaemic control. The four remission definitions are objectively reproducible and are not dependent on subjective clinical judgement.
* The same HbA1c thresholds, 1-year assessment time point, and requirement for no diabetes medication were applied to both randomized groups.
* The investigators report that the trial was “**double-blind (patient and assessor)**,” and that “**all clinical data were extracted by medical records review**.” In addition, endocrinologists responsible for postoperative metabolic medication decisions were “**blinded to treatment allocation**.”
* Although participants may potentially have inferred their procedure from postoperative experiences or complications, laboratory HbA1c measurement itself is not plausibly influenced by that knowledge. Medication status could theoretically be affected by clinical decisions, but the prespecified standardized medication-review protocol and blinded endocrinology review reduce this concern substantially.

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The publication reports remission using four HbA1c thresholds at 1 year, whereas the trial registration provides only a broad primary outcome description and does not specify the HbA1c thresholds, time points, medication-free criterion, or a prespecified statistical analysis plan. There is no direct evidence that the reported results were selected because of their findings, but the available registration information is insufficient to exclude selective selection among multiple eligible outcome definitions and analyses.

* The trial registration identifies the primary outcome only as: “**Remission of type 2 diabetes mellitus/ glycaemic control**.” It does not specify:
  * the exact HbA1c threshold(s);
  * whether absence of glucose-lowering medication was required;
  * the relevant follow-up time point;
  * which of several possible remission definitions was primary; or
  * a prespecified statistical analysis plan.
* In contrast, the article reports four alternative 1-year definitions: HbA1c ≤38 mmol/mol, <42 mmol/mol, <48 mmol/mol, and <53 mmol/mol, all without diabetes medication.
* The article describes the primary outcome as “**the proportion of patients achieving HbA1c <42 mmol/mol (<6%) without glucose lowering therapy at 5 years**,” while reporting the four thresholds at 1 year as outcomes intended to facilitate comparison with prior studies. This distinction is clinically reasonable, but it means the 1-year, four-threshold results cannot be fully verified against a detailed prospectively finalized analysis plan from the supplied registration record.
* All four 1-year threshold results are presented, with no indication that only favourable thresholds were reported. The results are consistently null for the comparison of procedures. Thus, there is no affirmative evidence that the selected result was chosen on the basis of its magnitude, direction, or statistical significance.
* Nevertheless, because multiple threshold definitions of remission and potentially multiple analytic choices were available, and because the registry entry is insufficiently detailed to verify prespecification, the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.
