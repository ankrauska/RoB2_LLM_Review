1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was explicitly computer-generated and used a recognized covariate-balanced randomization approach: “*Randomization was performed by means of a computer-generated random sequence with stratification for age, gender, and positive family history for CHD using the Pocock and Simon method.*” The investigator who generated the random allocation sequence is identified.

   Allocation appears to have been effectively concealed until after enrolment/baseline assessment: participants were randomized only “*once a study participant completed the first study visit and scheduled the second study visit*.” Thus, recruitment and baseline-data collection occurred before allocation, limiting the opportunity for knowledge of upcoming assignment to influence enrolment.

   Baseline characteristics were balanced between groups. For example, mean age was identical (59.0 years in both groups), sex distribution was similar (48.5% versus 46.2% male), and no important imbalance was apparent in cardiovascular risk factors, education, BMI, smoking, blood pressure, or lipid measures. The article states that “*baseline characteristics including age, sex, smoking status, and other CHD conventional risk factors were similar between the two groups*.” These data do not suggest a problem with the randomization process.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of being assigned to disclosure of conventional CHD risk alone (CRS) versus genetics-informed CHD risk (CRS×GRS), regardless of subsequent treatment decisions or statin use.

   Participants, genetic counsellors, and physicians were necessarily aware of the assigned risk-disclosure approach; the registry also specifies no masking. However, lack of blinding alone does not establish bias in this RoB 2 domain for the effect of assignment. The relevant question is whether deviations from the intended disclosure intervention arose because of the trial context, affected SDM, and were unbalanced between groups.

   The two intended interventions were delivered as planned: both groups received a genetic-counsellor session, a physician visit, and the Statin Choice decision aid. The difference—whether the decision aid incorporated genetic risk—was the intended randomized intervention. The article describes that “*disclosure of risk in both arms was facilitated by the use of a modified version of the Statin Choice decision aid*.” No material trial-context deviations, differential co-interventions, implementation failures, or contamination between groups are reported.

   The analysis was conducted according to randomized groups: “*Analyses were performed with randomization groups as the independent variables and physician visit satisfaction and SDM scores as the dependent variables.*” All 207 randomized participants completed the second study visit, and the SDM outcome was available for 206 participants. This is consistent with an appropriate randomized-group analysis for the effect of assignment; the one participant without an SDM survey is addressed under missing outcome data.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:** SDM survey data were available for 206 of 207 randomized participants (99.5%). The report states: “*The SDM survey was completed by 206 study participants*.” Therefore, only one participant had missing SDM outcome data.

   For this continuous outcome, data availability for more than 95% of randomized participants would generally be sufficient under RoB 2 to conclude that the missing data could not plausibly have made an important difference to the estimated between-group effect. In addition, all 207 randomized participants completed the relevant second study visit, including the genetic-counsellor and physician encounters. There is no evidence of differential attrition, differential survey completion by arm, or outcome-related reasons for missingness.

   Although the paper does not provide a reason for the single missing SDM survey, the amount of missingness is too small to have a material impact on the reported SDM comparison.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The shared decision-making score was a participant-reported, subjective outcome measured using the 11-item SDM Questionnaire (SDM-Q): “*SDM was evaluated using the SDM Questionnaire (SDM-Q), a validated 11-question survey that assesses patients' perception of the SDM process during clinical encounters.*” Use of a validated questionnaire is an appropriate measurement method, and the same questionnaire and scoring procedure were used in both randomized groups.

   However, the outcome assessors were the participants themselves, and participants were aware of the intervention received: whether their CHD risk disclosure included genetic-risk information. The registry specifies “masking: NONE,” and the intervention was inherently apparent during the disclosure and decision-making encounters. Because SDM-Q assesses the participant’s perceptions of the consultation—including whether their views were considered, whether options were discussed, and whether they felt included—responses could plausibly be influenced by knowledge that genetic information was or was not provided.

   In this context, it is likely that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment. The intervention was not merely a treatment with a separate blinded outcome; it was a different communication/disclosure strategy, and the outcome directly measured the participant’s perceived quality of that communication and decision-making process. This creates a substantial risk of differential reporting or expectation effects favouring either disclosure strategy, even though the study found little difference between groups.

   The OPTION5 analysis of video-recorded encounters provides an observer-based supplementary assessment, but it was conducted only in a random subset of 80 encounters and does not remove the risk of bias in the primary patient-reported SDM-Q score being assessed.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was registered before recruitment began, and the registry identifies “*Shared decision making (as assessed by survey)*” as a secondary outcome. The published report does present a patient survey-based SDM outcome, consistent with that registered outcome. It also reports individual SDM-Q items, the overall SDM-Q score, and an additional observer-rated OPTION5 assessment from video-recorded encounters.

   However, the registry description is insufficiently specific to establish that the reported numerical SDM-Q result was analysed according to a detailed, prespecified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The registration does not specify the SDM instrument, scoring approach, timing, handling of incomplete questionnaires, primary SDM analysis, or whether the observer-rated OPTION5 measure was prespecified. No dated statistical analysis plan is available.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported SDM-Q result was selected from multiple outcome measurements or analyses on the basis of its statistical significance or direction. Indeed, the article reports both the patient-reported SDM-Q and the OPTION5 findings, which were similarly non-significant. Nevertheless, because multiple eligible SDM measures and analytical choices were possible and a sufficiently detailed prespecified analysis plan is unavailable, low risk of bias cannot be confirmed.
