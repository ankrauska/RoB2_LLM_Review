1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The study is described as a “prospective, 1:1, randomized study,” with 80 participants allocated 1:1 (40 conventional care; 40 conventional care plus nutritional counselling). However, neither the publication nor the registry describes how the random allocation sequence was generated or whether allocation was concealed until enrolment and assignment. A statement that allocation was random, without details of sequence generation, is insufficient to establish that the sequence was random under RoB 2; and concealment cannot be assumed.

   Baseline data do not suggest a material problem with randomization. The groups were equal in size and broadly similar for measured prognostic characteristics, including age (68.3 versus 67.9 years), sex, education, baseline MoCA score, BMI category, comorbidities, physical activity, APOE genotype, and MCI type. The authors state: “There were no significant differences between the groups at baseline for age, gender, education, comorbidities, genotype, measurements, or laboratory analysis.”

   Thus, there is no evidence of baseline imbalance suggestive of failed randomization, but lack of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   This assessment concerns the effect of **assignment** to nutritional counselling plus conventional care versus conventional care alone.

   Participants and the personnel delivering care/counselling were necessarily aware of assignment. The intervention comprised 26–28 group nutrition meetings over 12 months, whereas the comparator received conventional medical care; blinding of participants and intervention personnel was therefore not feasible. The registry reports only single masking of the outcome assessor.

   There is evidence that trial-context effects may have reduced the contrast between groups. Although counselling was intended to promote caloric restriction and weight loss, both groups lost weight and the between-group difference was not statistically significant: mean BMI change was −2.1 kg/m² in the intensive group and −1.3 kg/m² in the conventional-care group (*P* = .428). The authors explicitly acknowledge a plausible trial-context explanation:

   > “Because the process of recruiting volunteers included information on the risks of obesity, it is also possible that this may have increased motivation to lose weight in both groups.”

   This could represent a deviation from the intended conventional-care strategy arising from trial recruitment/engagement. Such changes could affect neuropsychological performance, because the authors’ exploratory analyses found BMI reduction to be associated with improvements in several cognitive domains. However, the observed weight loss was relatively similar between groups, suggesting that the relevant deviation may have been broadly balanced rather than clearly differential.

   The primary randomized comparison was analysed using generalized estimating equations with group, time, and group-by-time interaction terms. This preserves randomized group assignment among participants with observed outcome data; exclusions due to missing outcome data are considered in the missing-data domain. Therefore, the analysis approach is acceptable for the assignment effect. Nonetheless, the likely diminution of the intervention contrast through trial-context-related behaviour change in both groups gives **some concerns**.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data at 12 months were available for 75 of 80 randomized participants (93.75%). The flow diagram indicates that 37/40 conventional-care participants and 38/40 intensive-intervention participants were analysed. Thus, missing data were limited in absolute number (five participants) and were similar in frequency between groups.

   The report states:

   > “Five women did not complete the follow-up: 4 dropped out and one died of pneumonia.”

   However, it does not report the intervention group of each dropout, detailed reasons for the four withdrawals, whether they differed by intervention group, or any sensitivity analysis addressing the potential effect of missing neuropsychological outcomes. Neuropsychological performance in older participants with MCI could plausibly be related to health deterioration, cognitive decline, functional limitations, or willingness/ability to attend follow-up; therefore, missingness could depend on the true, unobserved cognitive outcome.

   The amount of missing data is modest and does not by itself establish material bias, particularly because numbers missing appear balanced. Nevertheless, 75/80 is below the approximate 95% level often considered “nearly all” for continuous outcomes, and there is insufficient information to exclude outcome-related missingness. This results in **some concerns** rather than high risk.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Neuropsychological performance was assessed using established instruments covering relevant cognitive domains, including CAMcog, IQCODE, the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test, digit-span tests, Trail Making Tests, verbal fluency, and the Modified Wisconsin Card Sorting Test. These are appropriate methods for measuring cognitive performance in participants with MCI. The same neuropsychological battery was applied at baseline and at 12 months in both randomized groups.

   The registry specifies single masking, with the masked party being the:

   > “OUTCOMES_ASSESSOR.”

   This is important because several neuropsychological tests require administration and scoring by an assessor and could otherwise be affected by subjective judgement or differential encouragement. There is no indication that test methods, timing, or ascertainment differed between groups. Although participants could not be blinded to whether they attended nutritional counselling, the relevant outcome assessor was reportedly blinded, which reduces the risk of differential assessment or scoring.

   The method of outcome measurement was appropriate, comparable across groups, and outcome assessment was masked. Therefore, this domain is at **low risk of bias**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The trial was prospectively registered as NCT01286389 and listed “Neuropsychological test performance” as a primary outcome. This supports that cognition was a prespecified outcome domain. However, the registry entry does not specify the neuropsychological instruments, individual cognitive domains, assessment time points, scoring approaches, primary contrast, or statistical analysis plan. No dated, detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before access to unblinded outcome data is available in the supplied materials.

   The publication reports numerous individual cognitive measures and several derived cognitive-domain analyses. It also reports analyses that were explicitly conducted after observing that there was no material between-group difference in weight loss:

   > “Because both groups lost similar amounts of weight after the intervention period … we decided to **a posteriori pool** the longitudinal analysis between groups and to test for the effects of change of BMI as a continuous variable.”

   Further, the authors acknowledge:

   > “Due to the exploratory nature of the analyses and the relatively small sample, it was not possible to carry a multiple comparisons correction.”

   These post hoc pooled and BMI–cognition association analyses do not estimate the prespecified randomized comparison of assignment to counselling versus conventional care. The report does state that there was “no significant time-group interaction for most of the tests after 12 months,” which reduces concern that only favourable randomized comparisons were reported. However, without a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan, it is not possible to determine whether the reported cognitive measures, time points, domain definitions, or analyses were selected from multiple eligible alternatives based on their results.

   Accordingly, there is no definitive evidence that a specific reported cognitive result was selectively chosen, but the absence of a detailed prespecified analysis plan and the use of acknowledged post hoc exploratory analyses lead to **some concerns**.
