# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Pain VAS Scale (Post-treatment)
## Effect of Interest: Assignment to Intervention (ITT)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise from lack of detail on allocation concealment.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation (random):** "Yes." The article states: "Each participant was assigned to one treatment group, at random, using a numbered list generated through the random permuted blocks method, applied by a statistician." This is an adequate random sequence generation method.
- **Allocation concealment:** "No information." The article does not describe how the allocation sequence was concealed from the person enrolling participants. While a statistician generated the list and was "not involved in data collection or analysis," there is no explicit statement about how (or whether) the sequence was concealed from those enrolling/assigning participants (e.g., no mention of sealed envelopes, central randomization, etc.). Given that random permuted blocks were used, there is also a possibility of predictability of allocation near the end of blocks if concealment was imperfect and block size was fixed/known.
- **Baseline imbalance:** "No" — Table 1 shows no significant differences in age, sex, time since onset, dominant shoulder, or type of injury (all P>0.05). Baseline VAS, CMS, DASH values were also similar (Table 2), and the article states "At baseline, there were no differences between the groups."

Applying the algorithm: sequence random = Yes; allocation concealment = No information; imbalance = No → this maps to "Some concerns" (per Table 4, when concealment is NI and there's no evidence of imbalance problem, but sequence question doesn't reach the "Low" threshold without concealment being adequately established as Yes).

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Single-blinding of participants was not achieved for personnel; no evidence of use of an appropriate ITT analysis strategy is described, though no obvious exclusions/deviations are reported.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Participant awareness (2.1):** Per the trial registry, masking was "SINGLE" with only the participant masked—however, in practice, participants receiving ultrasound (no drug, gel applied externally) vs. iontophoresis (electrical current, distinct sensation) vs. phonophoresis are likely to be able to distinguish which treatment they are receiving due to the very different sensory experience (electrical current for iontophoresis vs ultrasound gel application), making true participant blinding questionable. Answer: "Probably yes" (aware).
- **Personnel awareness (2.2):** The physiotherapists administering treatment were clearly aware of the intervention they were delivering (different equipment/procedures for each arm). Answer: "Yes."
- **Deviations arising from trial context (2.3):** No information is provided suggesting non-protocol co-interventions or contamination occurred due to trial context (e.g., no mention of participants seeking additional treatments due to awareness of assignment). Answer: "No information."
- **Appropriate analysis (2.6):** The article does not explicitly describe handling of participants who deviated from protocol, but the flow appears consistent with all randomized participants being analyzed with data collected at each of the three time points ("Ninety-nine participants... were assigned"; no reported dropouts or exclusions post-randomization in the main text, though a "Fig. A" flow diagram exists in supplementary material that could not be reviewed here). Assuming a standard analysis of all randomized participants who completed follow-up, this is treated as "Probably yes" for appropriate analysis, though this cannot be fully confirmed without the supplementary flow chart.

Given awareness of participants/personnel but no evidence of related deviations affecting outcome, and no information on whether deviations occurred because of trial context, this results in a judgement of "Some concerns."

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Insufficient reporting in the main text on missing data/dropouts for the post-treatment VAS outcome specifically.

**b. Rationale:**
- The main text states sample size calculations "allow[ed] for a 20% dropout rate," implying dropout was anticipated, but the results section does not explicitly report the number of participants analyzed at each time point (post-treatment) versus originally randomized (n=99: 32/33/34 across groups).
- Table 2 presents means and SDs at each visit, but it is unclear if all 99 participants contributed to the post-treatment VAS assessment or if there was any attrition.
- A participant flow diagram is referenced ("Fig. A, see online supplementary material") but is not available in the provided article text, so information about missing outcome data cannot be confirmed here.
- No explicit statement about reasons for or proportions of missing data at post-treatment. This leads to "No information" on the completeness signalling question, and without evidence that missingness (if any) was unrelated to the true outcome value, we cannot rule out risk of bias.

This leads to a default judgement of "Some concerns" (insufficient reporting to confirm low risk, but no strong evidence of differential/high missingness either).

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The VAS is a participant-reported outcome, and given the likely lack of true participant blinding (due to markedly different sensory experiences between ultrasound, phonophoresis, and iontophoresis) combined with the open, single-blind design (only participants nominally blinded, but this is implausible for these interventions), there is a high risk of bias due to potential influence of intervention awareness on self-reported pain scores.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method (4.1):** VAS is an appropriate, standard method for measuring pain. "No" (not inappropriate).
- **Differential measurement (4.2):** The method of measurement (VAS self-report) itself did not differ across groups; the same scale was used identically for all arms. "No."
- **Outcome assessor awareness (4.3):** For participant-reported outcomes, the assessor is the participant. Given the distinct nature of the three interventions (ultrasound gel vs. electrical current for iontophoresis vs. phonophoresis gel + ultrasound), it is highly likely participants could distinguish their treatment allocation despite being nominally "blinded." Additionally, the rehabilitation doctor conducting assessments was "the same blinded rehabilitation doctor," but this pertains to the assessor of CMS (clinician-administered), not the VAS, which is self-reported by the participant. Therefore, for VAS, the participant (as outcome assessor) was likely aware of their intervention: "Probably yes."
- **Could assessment be influenced (4.4):** Yes — participant expectations, beliefs about drug-based vs. non-drug treatments, or the more "active" sensation of iontophoresis could plausibly influence self-reported pain. "Probably yes."
- **Likely influenced (4.5):** Given that this trial explicitly compared an active pharmacological add-on (dexketoprofen) against a non-drug ultrasound comparator, and that participants were not blinded to which physical modality they received (very different procedures/sensations), there is a reasonably high likelihood that participant expectation influenced self-reported VAS pain scores, particularly given that the differences observed between groups were modest and the treatments differ substantially in perceptibility. "Probably yes" — leading to a judgement of "High risk of bias" for this domain.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The primary outcome (VAS post-treatment) was reported as pre-specified in the trial registry, but no protocol or statistical analysis plan was available to confirm that reported analyses correspond fully to pre-specified plans, and multiple time points/comparisons were reported without clear indication that all were pre-specified with a hierarchy.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registry (NCT01748188) lists "Pain measure by the visual analogic scale pain score" as the primary outcome, consistent with reporting of VAS in the article. This supports that the specific outcome domain (pain via VAS) was not selectively swapped for another measure. "Probably yes" — no evidence of switching outcome measures.
- However, no statistical analysis plan (SAP) or full trial protocol was available to verify whether the specific analyses reported (e.g., pairwise comparisons between groups via ANOVA/Scheffé post hoc, and comparisons to baseline via repeated-measures ANOVA) were pre-specified before unblinded data was available, or whether other analyses were conducted but not reported. "No information" for question 5.1.
- The registry does not specify exact statistical methods or timepoint hierarchy (e.g., which pairwise comparisons were primary), and multiple pairwise comparisons across three groups and three time points were conducted and reported (Tables 2 and 3), raising some possibility of selective emphasis on statistically significant findings (e.g., ultrasound vs. iontophoresis differences were highlighted while other comparisons were not significant). No clear evidence of selection based on results, but insufficient information to fully rule this out. "No information" for 5.2/5.3.

Given no protocol/SAP was available for verification, and no direct evidence of selective reporting was found, this results in a judgement of "Some concerns."
