**Outcome assessed:** quality of movement of the impaired upper limb, measured by the Melbourne Assessment of Unilateral Upper Limb Function (MUUL), at 26 weeks. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to CIMT versus bimanual training (BIM) at baseline**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The allocation sequence was random and concealed. Children were matched into pairs before randomization according to important prognostic characteristics—age, sex, side of hemiplegia, and baseline MUUL score—and then allocated using an appropriate random method:

   > “Once matched, children were randomized to pairs using a computer generated list of random numbers and concealed envelopes opened by non-study personnel.”

   Computer-generated random numbers support a genuinely random allocation sequence. The use of concealed envelopes opened by personnel not involved in the study provides reasonable assurance that the allocation was concealed until enrolment/assignment.

   Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable between groups, including the baseline outcome measure: mean MUUL score was 67.1 in the CIMT group and 70.8 in the BIM group. The authors state:

   > “After matching and concealed random allocation within pairs, there were no differences between groups on demographic or baseline measures.”

   Although there was a modest numerical difference in baseline MUUL scores and an imbalance in side of hemiplegia, these differences do not appear sufficiently large or systematic to suggest manipulation or failure of randomization. The matched-pairs process was specifically designed to minimize such imbalances.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Participants, therapists, and other personnel delivering the camp interventions were necessarily aware of allocation because CIMT involved wearing a constraint glove and BIM involved explicitly bimanual activities. Thus, lack of blinding was inherent to the intervention comparison.

   However, for the assignment-to-intervention effect, RoB 2 considers only deviations that arose **because of the trial context**, were inconsistent with the protocol, and were likely to affect the outcome. There is no indication of important trial-context deviations or unequal co-interventions that would have materially affected MUUL outcomes. Both groups received the same planned dose and broad content of intervention:

   > “Each intervention was delivered in groups of 9 to 13 children for 6 hours daily for 10 days… (total 60 hours of intervention).”

   > “Each group received the same amount and overall content of intervention, delivered in the same environment.”

   Adherence was high:

   > “Compliance was 100% for CIMT, and 97% with BIM training.”

   The authors reported that concurrent treatment was largely stable:

   > “At 3 and 26 weeks, there were no changes to pre-existing therapy for all children with the exception of one child (CIMT group) who received upper limb BoNT-A injections between the 3- and 26-week follow-ups.”

   The single participant receiving botulinum toxin A could potentially influence the 26-week MUUL result, but there is no evidence that this was a deviation caused by trial participation or by personnel undermining the allocated comparison. Its isolated occurrence is unlikely to have materially biased the group-level result.

   The reported analysis was consistent with estimating the effect of assignment:  

   > “Analyses were on an intention-to-treat basis according to CONSORT guidelines.”

   The GEE models included participants according to allocated treatment group and used repeated measurements at baseline, 3 weeks, and 26 weeks. Therefore, the analytical approach was appropriate for the assignment effect. Any impact of participants with unavailable outcome data is considered under the missing-outcome-data domain.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data appear available for all or nearly all randomized participants. Sixty-four children were randomized, and the report describes only very limited loss/non-completion:

   > “Sixty-four children (32 matched pairs) were randomly assigned to treatment and 62 children received their allocated intervention.”

   > “There was one dropout on day two of the camp from the BIM training group, because of pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties.”

   The baseline table includes 32 children in the CIMT group and 31 in the BIM group, indicating that at most one participant was unavailable for the presented baseline/outcome analyses in that table. Even accepting the minor discrepancy between the number randomized (64), treated (62), and tabulated at baseline (63), the proportion with potentially missing MUUL data was very small—approximately 2–3%.

   For a continuous outcome such as MUUL, outcome availability for approximately 97% or more of randomized participants would ordinarily be sufficient for the missing observations to be unlikely to make an important difference to the estimated intervention effect. The stated reason for the reported BIM dropout—pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties—does not clearly indicate that missingness depended on the unobserved MUUL value. There is no evidence of substantially differential missingness between groups or of outcome-dependent dropout.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   MUUL was an appropriate outcome measure for assessing the quality of movement/unimanual capacity of the impaired upper limb. The authors describe it as the selected measure for this construct:

   > “The MUUL was selected to measure quality of movement of the impaired upper limb…”

   It was applied at the same scheduled time points in both intervention groups—baseline, 3 weeks, and 26 weeks—so there is no indication that the measurement method or timing differed between groups.

   Importantly, the primary outcome was video-recorded and assessed by raters masked to intervention allocation:

   > “The primary outcome measures were video-taped and scored in random order by trained occupational therapists masked to group allocation.”

   This is particularly important because MUUL scoring involves professional judgement. Blinded central scoring of videos reduces the likelihood that knowledge of CIMT or BIM allocation influenced outcome measurement.

   The report also provides evidence of good reliability of the independent MUUL rater:

   > “The ICC for the total percentage score of MUUL was 0.93 (95% CI 0.84–0.97).”

   Therefore, the measurement approach was suitable, comparable across groups, and protected against differential assessor bias.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   MUUL was clearly identified as a primary outcome in the report, and the article reports MUUL results at the planned assessment points:

   > “The primary outcome measures were [the] Melbourne Assessment of Unilateral Upper Limb Function… and Assisting Hand Assessment…”

   > “Outcome measures were conducted at baseline, 3 and 26 weeks…”

   The report provides between-group results for MUUL at both 3 and 26 weeks, as well as within-group changes. This reduces concern that only a favourable time point was reported. The 26-week MUUL result was reported as:

   > “CIMT had superior outcomes compared with BIM for unimanual capacity at 26 weeks (estimated mean difference [EMD] 4.4, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.2–6.7; p<0.001).”

   Nevertheless, a **low-risk** judgement requires confirmation that the specific MUUL result was analysed according to a sufficiently detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The supplied material identifies a study protocol publication and indicates intention-to-treat GEE analysis, but it does not provide a dated, detailed prespecified statistical analysis plan or enough information from the registration record to establish that the precise analysis—such as the selected model, covariate adjustment, handling of missing observations, and prioritization of time points—was finalized before outcome data were examined.

   In addition, MUUL could potentially be analysed in several eligible ways (for example, post-intervention score, change from baseline, adjusted versus unadjusted models, or different repeated-measures specifications). Although there is no direct evidence that the reported 26-week result was selected because it was favourable to CIMT, the absence of accessible detailed prospective analysis specifications leaves residual uncertainty. Therefore, the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.
