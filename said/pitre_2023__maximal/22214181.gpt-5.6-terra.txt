**Outcome assessed:** List of Attributions Task (LAT), measured immediately post-intervention (fatigue-information [FI] versus sleep-information control). The reported LAT results include the proportion/number of sleep-related attributions, their frequency ranking, and the importance allocated to sleep-related attributions.

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The allocation sequence itself was probably random: the authors report that “a Microsoft Excel random number generator was used to randomize participants into the two conditions.” This supports random sequence generation.

However, allocation concealment was not reported. There is no information that assignment was administered centrally, by an independent party, through concealed envelopes, or by another process that prevented the researcher enrolling participants from foreseeing the next assignment. Since the study investigator individually delivered the intervention, lack of reporting of concealment is relevant.

There was no apparent evidence of a baseline imbalance problem. The authors state that the FI and control groups did not differ significantly at baseline in insomnia, fatigue, depression, dysfunctional sleep beliefs, or any LAT outcomes: “There were also no significant group differences on the LAT outcome measures at baseline.” Group sizes were also equal in the analysed sample (44 per group).

Thus, random sequence generation and baseline balance are reassuring, but the unreported allocation-concealment procedure leads to **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.

---

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

**b. Rationale:**  
Participants and the study investigator delivering the materials were necessarily aware of the assigned information condition. The FI group received information about non-sleep causes of fatigue, whereas the control group received sleep-related information. The article states: “The study investigator read the information sheet alongside each participant individually.”

Nevertheless, for the effect of assignment to intervention, awareness alone does not imply bias. The relevant question is whether deviations from the intended interventions arose *because of the trial context*, affected the outcome, and were unbalanced between groups.

The intervention appears to have been delivered as intended to both groups. The paper does not report crossovers, contamination, unequal cointerventions, or protocol departures. Both groups had individual sessions, comparable quantities of information, and repeated LAT assessment. The authors explicitly designed the control material to control for the time and sleep-related content of the FI intervention: “The control intervention consisted of a comparable amount of information to the FI condition.”

The analysis also appears to retain participants in their randomized groups: the analysed sample comprised 44 participants per group, and the reported analyses were group-by-time mixed ANOVAs. There is no indication of an as-treated or per-protocol analysis based on post-randomization adherence.

Although demand characteristics and priming are plausible in this unblinded psychoeducational study, these principally concern how participants reported the subjective LAT outcome, and are considered under measurement of the outcome rather than as deviations from intended intervention. No important trial-context deviations from the assigned interventions were documented.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **some concerns**

**c. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**d. Rationale:**  
The final analysed sample was 88 participants, with 44 in each group. However, the report indicates that five participants’ data were excluded from analysis:

> “Five participants’ data were not included in the analyses.”

The stated reasons were that two participants were younger than 18 years, one did not complete demographic information, and two did not complete the outcome measures in a way permitting calculation of a total score.

The exclusion of the two participants aged under 18 may represent exclusion of participants who did not meet an intended eligibility criterion, although the article also states that all introductory psychology students were eligible. More importantly for the LAT outcome, two participants had unusable outcome measures. It is unclear whether the participant missing demographic information had complete LAT data but was nevertheless excluded from all analyses.

The missing/excluded proportion is approximately 5% if 93 individuals initially participated. This is close to, but not clearly within, the “nearly all” threshold for a continuous outcome. The report provides reasons for exclusions, but does not report exclusions separately by randomized group, does not provide a participant flow diagram, and does not demonstrate that missingness could not be related to the true LAT outcome. For example, inability to complete the LAT adequately might plausibly be related to participants’ capacity or willingness to generate and rank attributions.

There is no sensitivity analysis or other analysis demonstrating that the results were robust to missing LAT data. Therefore, the amount of missing data is not clearly sufficient to establish low risk, but there is insufficient evidence that missingness was likely dependent on the true outcome value to justify high risk.

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **high risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The LAT was developed specifically for this study and required participants to self-report, immediately after the information intervention, factors that accounted for their own daytime fatigue, along with rankings and importance ratings. The outcome assessor was therefore the participant.

The method was applied similarly across groups at the same pre- and post-intervention time points. Coding of the written responses had some safeguards: a laboratory volunteer “was blind to the study hypotheses,” and the investigator reviewed coding while “blind to participants’ random assignment.” These procedures reduce concern about bias in categorizing responses as sleep- versus non-sleep-related.

However, the central LAT outcome itself is participant-reported and highly susceptible to knowledge of the assigned intervention. Participants in the FI condition had just received information explicitly describing multiple non-sleep causes of fatigue, immediately before being asked to list and rate possible causes of their fatigue. Thus, outcome assessment could readily have been influenced by intervention knowledge and the study context.

The authors acknowledge this limitation directly:

> “It is also possible that demand characteristics or priming played a role in FI participants’ post-intervention responses on the LAT.”

They further note that:

> “the information-based intervention may have influenced participants to respond in a certain manner.”

The intervention was intended to alter fatigue attributions, so a change in actual attributions is a legitimate potential effect. However, the immediate, unblinded, self-reported outcome cannot distinguish a genuine change in personal beliefs from reporting shaped by demand characteristics, recall of the educational material, or perceived expectations. The intervention’s content directly supplied the kinds of non-sleep explanations that participants were then asked to generate. Accordingly, it is likely that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced outcome assessment.

### 5. Selective reporting: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The article describes three LAT-derived outcomes in the methods and reports results for all three:

1. proportion of sleep-related attributions (`NumberAttributionScore`);
2. frequency/rank score for sleep-related attributions (`FrequencyScore`);
3. proportion of importance allocated to sleep-related attributions (`ImportanceScore`).

The corresponding group-by-time analyses are reported, including effect estimates in the form of F statistics and p-values. This consistency between the stated methods and reported LAT results is reassuring. For example, the authors explicitly describe the predicted changes in the number/proportion, rank/frequency, and importance of sleep-related attributions, and subsequently report analyses for each.

However, there is no accessible pre-specified statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the specific LAT scoring rules, choice of analyses, and the reported post-intervention results were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The supplied report does not provide a registration or protocol date-stamped before data analysis, nor does it state that the analysis plan was pre-specified.

In addition, the LAT permitted several possible ways to summarize responses—for example, raw numbers of sleep and non-sleep attributions, proportions, rankings, and likelihood ratings. The report does not establish that the selected aggregate scores and analyses were the only analyses performed or were chosen independently of the observed results.

There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selectively chosen because it was favourable, so high risk is not justified. However, the absence of a clearly pre-specified, finalized analysis plan means that selective reporting cannot be ruled out.
