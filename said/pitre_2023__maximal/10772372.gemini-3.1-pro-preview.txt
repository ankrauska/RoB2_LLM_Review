**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
a. Judgement: The allocation sequence was random, but there is no information provided on whether the allocation sequence was concealed. There were no obvious baseline imbalances to suggest a problem with the randomization process.
b. Rationale: The trial publication states, "Patients were randomized to telephone contact or no telephone contact using a computer-generated list of random numbers." However, no details on allocation concealment (such as the use of opaque sealed envelopes or central randomization) are reported. The authors note, "Baseline characteristics were similar for both groups," which does not suggest a failure of randomization, but the lack of concealment details leads to some concerns.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Participants and personnel were aware of the assigned intervention due to the nature of the trial (telephone contact vs. no contact). However, there is no evidence that deviations from the intended intervention occurred because of the trial context. Furthermore, all participants were analyzed in the groups to which they were randomized, consistent with an appropriate analysis of the effect of assignment to intervention.
b. Rationale: The intervention (weekly telephone calls) precludes blinding. The study reports that "Thirty patients were enrolled in the study, none of whom was lost to follow-up" and that "Fifteen patients were randomized to telephone contact and 15 to no telephone contact." There were no post-randomization exclusions, indicating an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis approach was used. 

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
c. Judgement: Outcome data were available for all randomized participants, meaning the results could not be biased by missing outcome data.
d. Rationale: The authors explicitly state, "Thirty patients were enrolled in the study, none of whom was lost to follow-up." Therefore, missing outcome data is not an issue in this trial.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The methods used to measure the outcome (compliance) were appropriate and comparable between groups. Although outcome assessors were likely aware of the intervention received, the assessment methods used (pill counts and pharmacy refill records) are objective and unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of the intervention.
b. Rationale: The outcome was measured objectively: "Compliance was determined by pill and packet counts (not performed within the patient’s view) at the 6- and 12-week clinic visits. To assess long-term compliance, pharmacies at which patients filled their prescriptions were contacted at 1 and 2 years to document refill information." Because these are objective measures (counting pills and checking pharmacy records), the lack of blinding is highly unlikely to have influenced the outcome assessment.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
a. Judgement: The trial results were reported in a manner consistent with the methods section of the publication. However, there is no evidence of a pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial registration available to definitively rule out selective reporting of outcomes or analyses.
b. Rationale: The authors reported compliance at all the time points specified in the methodology (6 weeks, 12 weeks, 1 year, and 2 years). Nevertheless, without a date-stamped protocol or trial registry entry finalized before unblinded data were available, a judgment of "some concerns" is appropriate according to RoB 2.0 guidance.
