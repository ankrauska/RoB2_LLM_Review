# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Rate of Emergency Hemostasis

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise primarily from lack of information about allocation concealment, despite an adequate random sequence generation method and no evidence of problematic baseline imbalance.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation:** The article states "A random number table was produced through Microsoft Excel. The patients were divided into a control group and a therapy group following the random number table." This constitutes an adequate random component (Answer: Yes).
- **Allocation concealment:** There is no information provided regarding how the allocation sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants. No mention of sealed envelopes, central randomization, or any concealment mechanism (Answer: No information).
- **Baseline imbalance:** The baseline characteristics table shows no statistically significant differences between groups (all P-values >0.05, e.g., sex P=1.000, age P=0.245, active esophageal bleeding P=1.000, Child-Pugh score P=0.325). These differences are compatible with chance (Answer: No).

Per Table 4 of the RoB 2 guidance: when sequence is random (Yes) but allocation concealment has "No information" and imbalance suggests no problem (No), the default judgement is **"Some concerns."**

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to baseline): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as blinding of participants/personnel was not feasible or necessary for this objective, procedurally-determined outcome, and an appropriate intention-to-treat-consistent analysis was used.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Awareness of intervention:** This trial compared two procedural strategies (simultaneous vs. sequential embolization and EVL). By nature of the interventions, participants and treating endoscopists were aware of which intervention was being delivered at the time (Answer: Yes to both 2.1 and 2.2). This is an open-label procedural trial; blinding is not feasible.
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** There is no evidence in the article that non-protocol interventions or deviations arose *because of trial context* (e.g., due to recruitment/consent processes or clinician's lack of equipoise). The interventions in both groups were carried out per protocol as planned—embolization for gastric varices was performed in all patients, followed by EVL either simultaneously (therapy group) or after 2 months (control group). No indication that clinicians deviated from the assigned strategy based on the trial context itself (Answer: No/Probably no to 2.3).
- **Analysis:** All 172 randomized patients (90 in therapy, 82 in control) appear to have been analyzed according to their randomized group; the "rate of emergency hemostasis" was reported for all patients in each arm without exclusions. This is consistent with an intention-to-treat approach (Answer: Yes to 2.6, appropriate analysis).

Per Table 6 (Part 1): Awareness (Yes) + No deviations from trial context (No) → Low risk for Part 1. Combined with appropriate analysis (Part 2: Low), overall domain judgement is **Low risk of bias.**

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as outcome data (emergency hemostasis) were available for all randomized participants in both groups.

**b. Rationale:**
- The outcome of "rate of emergency hemostasis" was reported for the full analyzed cohort: "Hemorrhaging was controlled in all 90 patients in the therapy group, the rate of emergency hemostasis was 100.0%. In the control group, hemorrhaging was controlled in 72 out of 82 patients and the rate of emergency hemostasis was 87.8%."
- This outcome, by its nature, is ascertainable in the immediate/acute treatment period for essentially all patients (it reflects whether bleeding was stopped during the index procedure), and there is no indication of loss to follow-up or missing data for this particular outcome. All 90 and all 82 patients contributed data to this outcome (Answer: Yes to signalling question 3.1).

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to lack of blinding of outcome assessors and the outcome measurement method potentially differing between groups given the unblinded, non-standardized assessment of hemostasis, though the outcome itself has relatively low subjectivity.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** The method for measuring hemostasis (endoscopic visualization of bleeding cessation) is appropriate and standard for this outcome (Answer: No to 4.1 – not inappropriate).
- **Comparability of measurement between groups:** Because control group patients could receive rescue EVL if embolization alone failed to control bleeding ("Hemorrhaging in 10 patients among the 20 patients with active esophageal variceal bleeding was not controlled and EVL was performed for the patients whose hemorrhage was not controllable. Hemorrhaging was controlled after EVL"), there is potential ambiguity in how "emergency hemostasis" was defined/timed across groups — i.e., whether hemostasis was assessed immediately after the *initial* procedure only, or after any rescue intervention. This raises the possibility that ascertainment methods/timing differed between groups (Answer: Probably yes to 4.2, some difference in ascertainment possible).
- **Blinding of outcome assessors:** No blinding of endoscopists/outcome assessors is described or plausible in this open-label procedural trial; the assessor (endoscopist) directly performing the procedure would be aware of which treatment strategy was used (Answer: Yes to 4.3).
- **Potential for influence on assessment:** This outcome (visualized cessation of active bleeding) involves some degree of clinical judgment (observer-reported outcome involving some judgement), though it is relatively objective compared to patient-reported outcomes. Given that unblinded proceduralists determined both the intervention and outcome ascertainment, and that a somewhat subjective judgment of "controlled" bleeding is involved, there is potential for influence, but no strong evidence that assessment was in fact biased by knowledge of treatment assignment (Answer: Probably yes to 4.4 potential influence; No/Probably no to 4.5 – no strong evidence it was *likely* influenced).

Based on Table 12: with 4.1=No, 4.2=Probably yes (or uncertain), 4.3=Yes, 4.4=Yes, 4.5=No/Probably no → default judgement is **Some concerns.**

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to the absence of a publicly available trial protocol or pre-specified statistical analysis plan against which to verify that the reported result was not selectively reported from multiple analyses or outcome definitions.

**b. Rationale:**
- No trial registration record was identified/available for this study (the study was conducted 2000–2006, prior to widespread mandatory prospective trial registration), and no protocol or statistical analysis plan is referenced or available for comparison.
- The outcome "rate of emergency hemostasis" is reported in a straightforward manner consistent with the stated Methods, and there is no internal evidence (e.g., inconsistent definitions, unusual subgroup reporting, or discrepant outcome sets) suggesting selective reporting specifically for this outcome.
- However, in the absence of a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available, it is not possible to confirm that this result was not selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible analyses (e.g., alternative definitions of hemostasis, different timepoints for assessing rebleeding/hemostasis) (Answer: No information to signalling questions 5.1–5.3).

Per Table 14: "No information" on pre-specified plan and on selection from multiple outcomes/analyses → default judgement is **Some concerns.**

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

**Summary:** The trial is judged to have "Some concerns" for the outcome of rate of emergency hemostasis. This is driven by (1) lack of reported allocation concealment procedures, (2) potential inconsistency in outcome ascertainment/definition timing between groups combined with lack of blinding of outcome assessors, and (3) absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan or trial registration. No domain was judged to be at **high** risk of bias, and the domains of deviations from intended intervention and missing outcome data were judged to be at **low** risk of bias. Per Table 1 criteria, since at least one domain (in fact, three domains) raised "Some concerns" without any domain reaching "High risk," the overall judgement is **Some concerns**, though the multiplicity of domains with concerns means confidence in the result is somewhat reduced.
