**Outcome assessed:** HAM-D (Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression) score for **sertraline versus placebo**, assessed for the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The allocation sequence appears to have included a genuine random component: participants were assigned using “**a block design (blocks of 8) with computer-generated random number sequences**.” This supports random sequence generation.

However, allocation concealment is insufficiently described. Allocation was reportedly implemented using “**individual presealed envelopes**,” and later “**by the pharmacy**.” The report does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, opened only after irrevocable enrolment, or otherwise protected from prediction/manipulation. Thus, it cannot be confirmed that allocation was adequately concealed until participants were enrolled.

Baseline data also require some caution. The randomized sample was reasonably balanced in numbers (27 sertraline; 25 placebo), but the published baseline table is based on the 41 completers rather than all 52 randomized participants. There were apparent differences in injury severity (mild TBI: 20% sertraline versus 50% placebo) and sex distribution, although the authors report no statistically significant group differences in measured baseline variables. These differences are not sufficiently extreme, on their own, to establish a failure of randomization, but assessing them among completers rather than all randomized participants limits reassurance.

Therefore, random sequence generation was adequate, but incomplete information on allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement.

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
This was described as a “**double-blind randomized controlled trial**.” The pharmacy “**implemented masking and random assignment**,” and medication and placebo had “**identical markings**.” The article explicitly states that “**both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received**.” Therefore, participants, treating clinicians, and study personnel were unlikely to have knowingly delivered differential co-interventions or altered treatment because of intervention assignment.

Participants were asked not to seek new psychosocial or pharmacological treatments during the trial, and study contacts were standardized: “**research staff followed a standard protocol, and the duration of each contact was kept constant**.” No evidence is reported of important deviations arising specifically from the trial context, differential co-interventions, or differential implementation of the protocol between groups.

For the effect of assignment to intervention, the analysis was broadly appropriate. The authors state that an “**intent-to-treat analysis compared the outcomes of all study participants, including participants who received minimal treatment**.” The repeated-measures random-effects models also incorporated available observations at six time points and were stated to tolerate differing numbers of observations. Although missing data raise major concerns separately, the available analyses did not appear to reassign participants according to treatment received or exclude participants merely because of non-adherence.

Accordingly, there is no evidence that deviations from intended intervention, caused by the trial context, materially biased the effect of assignment.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **high risk of bias**

**c. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**d. Rationale:**  
Outcome data were not available for all or nearly all randomized participants. Of 52 randomized participants, only 41 completed the trial: 11/52 (21%) did not complete follow-up. Attrition was similar numerically between groups according to the registry (5/27 sertraline and 6/25 placebo), but the amount of missing data is substantial for a small trial.

Importantly, there is evidence that missingness was related to depression severity—the outcome being assessed. The authors report that participants who dropped out had higher HAM-D scores at their last assessment than those who remained in treatment: “**the dropouts tending to have higher HAM-D scores**” (*F*=4.0, *P*=.052). This indicates that missing HAM-D values could plausibly depend on the unobserved true outcome value.

Reasons for non-completion included clinical deterioration or adverse events, as well as non-adherence to study procedures:
- one participant was removed because of increased anxiety;
- one because of increased depression after a family death;
- one because of an allergic reaction;
- eight others missed appointments, did not collect medication, or did not respond to follow-up.

These reasons could be associated with participants’ mental health status and hence their missing HAM-D outcomes. In addition, two of the clinically removed participants were in the placebo group, although the group-specific distribution of all dropout reasons is not fully reported.

The authors used a last-observation-carried-forward (LOCF) approach for some analyses. LOCF does not establish that the result is robust to plausible values of the missing final HAM-D scores, particularly when those who discontinued had worse depression scores. No sensitivity analysis addressing missing-not-at-random assumptions was reported.

Thus, the substantial attrition, evidence that dropouts had poorer depression scores, and lack of adequate robustness analyses support a high risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The HAM-D is an appropriate outcome measure for depressive symptom severity. It is a “**17-item HAM-D**” and is described as a “**widely used clinician-rated measurement of depression severity**.” The outcome was measured repeatedly at the same planned study visits in both treatment groups.

The trial used masking procedures that extended to study staff: “**both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received**.” In addition, depression and anxiety symptoms were rated by “**a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist … independently from the physician**,” and final assessments were administered by “**a different staff member than the person who administered the initial assessment**.” These procedures reduce the potential for differential assessment related to clinicians’ involvement in dose adjustment or treatment delivery.

Because outcome assessors were apparently blinded, and because the same clinician-rated HAM-D method and assessment schedule were used across groups, differential measurement error due to knowledge of treatment assignment is unlikely. The outcome measure itself was suitable for the outcome domain.

### 5. Selective reporting: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The trial registration record identifies depression/HAM-D at the end of treatment as a primary outcome, which is consistent with the article’s identification of the HAM-D as the primary outcome measure. The registry also identifies an end-of-treatment time frame of approximately 10 weeks, consistent with the publication.

However, the registration does not provide a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan for the specific reported HAM-D result. It does not clearly prespecify:
- the primary analytic model (e.g., random-effects repeated-measures model, ANCOVA, or endpoint comparison);
- whether the principal result would use total HAM-D score, change from baseline, endpoint score, or responder status;
- the handling of missing data, including LOCF;
- the role of the six biweekly assessments;
- adjustment variables or subgroup/subscale analyses.

The article reports multiple potentially eligible HAM-D analyses and measurements: total HAM-D scores over six time points, baseline-to-endpoint repeated-measures analyses, random-effects models, LOCF analyses, responder analyses, and four alternative HAM-D subscales. Two subscales yielded nominally significant group effects, whereas the total HAM-D analyses did not. Although the principal total-HAM-D result is reported, the absence of a detailed pre-specified analysis plan finalized before outcome data were available means that selection among multiple possible measurements and analyses cannot be excluded.

There is no clear evidence that the specific total HAM-D result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable. Therefore, high risk is not justified. Nevertheless, the lack of a detailed prospective analysis plan and the availability of multiple outcome analyses result in some concerns about selective reporting.
