**Outcome assessed:** participant-reported pain on the visual analogue scale (VAS), principally the reported improvement at discharge after 25 treatment sessions (active therapeutic ultrasound plus usual physiotherapy versus sham ultrasound plus the same usual physiotherapy). The assessment targets the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The report states that participants were assigned using “sex-stratified randomization” and that “the allocation sequence was blinded through the use of sealed envelopes.” This supports an intention to randomize and conceal allocation, but key details required to establish low risk are absent.

   * **Random sequence generation:** The trial does not state how the sequence within sex strata was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, random permuted blocks). The abstract’s phrase “assigned by simple random selection” is not sufficiently detailed to confirm a random component.
   * **Allocation concealment:** “Sealed envelopes” alone is insufficient to establish adequate concealment. The report does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, opened sequentially, or opened only after irreversible enrolment.
   * **Baseline balance:** Baseline VAS values were reasonably similar: control median 7.0 (IQR 1.9) and experimental median 7.4 (IQR 2.3), with *p* = 0.421. There is therefore no evident VAS imbalance suggesting a failure of randomization. Group sizes were also equal (27 per group).

   Thus, there is no evidence that randomization failed, but inadequate reporting of both sequence generation and allocation-concealment procedures prevents a low-risk judgement.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The study was described as having “blinding of patients and blind assessor.” Participants in the control group received a sham intervention: “the ultrasound was off (sham US),” whereas the experimental group received active ultrasound. Both groups received the same massage, exercise, microwave treatment and subsequent 15-session protocol without ultrasound.

   Although the physiotherapist delivering treatment could not be blinded—acknowledged by the authors as a limitation—there is no report of departures from the assigned protocols, differential co-interventions, crossover, or differential treatment intensity apart from the planned active versus sham ultrasound contrast. The report states:

   > “During the study no patient was lost or dropped out.”

   and:

   > “The analysis was done by intention to treat.”

   For the assignment effect, the relevant concern would be deviations caused by the trial context that were inconsistent with the protocol, affected VAS pain, and were unbalanced between groups. No such deviations are reported or otherwise suggested. The intended comparison itself was maintained: active ultrasound versus placebo ultrasound during the first 10 sessions, with identical co-interventions and later treatment in both arms.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data appear available for all randomized participants. The flowchart reports that 54 eligible consenting participants were randomized, 27 to each group, and the article explicitly states:

   > “During the study no patient was lost or dropped out.”

   The VAS outcome was measured before session 1, session 6, session 11, and at completion of session 25. There is no indication of missing VAS observations, exclusions from the VAS analysis, or differential loss between groups. With no reported attrition, there is no basis to suspect that missingness depended on the true pain level or that missing outcome data biased the result.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   VAS is an appropriate and commonly used instrument for measuring pain intensity. The same method and assessment schedule were used in both trial arms:

   > “A physiotherapist appraiser assessed the patients’ pain using the VAS and algometer before starting the 1st … 6th … and 11th sessions and on completing the 25th session.”

   The report also describes a “blind assessor,” so the physiotherapist recording VAS data was intended to be unaware of intervention allocation. Importantly, for a VAS pain outcome the participant is the ultimate outcome assessor because pain is participant-reported. The authors state that patients were blinded, and the comparator was sham ultrasound with the machine switched off. On the information available, participant awareness of active versus placebo ultrasound is not established.

   While the success of patient blinding was not tested or reported, there is no direct evidence that participants knew their allocation or that VAS measurement differed between groups. Therefore, the risk of differential outcome measurement is judged low.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   No statistical analysis plan is reported in the article. Although a trial-registration record is referenced in the request, its substantive contents—such as registered primary outcome, VAS time point, analysis population, and pre-specified analysis method—are not available in the supplied material. It is consequently not possible to confirm that the reported VAS discharge analysis followed an analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The article indicates that VAS was measured repeatedly:

   > “A physiotherapist appraiser assessed the patients’ pain using the VAS … before starting the 1st … 6th … and 11th sessions and on completing the 25th session.”

   However, the published tables provide baseline values and improvement results after 10 and 25 sessions, but do not provide the VAS results at the sixth-session assessment. The report emphasizes the statistically significant VAS difference at discharge (*p* = 0.021, one-sided), whereas the between-group difference after 10 ultrasound sessions was non-significant (*p* = 0.373). This pattern does not prove selective reporting, but it demonstrates multiple eligible measurement time points and analysis choices.

   In addition, the analysis used improvement indices and reports one-sided significance tests, but there is no pre-specified plan explaining why this metric and one-sided testing were chosen. In the absence of a dated, sufficiently detailed protocol or statistical analysis plan, selective choice among VAS time points and/or analytic approaches cannot be excluded.
