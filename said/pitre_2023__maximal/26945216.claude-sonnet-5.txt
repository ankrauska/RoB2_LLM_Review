# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Pain Intensity

---

## 1. Bias due to Randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation:** The trial states "computerized block randomization" was used — "allocated to one of three treatment interventions using computerized block randomization." This satisfies the requirement for a random component (Y).
- **Allocation concealment:** Allocation was concealed using envelopes: "allocation group concealed in envelops." However, there is no description of whether envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, or sealed, and no detail on safeguards to prevent tampering. This is insufficient detail to confirm robust concealment, leading to a "Probably yes/No information" judgement rather than a clear "Yes."
- **Baseline imbalance:** Baseline pain intensity (PI1) did not differ significantly between groups (F=2.96, P=0.06), which is compatible with chance. However, there was a statistically significant imbalance in a potentially prognostic variable — affected side (right vs. left), χ²=5.952, P=0.015 — with the IFT group having a notably higher proportion with right-sided pain (25/35) versus TENS (20/35) and Control (20/35). Age also showed borderline imbalance (P=0.061), with the Control group being somewhat older. While these could be due to chance, the significant imbalance in a plausible prognostic factor (laterality), combined with incompletely described allocation concealment, raises some concern about randomization integrity.

Given adequate (but not fully detailed) allocation concealment, a random sequence generation method, and a statistically significant baseline imbalance not clearly attributable to chance, this domain is judged as **Some concerns**.

---

## 2. Deviations from the Intended Intervention (Effect of Assignment to Intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- **Awareness of assignment:** This was a "single-blind" trial: "The participants were blind to the allocation to treatment groups after randomization." However, participants received either TENS or IFT with clearly distinguishable electrode configurations, numbers of electrodes, and different physical sensations (TENS uses 2 electrodes and a distinctive stimulation pattern versus IFT's 4 electrodes/tetrapolar arrangement, and different frequency currents). Given the physical differences in electrode placement/number/sensation, it is very likely participants could distinguish which modality they received, undermining blinding despite the "single-blind" label.
- **Personnel awareness:** Critically, "The principal investigator acted as both outcome assessor, taking all study measurements, and as therapist administering all study treatment." Thus, the treating personnel (and outcome assessor) were fully aware of intervention assignment — a major source of potential bias.
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** Given that the same unblinded individual delivered treatment, assessed outcomes, and had a stated hypothesis based on prior literature, there is a substantial risk that expectation bias affected how pain intensity was assessed, or how much reassurance/encouragement was given to participants in each arm (differential trial-context deviations are plausible, e.g., encouragement, verbal reassurance, quality of delivery).
- **Balance of deviations:** There is no information suggesting any measures were taken to standardize interactions across groups to offset the unblinded, single-person delivery/assessment model. This raises high concern that deviations (e.g., differential encouragement or performance effects) were not balanced between groups.
- **Analysis:** All 105 randomized participants were included in the analysis (105/105 completed), consistent with an ITT-type analysis for this outcome, which is appropriate for question of assignment effect analysis.

Because participants were likely not effectively blinded (due to detectable differences in modality), and the single unblinded individual delivered all interventions AND assessed the subjective, participant-reported outcome (pain), with no evidence that these influences were balanced across arms, and this deviation likely influenced the pain-intensity outcome — this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

---

## 3. Missing Outcome Data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- The CONSORT flow chart and text confirm no attrition: "There were 105 individuals who expressed interest in participating in the study. All completed the study and were entered into the analysis."
- Outcome data (pain intensity) were available for all 105 randomized participants at all four time points (baseline, immediately after first treatment, before eighth treatment, and 1 week after eighth treatment).
- With 100% of participants having complete outcome data, missingness cannot have biased the result.

---

## 4. Measurement of the Outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of measurement method:** Pain intensity was measured using a validated 100-mm visual analog scale (VAS), a reasonable and widely accepted method for this outcome; the method itself is not inappropriate.
- **Comparability of measurement method:** The same VAS was used across all groups, so no evidence that measurement methods differed structurally between arms.
- **Outcome assessor blinding:** Pain intensity is a participant-reported outcome; therefore, the participant is the outcome assessor. As discussed above, blinding of participants to intervention (TENS vs. IFT vs. control) was likely compromised due to detectable differences in electrode number/placement and sensation between modalities (and the very fact that the control group received no electrical stimulation at all makes it obvious to control participants they received "no additional treatment"). Furthermore, "The principal investigator acted as both outcome assessor, taking all study measurements, and as therapist administering all study treatment" — meaning the same unblinded individual administered treatment and recorded the participant's pain scores, allowing further opportunity for conscious or unconscious influence (e.g., interviewer effects, cueing, expectation) on the recorded VAS values.
- **Potential for influence:** Because pain intensity is a subjective, participant-reported outcome, it is highly susceptible to expectation and placebo-type effects. Given the lack of true blinding (participants could distinguish interventions, especially control vs. active treatment) and the unblinded assessor role of the principal investigator, it is likely (not merely possible) that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of intervention received — particularly relevant to a comparison against a no-electrotherapy control condition, and plausible even between TENS and IFT given their differing tactile sensations.

Given these considerations — a participant-reported outcome, likely unblinded participants, and an unblinded single assessor/therapist — this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Selective Reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial was prospectively registered ("the study was registered as a clinical trial with the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry"), allowing comparison of pre-specified vs. reported outcomes.
- Pain intensity was clearly pre-specified as a primary outcome measured at standard, pre-defined time points (baseline, immediately after first treatment, before eighth treatment, 1 week after eighth treatment), and all four time points were fully reported in Table 2 with consistent analysis (change from baseline, one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc).
- There is no indication that multiple pain scales, alternate cut-points, or alternate analytic approaches were tried and selectively reported; only one pain measure (100-mm VAS) and one straightforward analytic approach (change from baseline compared via ANOVA) were used and reported completely across all groups and time points.
- No evidence of discrepancy between the registered trial design and the outcomes/analyses reported in the manuscript for pain intensity.

Based on the information available (registry entry, consistent and complete reporting of the pre-specified pain outcome across all time points and groups), this domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

**Overall Risk of Bias for Pain Intensity: HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

Per RoB 2.0 guidance, a judgement of "High risk of bias" in any single domain results in an overall "High risk of bias" judgement. Here, two domains (deviations from intended intervention, and measurement of the outcome) are judged at high risk, both driven by the same core methodological limitation: the principal investigator served simultaneously as therapist and outcome assessor for a subjective, participant-reported outcome, in a trial where blinding of participants across three visibly/physically distinguishable interventions (TENS, IFT, no-electrotherapy control) was unlikely to be fully successful.
