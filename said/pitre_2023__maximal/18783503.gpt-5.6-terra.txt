1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The report describes an explicitly random and concealed allocation process: participants were “assigned randomly” using “a randomization automated system on a one-to-one basis.” Importantly, “the randomization list was held securely, and released only after study completion.” This provides reasonable assurance both that the sequence contained a random component and that recruiters could not foresee upcoming allocations.

   The groups were equal in size at baseline (20 participants each). Reported baseline values did not suggest an important imbalance: mean baseline SOWS scores were 12.6 (SD 7.3) in the gabapentin group and 14.9 (SD 10.4) in the placebo group, and the authors reported no significant baseline differences in age, methadone dose, or use of symptomatic medicines. The observed SOWS difference is compatible with chance in this small trial and does not, by itself, suggest a failure of randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   *Effect of interest: effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.*

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Participants received gabapentin or placebo “under double-blind conditions.” The placebo capsules were “identical appearing,” and placebo participants took the same number of capsules as those allocated to gabapentin. These measures make it unlikely that participants, carers, or those delivering treatment knew the assigned intervention.

   Both groups underwent the same methadone-assisted detoxification programme and could receive symptomatic medication, including clonidine, oxazepam, or trazodone, “if required.” The authors reported no significant between-group difference in doses of methadone or symptomatic medicines. Thus, there is no evidence of important differential co-interventions arising from the trial context.

   Three placebo-group participants discontinued or were excluded because of non-compliance and illicit/over-the-counter drug use. However, for the effect of assignment, non-adherence or treatment discontinuation does not itself cause bias unless it arose because of the trial context and led to differential deviations from protocol. The report does not provide evidence that these deviations were caused by knowledge of allocated treatment or other trial-context effects. The reported analysis compared the randomized groups, with exclusions apparently attributable to missing follow-up data; this is acceptable for this RoB 2 domain, with the consequences of missing data considered separately.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data were not available for all randomized participants at the later assessment stages. All 20 gabapentin participants completed detoxification, whereas three of 20 placebo participants were excluded: one at day 14 and two at day 17. Accordingly, the final reported SOWS assessment included 20 gabapentin participants but only 17 placebo participants.

   The missingness was both differential between groups and plausibly related to the true value of the outcome. The report states that placebo participants were excluded because of “non-compliance with the scheduled treatment programme and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter (OTC) drugs.” In an opioid-withdrawal study, non-compliance and self-medication are plausibly associated with more severe withdrawal symptoms. The authors themselves state that this “may be due to the greater severity of withdrawal symptoms.”

   No sensitivity analysis, imputation approach, or other analysis was reported to demonstrate that the result was robust to the missing SOWS data. Because missing SOWS outcomes occurred only in the placebo group and were plausibly related to withdrawal severity, the missing-data mechanism is likely informative and could materially distort the estimated between-group effect.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The outcome was measured using the Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS), a 16-item patient-rated scale. The report states that the measure “has been shown to be a valid and reliable indicator of the severity of the opiate withdrawal syndrome over a wide range of common symptoms.” Thus, the outcome measurement method appears appropriate for subjective opioid-withdrawal severity.

   The scale was administered at the same planned time points in both groups. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment procedures differed by intervention group.

   The SOWS is a participant-reported outcome, so the participant is the outcome assessor and knowledge of treatment could potentially influence reporting. However, participants received identical-appearing gabapentin or placebo capsules under double-blind conditions. There is no evidence that blinding was compromised or that participants’ SOWS responses were influenced by awareness of allocation. Therefore, the risk of differential outcome measurement is low.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The trial reports SOWS results at several assessment time points and presents more than one form of analysis, including repeated-measures analysis of variance adjusted for age and methadone dose, Mann–Whitney testing of total symptom scores, and analyses of individual SOWS symptoms. This creates opportunities for selection among time points, outcome summaries, and analytic approaches.

   There is no clearly reported, dated statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. Although trial-registration information can establish that the study was prospectively registered and may identify planned outcomes, the available information does not establish a sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan for the specific SOWS result assessed—such as the primary time point, whether total SOWS score or individual symptom scores were primary, the handling of post-randomization missing data, and the covariate-adjustment strategy.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported SOWS result was selected because of its direction, magnitude, or statistical significance; therefore, a high-risk judgement is not warranted. However, absence of a sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan means that selective reporting cannot be excluded.
