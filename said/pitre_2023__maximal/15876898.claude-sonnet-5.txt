# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment: Weight Outcome
**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)**

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise because although allocation concealment appears plausible, there is no information about whether the sequence itself was generated using a genuinely random method, and the description of concealment procedures lacks the detail needed for a confident "Low risk" judgement.

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: The article states only that "The randomization was carried out confidentially by the clinic administration, and arranged so that 25 test persons would be treated with the active drug and 24 with a placebo." No description of the actual method used to generate the sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random number tables) is provided → **No information** on signalling question 1.1.
- Allocation concealment: The phrase "carried out confidentially by the clinic administration" suggests some form of centralized/administrative control, which could imply concealment, but no explicit description of concealment mechanisms (e.g., sealed envelopes, pharmacy-controlled randomization) is given → best answered as **Probably yes**, but with residual uncertainty.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows the TG and PG groups were similar in age, height, initial body weight, weight gain, duration of olanzapine treatment, and current olanzapine dose. No baseline imbalance suggestive of a randomization problem is apparent (**No** to signalling question 1.3).
- Notably, the number of participants randomized to placebo differs between the text (n=24 intended) and Table 1 (n=18, after 6 dropouts), which is due to missing data rather than a randomization problem per se, but the initial reporting is imprecise.

Given uncertainty in sequence generation method combined with imprecise reporting of concealment procedures, "Some concerns" is the more appropriate default rather than "Low risk."

---

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias because the trial was double-blind (participants, clinicians blinded), reducing likelihood of trial-context-driven deviations, and the analysis approach appears consistent with a modified intention-to-treat approach based on randomized allocation.

**b. Rationale:**
- "Both subjects and clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment" — this directly addresses signalling questions 2.1 and 2.2 (**No** — participants and personnel unaware of assigned intervention).
- Because both groups were blinded, there is no indication that deviations from intended intervention (e.g., co-interventions, non-protocol treatments) arose specifically "because of the trial context." Concomitant medications (benzodiazepines, antidepressants) were held constant during the study for both groups: "the doses of olanzapine and the concomitant medications... remained constant."
- Analysis: Participants were analyzed according to their randomized group; the 6 dropouts (all placebo) were excluded due to missing outcome data (not because they were treated with an alternative intervention or excluded post-hoc based on treatment received). This is consistent with a modified ITT analysis (excluding those with missing outcome data), which is considered an acceptable analysis for the effect of assignment to intervention — the potential bias from these exclusions is addressed separately under the missing outcome data domain.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias due to substantial differential attrition between groups (0% in the topiramate group vs. 25% in the placebo group), with a plausible relationship between reason for dropout and the true outcome (weight change).

**b. Rationale:**
- Of the participants originally allocated (25 TG, 24 PG), only 43 were analyzed (25 TG, 18 PG) — "Six subjects who failed to appear for more than one evaluation (all from the placebo group) dropped out of the study, and their data were not further analyzed."
- This represents **0% loss in the topiramate group but ~25% loss in the placebo group** — a substantial and completely one-sided differential in missing outcome data (signalling question 3.1: **No**, data not available for all/nearly all participants).
- No evidence is provided that the result was not biased by missing data (no sensitivity analyses, no imputation methods reported) (signalling question 3.2: **No**).
- Missingness could very plausibly depend on the true value of the outcome: participants receiving placebo who experienced no weight loss (or continued weight gain) may have been more likely to disengage from the trial ("failed to appear") than those receiving topiramate, who may have felt discouraged from returning if not benefiting, whereas participants in the topiramate arm, presumably experiencing benefit (weight loss), had no dropouts at all (signalling question 3.3: **Yes/Probably yes**).
- Given the complete asymmetry in dropout (100% attributable to one arm) and the biologically plausible relationship between weight status and continued study participation, it is likely that missingness depended on the true value of the outcome (signalling question 3.4: **Yes/Probably yes**).

This pattern of missing data — entirely concentrated in the comparator arm, with a plausible outcome-related mechanism, and no correction via sensitivity analysis — meets the criteria for **High risk of bias** in this domain.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as weight is an objective outcome measured similarly in both groups by staff who, although likely aware of allocation only if unblinding occurred, would have limited ability to influence an objective measurement such as body weight.

**b. Rationale:**
- The method of measuring the outcome (weighing on a scale) is objective and appropriate — not subject to interpretation (signalling question 4.1: **No**, not inappropriate).
- Measurement method/timing (every 2 weeks) appears standardized and identical across both groups (signalling question 4.2: **No**, did not differ between groups): "Every 2 weeks, subjects were seen, weighed, and tested with SF-36, EWL-60-S, Bf-S…"
- Both participants and personnel were blinded ("Both subjects and clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment"), so it is unlikely outcome assessors were aware of allocation (signalling question 4.3: **No/Probably no**).
- Even if partial unblinding occurred (e.g., through observed side effects), body weight as an outcome is an objective, observer-reported measurement not involving judgement, and is therefore very unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment (signalling questions 4.4/4.5: **No/Probably no**).

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the weight outcome specifically, because weight was measured using a single, objective method (body weight in kg) and reported fully, without evidence of selection among multiple outcome definitions or multiple analytic approaches.

**b. Rationale:**
- Body weight was explicitly identified as a "primary outcome measure" ("Primary outcome measures were weight checks and self-reported changes on the scales of the SF-36…") and consistently reported at all time points (Figure 1) with a clear, single metric (kg), a single comparison method (Mann-Whitney U test), and complete numeric reporting including means, SD, difference, 95% CI, and P value.
- There is no evidence in the article of multiple competing definitions of the weight outcome (e.g., % weight change vs. absolute change vs. BMI) or multiple analytic approaches being tested, with only one selectively reported based on results (signalling questions 5.2 and 5.3: **No/Probably no**).
- No prespecified statistical analysis plan or trial protocol is cited in the article itself (signalling question 5.1: **No information**), and specific information from the trial registry document was not directly incorporated into this text; however, in the absence of evidence of selection among multiple outcome measures/analyses for this straightforward objective outcome, the default judgment remains **Low risk** per the RoB 2 algorithm (No information on prespecification + No/Probably no evidence of selective reporting from multiple measures/analyses → Low risk).

---

# Overall Risk of Bias for Weight Outcome: **High risk of bias**

**Overall Rationale:** Although randomization concerns are only "Some concerns," and deviations from intended intervention, outcome measurement, and selective reporting domains are judged as low risk, the domain of **missing outcome data is judged as High risk of bias** due to complete asymmetry in dropout (0% vs. 25%) concentrated entirely in the placebo arm, with a highly plausible relationship between the reason for dropout (failure to return for evaluation) and the true outcome (weight change) — a mechanism not addressed via sensitivity analysis or appropriate statistical correction. Per RoB 2 guidance, a "High" risk of bias judgement in any single domain is sufficient to classify the overall risk of bias for this outcome as **High**.
