# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Nambour Skin Cancer Prevention Trial - Outcome: Change in Photoaging

**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)**

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The allocation sequence was random and there is no strong evidence of a randomization failure, but there is insufficient information about allocation concealment, leading to an overall judgement of "Some concerns."

**b. Rationale:** 
- *Sequence generation:* "One of our study investigators, who had no knowledge of the participants, randomly assigned them by using a computer-generated randomized list" — this supports **Yes** to random sequence generation.
- *Allocation concealment:* No details are provided on whether the sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants (e.g., sequentially numbered containers, central allocation). This is **No information**.
- *Baseline imbalances:* Table 1 shows generally similar baseline characteristics across groups, though slightly more participants were randomized to β-carotene than placebo ("P = 0.06"). This is a minor imbalance compatible with chance and does not raise major concern for the sunscreen comparison itself. Photoaging-relevant baseline factors (phenotype, sun exposure, sunscreen use pre-trial) were balanced: "There were no differences in phenotype, sun exposure, or pretrial sunscreen use between the intervention and control groups at baseline."
- Because allocation concealment is not described (No information) and there is no imbalance suggestive of a problem, per the algorithm (NI on concealment + No/NI on imbalance) → **Some concerns**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Participants and personnel were not blinded (sunscreen use is inherently unblinded, and a placebo sunscreen was explicitly not used), but there is no strong evidence that this led to differential deviations affecting the outcome; adherence differed but was assessed for balance, and an appropriate ITT-consistent analysis was used.

**b. Rationale:**
- Blinding was not possible: "placebo sunscreen was considered unethical." Thus participants and personnel (2.1/2.2) were aware of allocation → **Yes**.
- Potential deviations: the discretionary sunscreen group could have started using more sunscreen due to awareness of the trial (a form of trial-context contamination), and adherence differed by design (77% of daily users applying ≥3-4 days/week vs 33% of discretionary users by 1996). However, this reflects the intended "discretionary vs daily" comparison rather than a deviation from protocol per se — the comparator group was defined as "discretionary use," so some sunscreen use in that arm is consistent with protocol, not a deviation.
- Reported sun exposure and other sun-protection behaviors were similar between groups: "Reported sun exposure was similar between the daily and discretionary sunscreen groups during the trial... Use of sun-protection measures other than sunscreen was also similar." This suggests no strong evidence of unbalanced non-protocol co-interventions driven by trial context.
- Analysis: "Data were analyzed according to treatment as randomly allocated," consistent with an ITT approach appropriate for assignment effect → **Yes** to appropriate analysis.
- Given awareness of allocation (unblinded) but no clear/strong evidence of unbalanced trial-context-driven deviations affecting outcome, and an appropriate ITT-consistent analysis, this maps to **Some concerns** rather than high risk, following the algorithm (Y/PY on awareness, NI on whether deviations arose from trial context affecting balance).

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** A substantial proportion of participants (about one-third) had only one photoaging grade rather than two, and while the trial authors performed sensitivity analyses (complete-case and multiple imputation) that supported robustness, missingness was associated with a prognostic factor (photoaging of the neck), raising some concern, though it does not appear related to treatment allocation.

**b. Rationale:**
- Data completeness: "886 participants (98% of 903)... had at least 1 good-quality skin replica. Of these, 604 contributed replicas in 1992 and 1996, 213 in 1992 only, and 69 in 1996 only." Thus about 282/886 (32%) lacked one of the two measurements — this is **not** "all or nearly all" (signalling Q3.1 = No).
- Evidence bearing on bias: "Compared with those with 2 replicas, participants who contributed only 1 (n=282) were more likely to have severe photoaging of the neck and 2 to 5 total sunburns" — this indicates missingness could be related to a prognostic factor associated with the true value of the outcome (photoaging severity), i.e., Q3.3 = Yes/Probably yes.
- However, "Having a missing microtopography grade in 1996 was not associated with baseline microtopography grade" and "treatment allocation was not associated with missing grades" — suggesting missingness is not related to intervention group, reducing likelihood of a differential bias between arms (relevant to Q3.4).
- The authors performed sensitivity analyses: complete-case analysis and multiple imputation, both showing consistent results: "Results were not materially different from models... and were consistent with results using only those participants with complete photoaging grades... and with results incorporating multiple imputations of missing data." This is evidence supportive of no important bias (relevant to Q3.2), but this evidence is not fully conclusive because the imputation approach's validity depends on assumptions not fully described.
- Given: incomplete data (~32% missing one wave), plausible relationship between missingness and true outcome value (severity of photoaging), but no evidence of a treatment-related differential missingness pattern and reassuring (though not definitive) sensitivity analyses — this corresponds to **Some concerns** rather than High risk.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome (photoaging via microtopography grading of skin replicas) was measured using an appropriate, validated method, applied identically across groups, by assessors blinded to treatment allocation.

**b. Rationale:**
- Appropriateness of method: "severity of dermal elastosis is predicted by standard grading of the microtopography of the skin surface, which provides a valid measure of skin photoaging up to age 70 years," with strong reliability: "Intra- and intergrader repeatability of assessors was high, with weighted κ statistics of 0.81 and 0.86, respectively." → Q4.1 = No (not inappropriate).
- Comparable measurement between groups: Silicone replicas were taken from the same site (back of left hand) using the same protocol for all participants ("Participants were asked to not use moisturizer or sunscreen the day that the replicas were taken"), so measurement did not differ between arms → Q4.2 = No.
- Blinding of assessors: "Experienced assessors who were unaware of treatment allocation graded replicas" → Q4.3 = No (not aware). Since assessors were blinded and the outcome grading is somewhat objective/standardized (with high inter-rater reliability), it is unlikely that assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention.
- Authors explicitly note: "it seemed to be nondifferential with respect to treatment groups, particularly for microtopography, because assessors were blinded to allocations."
- This satisfies the criteria for **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The trial's pre-specified design (registered trial, published protocol) matches the outcome and analysis reported; there is no indication that the reported result was selected from multiple outcome measures or analyses based on the findings.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial has a registration (ACTRN12610000086066) and a published detailed protocol/design paper (reference 25, Control Clin Trials 1994), allowing comparison of pre-specified analysis intentions.
- The outcome measure (change in microtopography grade, Beagley and Gibson scale) is clearly pre-specified as the primary outcome for photoaging: "The primary outcome was change in photoaging from 1992 to 1996... assessed to trained personnel obtained skin surface replicas... graded by using the Beagley and Gibson scale."
- The statistical analysis approach (GEE ordinal logistic regression) is described in detail in the Methods with clear justification, and the same primary analytic approach was used and reported for both interventions (sunscreen and β-carotene), including pre-specified covariates (sunburns, neck photoaging). Sensitivity analyses (complete case, multiple imputation) were reported transparently and did not appear to be selected based on favorable results — both were consistent with the primary analysis, and this consistency was reported regardless of direction.
- There is no evidence of selective outcome-measure switching, undisclosed multiple analyses, or omission of unfavorable results — subgroup analyses (by baseline severity) are reported transparently, including results unfavorable to β-carotene (e.g., "tended to experience more skin aging" in the more severe baseline group).
- Based on the trial registry information and consistency between described methods and reported results, this domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

**Rationale:** No domain was judged at high risk of bias, but "Some concerns" were identified in three domains (randomization process, deviations from intended intervention, and missing outcome data). Per RoB 2.0 guidance, a judgement of "Some concerns" in one or more domains (without any "High risk" domain) yields an overall judgement of **Some concerns**, though the accumulation of concerns across three domains warrants some caution in interpreting the reported treatment effect (relative odds 0.76 [95% CI, 0.59–0.98] for daily vs. discretionary sunscreen use).
