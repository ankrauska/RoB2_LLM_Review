1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The allocation sequence was random and was concealed. The report states that, after eligibility assessment, patients were “**randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio via a centralized Interactive Web Response System**.” Randomization was stratified by prior treatment intensity and remission status, and “**a block size of four patients was used within each stratum**.” Centralized web-based allocation is an adequate allocation-concealment mechanism.

   Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable between groups. There was an imbalance in age ≥70 years: 58/191 (30%) in the elacytarabine group versus 35/190 (18%) in the control group (*P* = .007). Older age is prognostic in relapsed/refractory AML, so this imbalance warrants consideration. However, it was one difference among many reported baseline characteristics, group sizes were virtually identical (191 versus 190), and the adjusted survival estimate was materially unchanged: “**Adjusted for age, the hazard ratio between the two arms was 0.98 (95% CI, 0.79 to 1.28)**,” compared with the unadjusted HR of 0.97. The imbalance is therefore not sufficiently implausible or consequential to indicate a problem with the randomization process.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   This was an open-label trial: “**The study was an open-label, randomized phase III study**.” Thus, participants and treating personnel were aware of the assigned intervention. However, for the requested estimand—the effect of assignment—open-label status alone does not establish bias. Bias would require deviations from intended treatment that arose because of the trial context, affected overall survival, and were unbalanced between groups.

   The comparator regimen was chosen before randomization: “**To avoid bias, investigators chose and recorded a control treatment for each patient before random assignment.**” This is an important safeguard against treatment selection after knowledge of allocation.

   Some deviations occurred: 6 control-arm patients received treatment other than their pre-specified investigator-choice regimen. In addition, 6 patients assigned to elacytarabine and 16 assigned to control were randomized but not treated. The report does not indicate that these deviations arose because of the trial context—for example, because treating clinicians knowingly delivered differential non-protocol care after randomization. There is also no evidence that cointerventions or treatment deviations likely affecting survival were systematically unbalanced in a manner attributable to the trial context.

   The primary overall-survival analysis used the full randomized population: “**The intention-to-treat population comprised 191 patients in the elacytarabine group and 190 in the control arm**.” The reported primary OS comparison therefore follows the principle of analysing patients according to their randomized assignment. Although a supplementary analysis censored patients at stem-cell transplantation, the principal result assessed here is the ITT analysis (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.77 to 1.22).

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Overall survival data were available for nearly all randomized participants. Of 381 randomized patients, the trial reported 302 deaths at the analysis cutoff, with 74 participants alive and administratively censored at that date. Only five patients were lost to follow-up or withdrawn: one in the elacytarabine group and four in the control group.

   The report specifies: “**For patients confirmed to be alive on the cutoff date, this date was used to censor for survival and response. For patients lost to follow-up before the cutoff date, censoring was done on the last date the patient was known to be alive.**” The very small number of losses (approximately 1.3% overall) is unlikely to have made an important difference to the hazard-ratio estimate, even if their subsequent survival differed from that of retained participants. Although losses were slightly more frequent in the control arm, the absolute difference was very small (1 versus 4 participants).

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Overall survival is an objective outcome, defined by death. The study used conventional time-to-event methods: “**OS was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method and was compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to calculate the HR and 95% CI.**”

   Although the trial was open label, knowledge of treatment assignment is not plausibly able to influence whether a participant dies. There is no indication that survival ascertainment used different procedures or thresholds across treatment arms. The censoring rules were also stated consistently for both groups. Thus, outcome measurement and ascertainment were appropriate and unlikely to have been influenced by knowledge of assigned treatment.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Overall survival was explicitly specified as the primary endpoint in the report: “**The primary end point was overall survival (OS)**.” The trial was registered (NCT01147939), and the publication reports a clear primary ITT survival result: median OS 3.5 versus 3.3 months; HR 0.97 (95% CI 0.77 to 1.22; *P* = .96). This consistency reduces concern that the outcome itself was selectively omitted.

   However, there is no sufficiently detailed, dated statistical analysis plan available in the supplied material demonstrating that the exact reported OS analysis—including the choice of log-rank/Cox analysis, handling of censoring, covariate adjustment, and treatment of post-randomization stem-cell transplantation—was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. More than one eligible OS analysis was reported, including the primary ITT analysis, an analysis censoring participants at stem-cell transplantation, and an age-adjusted analysis. The report does not provide evidence that these analyses were all pre-specified or that the reported primary numerical result was selected independently of the results.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported ITT OS result was selected because it was favourable to either intervention; nevertheless, in the absence of a pre-specified analysis plan with adequate detail, selective reporting cannot be fully excluded.
