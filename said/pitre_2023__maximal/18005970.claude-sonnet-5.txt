# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment

**Outcome:** Number of mistakes made in diagnosing problems (diagnostic hypotheses) identified by students in Phases 1 and 2
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to lack of information on allocation concealment and lack of sequence generation details, combined with evidence of baseline imbalances between groups that could plausibly affect the outcome.

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: The article states "students enrolled in the research project were consequently randomised with a randomisation table into two different groups." Use of a "randomisation table" suggests a random component (Probably Yes to random sequence).
- Allocation concealment: No information is provided about concealment procedures (envelopes, central allocation, etc.). This is "No information," which in a small-scale academic trial without a clinical trials infrastructure should not automatically be upgraded to "Probably Yes." Judged as No information/Probably No.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 2 shows statistically significant differences between the Trento and Udine populations in previous work experience duration (p<0.05), voluntary work experience (p<0.01), and clinical training experience type (p<0.01). While much of this reflects between-campus (not within-trial randomized group) differences, the paper does not clearly report whether baseline hypothesis-generation performance (Phase 0) differed between the randomized intervention/control groups *within* each campus. Phase 0 data are reported only by campus, not by randomized subgroup, making it impossible to fully verify balance from randomization at the subgroup level.
- Given random allocation was used but allocation concealment is not described and baseline comparability of randomized subgroups cannot be fully verified, this domain is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias due to unblinded participants/personnel, substantial deviations arising from trial context, and use of an inappropriate analysis excluding a sizeable subset of participants.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Awareness of intervention:** Students and tutors were clearly aware of which tutorial strategy they were receiving (laboratory vs. no laboratory; intensive vs. weekly vs. routine tutoring) — blinding is not feasible for this type of educational intervention. Signalling questions 2.1 and 2.2: "Yes."
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** The article explicitly reports that a major deviation occurred: "While involved in the study the Trento Faculty of Nursing, experienced 'cut-backs' with reduced numbers of tutors allocated to supervising students... Student exposure to intensive tutorials was reduced and routine tutorials by ward staff... were introduced." This is a significant, unplanned change to intervention delivery that arose because of contextual/logistical factors during the trial (i.e., "arose because of the trial context" in a broad sense — an external administrative change affecting fidelity of intervention delivery during the study period), and it plausibly affected the primary outcome (diagnostic accuracy), given the paper's own conclusion that intensity of tutoring drove accuracy. This deviation was **not balanced** between groups — it specifically affected the Trento control group.
- The authors themselves acknowledge this complexity: "tutorial strategies... cannot be standardised and depend on multiple factors which are difficult to control," and describe how new tutorial programs starting mid-study (Udine) could "lead to poorer results in the first phase because of the necessity to develop specific training for the teachers."
- **Appropriate analysis:** From the study population of 179 eligible/enrolled students, only 144 (80.4%) were included in the analysis; 35 students were excluded prior to randomization for eligibility reasons (language, prior work experience, attendance), which is largely acceptable as pre-randomization exclusion. However, within the analysis, there is no indication of an ITT analysis accounting for all randomized participants through Phase 2 — sample sizes shift across phases (e.g., Phase 1 n=82 in Trento but Phase 2 splits into 41+41=82, and Udine Phase 1 n=62 but Phase 2 splits into 23+39=62), suggesting reasonably complete accounting. However, given the substantial, unbalanced, non-protocol change in intervention delivery (routine tutoring substituted for intensive tutoring in the Trento control arm due to external cutbacks) that is likely to have affected the outcome and was not balanced across arms, this alone is sufficient for a **high risk of bias** judgment for this domain.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as outcome data appear to have been available for nearly all randomized/enrolled participants across phases.

**b. Rationale:**
- Table 3 reports outcome data (average number of diagnostic hypotheses, wrong diagnostic hypotheses) for all subgroups at each phase with consistent denominators matching enrolled numbers (Trento n=82 in Phase 1, split into 41+41 in Phase 2; Udine n=62 in Phase 1, split into 23+39 in Phase 2).
- No dropout or missing outcome data are reported for the primary outcome (number of diagnostic errors) among the 144 included participants across Phases 1 and 2.
- The flow diagram (Figure 1) confirms consistent participant numbers through all three phases without apparent attrition affecting the primary outcome.
- There is no indication that missingness, if any, depended on the true value of the outcome.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to lack of blinding of outcome assessors combined with a moderate degree of judgement involved in scoring "correct" vs. "incorrect" diagnostic hypotheses.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** The method used (standardized simulated case "Valentina," judged against a "standard" set of problems agreed upon by a panel of 15 professional nurses using Cohen's Kappa with cut-off K>0.80) appears appropriate and reasonably validated: "A panel of 15 professional nurses independently defined the standard problems of the case history used. A definition of the standard was agreed upon by professionals, using the K of Cohen and taking a cut-off as K>0.80." Signalling question 4.1: "No" (method not inappropriate).
- **Comparability across groups:** The same case and same standard were used for all groups/phases, so measurement did not differ between arms. Signalling question 4.2: "No."
- **Blinding of outcome assessors:** "Two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students and these were compared to the 'standard'." Additionally, "The data were processed blind; only after this had been done were the identities of the Schools (Trento and Udine) revealed." This suggests assessors were blinded to group/campus identity when scoring, which is a strength.
- However, this is an **observer-reported outcome involving some judgement** (classifying diagnostic hypotheses as correct/incorrect against a standard requires interpretation), and it is unclear whether assessors were also blinded to the specific phase/timing or intervention arm (e.g., whether hypotheses were labeled by group before evaluation, only school identity was noted as blinded). Since the outcome assessors were "blind" specifically regarding school identity but the protocol does not explicitly confirm blinding to intervention arm within each school (Trento intensive vs. routine; Udine weekly vs. routine), there remains some ambiguity about whether sub-group assignment was also concealed from assessors during scoring.
- Given documented blinding efforts but incomplete certainty regarding blinding to sub-group assignment, and given this outcome involves some assessor judgement, this domain is rated "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to absence of a publicly available pre-specified analysis plan or trial registration, though outcomes reported appear generally consistent with the stated objectives and hypotheses.

**b. Rationale:**
- There is no evidence of trial registration or a published/pre-registered statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The paper does not reference a protocol or trial registry entry, and the "doc_id" reference for trial registration was not identifiable/available for cross-checking in this assessment.
- The primary outcome (number of diagnostic errors) is reported in a manner generally consistent with the stated objective and hypothesis in the Methods section, and Table 4 presents both univariate and multivariate models fairly transparently, including variables that were not statistically significant.
- However, multiple potential outcomes/analyses were available (comprehensive analysis, intra-trial analysis, inter-trial analysis; average number of hypotheses vs. number of wrong hypotheses vs. dichotomized median cut-offs), and the paper appears to select specific cut-off points (median values) for dichotomizing the "number of errors" outcome post hoc: "The median values of error in Phases 1 and 2 were regarded as reference 'cut-off' points to estimate the differences." This determination of cut-offs based on observed data distribution (rather than a pre-specified threshold) raises some possibility of outcome/analysis selection, though it is transparently described.
- Given the lack of independently verifiable pre-specified plan and the post hoc dichotomization approach, but no clear evidence of selective suppression of unfavorable results, this domain is judged as "Some concerns."

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Although randomization and missing data domains raise only some concerns (or low risk), the domain "Deviations from intended intervention" is judged at **high risk of bias** due to a substantial, documented, unplanned, and unbalanced deviation in intervention delivery (the Trento control group's shift from intensive tutoring to routine tutoring because of institutional staffing cutbacks during the trial), which plausibly affected the primary outcome. Per RoB 2.0 guidance, a "High" risk of bias judgement in any single domain results in an overall "High risk of bias" judgement for this outcome, compounded by "Some concerns" in three other domains (randomization, measurement, and selective reporting), further lowering confidence in the result.
