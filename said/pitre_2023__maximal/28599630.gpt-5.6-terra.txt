1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study is described as randomized and placebo controlled, and randomization was reportedly conducted independently of outcome/data-collection personnel. However, the report does not adequately describe either the random sequence-generation method or allocation concealment.

   - The authors state: *“The randomization was performed by a researcher not involved in the data collection using the participant identification (subject ID) after stratification for EPDS score and previous history of depression.”*  
     This indicates stratification and separation of the randomizing researcher from data collectors, but it does not state how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random permuted blocks) or how allocation was concealed from personnel enrolling participants before assignment.
   - Thus, for RoB 2 signalling questions:
     - **Was the allocation sequence random?** No information.
     - **Was allocation concealed until enrolment/assignment?** No information. Independence of the researcher is reassuring but does not establish concealment.
   - Baseline characteristics were generally similar. There was an imbalance in skin colour (non-white: 59.4% fish-oil group versus 82.1% control; *P* = 0.055), and modest differences in education and parity, but these do not clearly demonstrate a failure of randomization, particularly in a small trial. The stratification variables—EPDS score and previous history of depression—were reasonably balanced at cohort baseline.
   - The registered record is an observational cohort registration and does not provide a prospectively documented randomized-trial allocation procedure.

   Therefore, lack of adequate reporting of random-sequence generation and allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was designed to blind participants, research personnel, and technicians, and used visually identical placebo capsules. There is no evidence that differential, trial-context-related deviations from intended intervention occurred and affected depressive symptoms.

   - The authors state: *“Participants and all research assistants and technicians responsible for running both the cohort study and the RCT were blinded to the study allocation.”*
   - The intervention and placebo were designed to maintain masking: *“The capsules were identical regarding color, shape, size and packing.”* Both were deodorized.
   - Some participants discontinued or did not receive supplementation, and adherence was incomplete. However, for the requested **effect of assignment**, non-adherence or discontinuation does not itself create bias unless it arose because of the trial context and was differentially related to allocation.
   - The reported compliance was similar in the two groups among women receiving intervention: 74.4% in the fish-oil group and 73.5% in the control group (*P* = 0.903). Among completers, reported compliance was also similar: 82.4% versus 79.1% (*P* = 0.559).
   - There is no indication that important non-protocol cointerventions were differentially administered because personnel knew allocation. Both groups also received standard folic acid and iron supplementation.
   - The article explicitly reports an intention-to-treat analysis: *“The ITT population included all women randomized, regardless of whether or not they dropped out or fully adhered to the intervention.”* This is, in principle, an appropriate analysis approach for estimating the effect of assignment. Concerns arising from missing outcome measurements are addressed separately under the missing-data domain.

   Accordingly, despite incomplete adherence and discontinuation, there is no evidence of deviations caused by the trial context that would bias the assignment effect. This domain is judged **low risk of bias**.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:** Outcome data for EPDS ≥11 at the postpartum endpoint were not available for a substantial proportion of randomized participants, and the handling of these missing data is inadequately described and potentially inappropriate.

   - Sixty women were randomized (32 fish oil; 28 placebo), but only 32 completed the trial. The report states: *“A total of 32 women completed the trial.”*
   - The per-protocol postpartum analysis includes only 15 women in the fish-oil group and 17 in the control group: thus, observed postpartum EPDS data appear to have been available for only 32/60 randomized women (approximately 53%).
   - The article reports several post-randomization losses and exclusions:
     - before receiving intervention, 6 fish-oil and 4 placebo participants were lost, and 1 fish-oil participant declined participation;
     - after trial commencement, 7 fish-oil and 5 placebo participants discontinued intervention;
     - one participant in each group was excluded;
     - one control participant had a stillbirth;
     - two fish-oil participants missed the postpartum appointment.
   - Although the article labels the analysis as ITT, Table 3 presents postpartum results using all randomized women as denominators (fish oil: 8/32; placebo: 7/28), despite the much smaller per-protocol samples with observed follow-up. The paper does not explain how EPDS ≥11 status was obtained or imputed for participants with no postpartum EPDS assessment.
   - No multiple-imputation procedure, inverse-probability weighting, sensitivity analysis, or plausible-missing-data analysis is described. Nor is there a clear explanation that all randomized women were successfully assessed at postpartum despite discontinuation, transfer of care, pregnancy loss, or missed visits.
   - Missingness could plausibly depend on the true outcome. Depression symptoms may affect attendance, continued engagement, willingness to return questionnaires, and study participation. In addition, some losses were related to adverse pregnancy events or medical complications, which could be associated with postpartum depressive symptoms.
   - Missing outcome data were substantial, potentially differential in amount and reason between groups, and no convincing analysis demonstrates that the result is robust to plausible outcomes among participants with missing data.

   Therefore, the risk of bias due to missing outcome data is **high**.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** EPDS ≥11 was measured using an established and previously validated self-report instrument, administered at comparable time points in both trial groups; participants, who were the outcome assessors for this self-reported outcome, were intended to be blinded.

   - The primary outcome was explicitly specified as: *“Prevalence of EPDS ≥11 was the primary outcome.”*
   - The EPDS was administered using the same approach in both groups at the scheduled study visits. The article reports that the scale was administered at baseline and follow-up time points, including 4–6 weeks postpartum.
   - The EPDS is a suitable instrument for screening depressive symptoms. The authors report validation information for the Portuguese version and explain that the postpartum threshold of ≥11 was used for screening moderate/severe postpartum depressive symptoms.
   - Measurement procedures do not appear to have differed by treatment group.
   - For this self-reported outcome, participants are the outcome assessors. The study reports that participants were blinded to allocation, supported by use of identical, deodorized fish-oil and soybean-oil capsules.
   - Although a self-reported depression scale could be influenced by treatment knowledge if unblinding occurred, there is no evidence that blinding was compromised or that outcome assessment was differentially influenced by allocation knowledge.

   Therefore, measurement of EPDS ≥11 is judged at **low risk of bias**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies EPDS ≥11 as the primary outcome and reports it at the planned measurement occasions, but there is no prospectively registered randomized-trial protocol or dated statistical analysis plan specifying the randomized comparison, the EPDS ≥11 outcome, time point, and analysis approach before unblinded data became available.

   - The publication states: *“Prevalence of EPDS ≥11 was the primary outcome.”*
   - It reports results for EPDS ≥11 at T0, T1, T2, and T3, and presents longitudinal logistic-regression analyses. Thus, the outcome is not obviously omitted from the publication.
   - However, the ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT01660165) is explicitly an **observational prospective cohort** study rather than the nested randomized trial. It lists:
     - study type: *“OBSERVATIONAL”*;
     - observational model: *“COHORT”*;
     - primary outcome: *“Major depressive episode”*;
     - no intervention arms, randomized comparison, EPDS ≥11 primary outcome, outcome time point, or statistical analysis plan.
   - The article itself states that the study was *“retrospectively registered on 24 May 2012.”* Retrospective registration cannot establish that the reported EPDS ≥11 analysis was specified before outcome data were available.
   - The report contains multiple possible analyses: prevalence of EPDS ≥11 at several times, continuous EPDS scores, changes from T0 and T1, ITT and per-protocol analyses, subgroup analyses among women with previous depression, and adjusted/unadjusted longitudinal models. In the absence of a prospectively finalized analysis plan, it is not possible to exclude selective choice among time points, analysis populations, covariate adjustments, or outcome metrics.
   - There is no direct evidence that the reported EPDS ≥11 result was selected because of its statistical significance or direction; therefore high risk is not justified. Nonetheless, the absence of prospectively specified randomized-trial outcomes and analyses gives **some concerns**.
