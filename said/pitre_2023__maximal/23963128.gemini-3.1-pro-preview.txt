Based on the RoB 2.0 guidance and the provided trial text, here is the risk of bias assessment for the effect of assignment to the intervention on patient care (measured by a team checklist score):

**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
*   **Judgement:** The trial mentions that residents were randomized, but it does not provide any details regarding the method of sequence generation or whether the allocation sequence was concealed.
*   **Rationale:** The article states, "Residents were randomized to one of three levels of supervision," but gives no further methodological details. Baseline characteristics (Table 1) appear relatively balanced, but without information on sequence generation or allocation concealment, a judgement of "some concerns" is appropriate.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
*   **Judgement:** Participants and personnel were aware of the intervention (blinding is impossible given the nature of direct vs. distant supervision), but there is no evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose because of the trial context. Furthermore, all randomized participants appear to have been analyzed in their assigned groups.
*   **Rationale:** The trial design physically prevented blinding ("level of supervision was defined by the physical proximity of the supervisor"). However, there were no reported non-protocol interventions that arose due to the trial context. Fifty-three residents were recruited and 53 were analyzed (18 direct, 16 immediately available, 19 distant), indicating an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis without inappropriate exclusions.

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
*   **Judgement:** Outcome data were available for all randomized participants. 
*   **Rationale:** The methods state that 53 residents were recruited. Tables 1, 2, and 3 consistently report data for exactly 53 residents divided into their three assigned groups (n=18, n=16, n=19). Therefore, there is no missing outcome data that could bias the results.

**4. measurement of the outcome: some concerns**
*   **Judgement:** Outcome assessors (video raters) were likely aware of the intervention received because the physical presence of the supervisor is visible on the video. While the checklist is largely objective, some subjective judgement is involved, which could potentially be influenced by this knowledge. 
*   **Rationale:** The outcome was measured via a team checklist score assessed by "two independent raters... [who] independently reviewed and rated all the videotaped performance." Because the intervention involves the physical proximity of the supervising fellow, the raters watching the videos would clearly see which intervention group the resident was in. Because assessing clinical actions involves some interpretation, lack of blinding of the outcome assessors raises some concerns.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
*   **Judgement:** There is no evidence that the reported results were selected from multiple analyses, but there is no mention of a pre-specified statistical analysis plan or published protocol available before unblinded data were analyzed.
*   **Rationale:** The study reports the outcomes exactly as defined in the methods section (team checklist scores, time to stabilization, definitive management). However, without a date-stamped trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan provided in the text, it cannot be definitively confirmed that the analyses were conducted exactly as planned prior to the data being available, warranting a judgement of "some concerns."
