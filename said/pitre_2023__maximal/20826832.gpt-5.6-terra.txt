**Outcome assessed:** compliance with mammogram screening at 6 months after randomization, principally the intention-to-treat result based on electronic clinical records (standard care 6.0% [10/167], low-intensity intervention 51.8% [86/166], high-intensity intervention 70.1% [117/167]).

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The allocation sequence was generated using a computerized random-number generator and stratified by age. Allocation occurred after women had consented and completed baseline assessment, through a coordinating centre, which provides reasonable assurance that allocation was concealed from recruitment personnel.

The report states:

- “*A complete data base with 500 eligible women interviewed at baseline was sent to the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center in the US (i.e. the coordination center) to proceed with a random allocation*.”
- “*A stratified randomization method blocking by age (50–59; 60–70) was used to allocate women to each group using a computerized random generator program.*”

Thus, the sequence had a random component and allocation appears to have been undertaken centrally after enrolment. Baseline characteristics were reported as balanced: “*No significant differences were observed between groups in the demographic characteristics of the participants.*” Group sizes (167, 166, and 167) were also consistent with the intended allocation ratio. There is no indication that baseline imbalances suggest a failure of randomization.

---

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

**b. Rationale:**  
Participants and interventionists could not be blinded, as expected for a behavioural/outreach intervention:

- “*Blinding participants to their intervention group was not possible in this study.*”
- “*Blinding was not possible for participants and interventionists; therefore, they knew the allocation arms.*”

However, lack of blinding alone does not imply bias for the effect of assignment. For this RoB 2 assessment, deviations are relevant only if they arose because of the trial context, affected mammography screening, and were unbalanced between groups.

The principal cointervention—opportunistic advice from primary-care providers—was delivered similarly across groups:

- “*Brief advice about having a mammogram was delivered by a primary care provider to 15% (73/500) of participants. This intervention had a very similar distribution across groups.*”

The intervention components were implemented as intended: all participants in the low- and high-intensity arms received the mailing intervention. In the high-intensity arm, telephone/home contact was intended only for women who had not yet arranged screening after the mailing; therefore, receipt by 50% was consistent with the intervention strategy rather than evidence of non-adherence or implementation failure:

- “*All women in the low intensity group received the mail intervention.*”
- “*In the high intensity group, women received the mail intervention and those who did not get a mammogram were contacted by telephone, and subsequently … received a home visit.*”

The outcome analysis used all women randomized, in their assigned groups, with baseline denominators:

- “*An intention to treat analysis was conducted using electronic clinical records.*”
- “*They were reviewed for all women and compliance was estimated using the baseline denominators of each group.*”

Although the authors acknowledge possible contamination, they consider that it would most likely increase screening in the control group and attenuate, rather than create, the between-group contrast. There is no evidence of important, trial-context deviations that were both outcome-relevant and unbalanced between groups. The analysis was appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**c. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**d. Rationale:**  
While 38 of 500 participants (7.6%) did not complete the final self-report survey, the prespecified main analysis based on electronic clinical records included all randomized women. The report explicitly states that electronic records were reviewed for every participant and that baseline group denominators were retained:

- “*An intention to treat analysis was conducted using electronic clinical records.*”
- “*They were reviewed for all women and compliance was estimated using the baseline denominators of each group.*”

Thus, outcome data for the electronic-record mammography-screening result were available for all 500 randomized participants.

Loss to follow-up for the final survey was relatively low and similar across groups:

- “*After 6 months, 92.4% (462/500) of the women completed the final survey.*”
- “*Lost to follow-up … were very similar between groups (i.e. 6.6% in the control group, 7.8% in low intensity group and 8.4% in the high intensity group).*”

The reasons for non-completion were predominantly refusal to complete the final survey or inability to recontact the participant, rather than documented outcome-related reasons:

- “*Main reasons associated with lost of follow-up were refusing to participate in the final survey (20/38 women) and not being able to contact the participant after several attempts (17/38).*”

For the electronic-record outcome assessed here, missing data were effectively absent. Therefore, missing outcome data are unlikely to have materially biased the estimated intervention effects.

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The main result was based on electronic documentation of mammograms, using records from the radiology service and electronic clinical charts. This is an appropriate and comparatively objective method for ascertaining whether a mammogram was completed:

- “*Mammography screening was also measured using electronic records.*”
- “*The first source was the electronic database containing the reports of all tests conducted during the study at the radiology unit affiliated with the university clinic. The second source was the electronic chart where the results of all tests are registered.*”

The same electronic-record sources and outcome definition were used across all intervention groups. Moreover, personnel at the clinic and radiology unit were blinded to allocation:

- “*Staff at the clinic and at the radiology unit where participants got their mammograms were blinded to the allocation group.*”

The data analysts were also blinded:

- “*The analysis of the data was conducted by investigators of the coordination center at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center. They were blinded to the group assignment of participants.*”

The intervention itself encouraged participants to schedule and undertake mammography, but it did not create different diagnostic criteria or different recording methods between arms. Since the outcome was an electronically recorded completed mammogram rather than a subjective participant-reported belief or symptom, knowledge of allocation was unlikely to influence outcome ascertainment materially.

### 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The publication identifies mammography-screening compliance as the main outcome and describes measurement at six months by both self-report and electronic records:

- “*The main outcome of the study was compliance with mammogram screening.*”
- “*We measured mammogram screening by self report at baseline and six months after randomization. Mammography screening was also measured using electronic records.*”

The publication also reports that the primary analysis was an intention-to-treat analysis using electronic records. This is a plausible, objective analysis choice and is consistent with the study’s stated methods:

- “*An intention to treat analysis was conducted using electronic clinical records.*”

However, the supplied materials do not include a dated statistical analysis plan or sufficiently detailed trial-registration information demonstrating that the specific six-month electronic-record result, outcome definition, and analytical approach were finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. There were multiple possible outcome measurements—self-reported screening and electronic-record screening—and potentially multiple analytic choices. Although there is no direct evidence that the reported electronic-record result was selected because of its magnitude, direction, or statistical significance, selective reporting cannot be confidently excluded.

Accordingly, this domain raises **some concerns**, rather than being judged low risk.
