**Scope of assessment.** This assessment concerns the randomized Phase 2 comparisons of tutorial strategies and the outcome *number of wrong diagnostic hypotheses/problems identified* after the clinical-learning intervention. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment** to the tutorial strategy at baseline (not adherence to the assigned strategy).

**Important limitation:** Phase 1 compared all Trento students (laboratory session) with all Udine students (no formal strategy). That is an inter-campus, non-randomized comparison; it cannot validly be assessed as a randomized comparison using the standard individually randomized RoB 2 tool. The judgements below therefore principally apply to the Phase 2 within-campus randomized comparisons: intensive clinical tutoring versus routine tutoring in Trento, and weekly tutorials versus routine tutoring in Udine.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The investigators state that students were randomized using a randomization table, which supports use of a random allocation sequence. However, the report does not describe how allocation was concealed from persons enrolling students or implementing allocation. A randomization table alone does not establish concealment, particularly in a small educational trial in which staff may have been able to know or predict assignments.

   The reported data do not suggest major baseline imbalance between randomized groups. The paper states that: “**The two groups were similar in principal demographic characteristics**,” and Table 2 notes “**no significant difference between Trial and Control groups in each group, p>0.05**” for the reported characteristics. However, this does not overcome the lack of information on allocation concealment.

   Relevant RoB 2 signalling-question assessments:
   - **Was the allocation sequence random?** Probably yes. Students “**were consequently randomised with a randomisation table into two different groups**.”
   - **Was allocation concealed until enrolment and assignment?** No information. No central allocation, opaque sequential envelopes, independent allocator, or other concealment procedure is reported.
   - **Did baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization?** Probably no. There is no clear evidence of an imbalance that is implausible by chance in the reported within-campus trial/control comparisons.

   For the Phase 1 inter-campus comparison, allocation to laboratory versus no laboratory was determined by faculty/campus rather than individual randomization; that comparison is at serious risk of confounding and should not be interpreted as randomized evidence.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to tutorial strategy.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Students, tutors, and clinical staff were necessarily aware of the allocated educational strategy. For example, intensive tutoring involved daily tutor-led debriefing, whereas routine tutoring had “**no systematic or formal tutorial strategies**.” Thus, lack of blinding is clear and expected for this educational intervention.

   However, for the assignment effect, awareness alone does not establish bias. The relevant concern is whether deviations from assigned tutorial strategy arose because of the trial context, affected the outcome, and were unbalanced between groups. The report provides no indication that such deviations occurred. Rather, it describes the strategies as the educational approaches delivered in each randomized group and states: “**During the study, none of the teaching practices changed in either of the degree courses.**”

   All randomized Phase 2 students appear to have been retained in their assigned analysis groups:
   - Trento: intensive clinical tutoring, *n* = 41; routine tutoring, *n* = 41.
   - Udine: weekly tutorials, *n* = 39; routine tutoring, *n* = 23.

   The analyses compare the randomized groups as allocated; there is no indication of an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis, or of post-randomization exclusion based on tutorial receipt. The use of the original assigned-group totals in Table 3 supports an analysis consistent with the effect of assignment.

   The study acknowledges possible general “trial effects,” but this is not evidence of specific, outcome-affecting deviations from the protocol caused by the trial context that were demonstrably unbalanced between randomized arms.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data for the diagnostic-hypothesis outcome appear available for all enrolled and randomized participants. The article reports that 144 students were eligible and included: 82 in Trento and 62 in Udine. The Phase 2 group totals equal these full samples:

   - Trento routine tutoring: 41; intensive tutoring: 41; total = 82.
   - Udine routine tutoring: 23; weekly tutorials: 39; total = 62.

   Table 3 presents wrong-diagnostic-hypothesis data for all these students. There is no reported loss to follow-up, withdrawal, missing diagnostic-case assessment, or exclusion from the outcome analysis. The report also describes the included group as stable: “**The group included was stable in the first six months**.”

   Therefore, data for this outcome were available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants. No imputation or missing-data method was required. On the information reported, missingness could not plausibly have made an important difference to the estimated randomized-group effects.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The outcome was the number of erroneous diagnostic hypotheses generated in response to a standardized simulated clinical case. This is a reasonable and directly relevant operational measure of diagnostic-reasoning accuracy for the study question.

   The same underlying case (“Valentina’s case”) was used across participants and phases, with demographic/contextual/family details modified. The authors explain that this was done “**to guarantee a homogenous level of complexity for all the students**.” Students were given the same time allowance: “**They had 20 min to make a hypothetical diagnosis**.”

   Importantly, outcome assessment was blinded:
   - “**Two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students and these were compared to the ‘standard’.**”
   - The standard diagnoses/problems were independently defined by “**a panel of 15 professional nurses**,” with agreement assessed using Cohen’s kappa and a stated threshold of **κ > 0.80**.

   Thus:
   - The method of measurement was not evidently inappropriate.
   - The basic assessment method and case complexity were intended to be comparable between groups.
   - Outcome assessors were blinded to group identity.
   - Blinded scoring makes it unlikely that classification of hypotheses as correct or mistaken was influenced by knowledge of the assigned tutorial strategy.

   Repeated use of a modified version of the same case may have introduced learning or testing effects, as the authors acknowledge: “**Using the same clinical scenario three times … could have helped students remember what they had learned previously.**” However, this applied to all students and does not indicate differential outcome measurement between randomized groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The publication identifies the main outcome prospectively within its Methods section: “**The main outcome was the calculation of the number of mistakes made in diagnosing problems (diagnostic hypotheses) identified by the students in Phases 1 and 2.**” It also reports the outcome for both phases and presents several analyses, including group-specific data in Table 3 and univariate/multivariable analyses in Table 4.

   Nevertheless, there is no accessible, dated statistical analysis plan or sufficiently detailed prospective registration information in the materials provided that confirms:
   - the exact outcome metric to be prioritized (mean number of mistakes, median cut-off, dichotomized number of mistakes, or odds ratio);
   - the intended phase/time point to be primary;
   - the planned within-campus versus inter-campus comparisons;
   - planned covariates for regression analyses; or
   - planned handling of the multiple tutorial-strategy comparisons.

   The analysis includes several potentially eligible ways to analyse this outcome: continuous number of errors, dichotomization at the phase-specific median, univariate odds ratios, adjusted odds ratios, intra-trial analyses, and inter-trial analyses. For example, the report states that “**median values of error in Phases 1 and 2 were regarded as reference ‘cut-off’ points**,” but this thresholding strategy is not demonstrated to have been pre-specified before outcome data were known.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable. However, in the absence of a pre-specified, finalized analysis plan or sufficiently detailed registration record, it is not possible to exclude selective choice among multiple outcome analyses or comparisons. This leads to **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.
