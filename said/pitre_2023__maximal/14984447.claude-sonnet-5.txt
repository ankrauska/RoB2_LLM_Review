# RoB 2.0 Assessment: SBGM Frequency and HbA1c

**Preliminary considerations:** This is an individually-randomized parallel-group trial. Effect of interest assessed here is the effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), per instructions.

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The trial reports random allocation, and baseline characteristics appear generally well-balanced across the two groups, suggesting no major problem with the randomization process. However, there is insufficient detail regarding allocation concealment, which prevents a "Low risk" judgement.

**b. Rationale:** The article states, "The patients... were randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion to two groups of 31 patients each," and that stratification was performed by income category. No information is provided about the method of sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number tables) or about how allocation concealment was achieved (e.g., sealed envelopes, central randomization). Table 1 shows close matching between groups on age, sex, education, income, diabetes type, duration, insulin dose/injections, HbA1c, and glucose, with no statistically significant differences reported, suggesting no problem with the randomization process (signalling question 1.3: No). However, because there is no information on sequence generation (question 1.1: No information) or allocation concealment (question 1.2: No information), per the RoB 2 algorithm (any response to 1.1, No information to 1.2, and No/PN/NI to 1.3 → "Some concerns"), the judgement is **Some concerns**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Participants and personnel were not blinded to intervention group, deviations (differential access to test strips and associated behaviors) arose directly because of the trial context, these deviations plausibly affected the outcome (HbA1c and SBGM frequency, the very outcomes under study), and were clearly unbalanced between groups by design.

**b. Rationale:** This was described as "a single-blinded control-matched longitudinal study" — the singular blinding refers to patient-blinding to allocation *category*, but by necessity, patients knew whether they were receiving free strips or having to purchase them: "one group (no cost, NC) was provided glucometer test strips free of charge. The other group (control, C) had to purchase strips as they found it necessary." Personnel (research nurse) distributing strips were also certainly aware of allocation.

This deviation (free vs. paid strips) is inherent to and defines the intervention itself, but critically, the *trial context* (necessity of purchasing strips, patient awareness of group assignment) is what drives the difference in testing behavior — this is the entire causal mechanism being tested. Because participants' knowledge of their allocation (having to pay or not) directly and necessarily influenced their behavior (frequency of SBGM), and this deviation is intrinsically unbalanced between groups (by design, one group faced a cost barrier and the other did not), the deviations were clearly related to trial context, affected the outcome, and were unbalanced between groups. Per the algorithm (Y/PY on awareness, Y/PY on deviations arising from trial context, affecting outcome, and unbalanced) → **High risk of bias**.

Additionally, regarding the analysis: substantial differential dropout occurred ("a dropout rate of 19% in the NC group and 48% in the C group"), and the manuscript states, "Efficacy data from the intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward, were analysed" — LOCF is generally considered an inappropriate/biased method for handling missing data in ITT-type analyses (see Domain 3), further compounding concerns about deviations impacting the reported result.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** A substantial and highly differential proportion of participants had missing outcome data by the end of the trial, the missingness is very likely related to the true value of the outcome (glycaemic control/motivation), and no adequate sensitivity analysis was performed to demonstrate the result was robust to this missingness.

**b. Rationale:** The article states: "At the end of the study, there were 25 patients remaining in the NC group and 16 patients in the C group, i.e. a dropout rate of 19% in the NC group and 48% in the C group." This is a large and highly asymmetric dropout rate between groups (question 3.1: No). The only correction applied was LOCF ("Efficacy data from the intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward, were analysed"), which the RoB 2 guidance explicitly states should not be assumed to correct bias due to missing data (question 3.2: No). 

Regarding whether missingness could relate to the true outcome value (question 3.3): the authors themselves speculate, "We cannot exclude the possibility that patients with intrinsically poorer glycaemic control became discouraged and therefore did less testing or dropped out of the study altogether," directly acknowledging that missingness could be related to the outcome. Given the magnitude of the differential dropout (48% vs 19%) associated with a cost-related behavioral intervention likely tied to motivation/adherence (and hence glycaemic control), it is likely that missingness depended on the true outcome value (question 3.4: Yes). This yields a **High risk of bias** judgement.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome (HbA1c) was measured using an objective, validated laboratory method identical in both groups, by personnel who were blinded to the values and did not provide any feedback influencing behavior, making differential measurement error unlikely.

**b. Rationale:** HbA1c was measured using a standardized method: "HbA1c was measured using the DCA 2000 Analyser." This is an observer-reported outcome not involving judgement (an automated laboratory assay), and the same method was applied identically to both groups (question 4.1: No; question 4.2: No). Furthermore, the study specifically implemented masking of results from the assessment team to prevent bias: "The research nurse did not provide any feedback to the patient concerning glucometer readings, random blood glucose readings or HbA1c determinations. The nurse logged the results by patient number and no analysis of the data occurred until all the patients had completed the study." While the treating physicians and possibly patients may have had some knowledge of HbA1c values via routine care, the objective, automated nature of the measurement method makes it very unlikely that this knowledge influenced the recorded/measured value itself. Given the outcome type (objective assay) and lack of differential measurement approach, this domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** There is no accessible trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan with a date-stamp prior to unblinding of outcome data, so it cannot be confirmed that HbA1c (and SBGM frequency) were reported and analysed exactly as pre-specified, though there is no strong evidence of selection from multiple outcome measures or analyses for this particular outcome.

**b. Rationale:** The associated trial registration record (doc_id: 4dab5cab-2b44-42f8-8559-2dd9073b18b5) does not appear to provide a prospectively registered, date-stamped analysis plan predating outcome data collection for this study (conducted 1998–2000, prior to widespread mandatory prospective registration). The published Methods section describes the analysis approach (General Linear Model with baseline covariates, ITT with LOCF, repeated measures ANOVA for completers) in a manner consistent with what is reported in Results, and HbA1c/SBGM frequency were clearly primary, prespecified outcomes central to the study's stated objectives, measured at fixed prespecified time points (every 2 months). There is no evidence of selective reporting from multiple alternative HbA1c measures, definitions, or analytic models (e.g., no evidence that alternative adjustment sets or cut-points were tried and only favourable ones reported). However, in the absence of a verifiable prospective protocol/SAP finalized before unblinded data were available, signalling question 5.1 must be answered "No information," leading to an overall judgement of **Some concerns** per the RoB2 algorithm.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias for this outcome (SBGM frequency and HbA1c): **High risk of bias**

Because at least one domain (Deviations from Intended Interventions, and independently Missing Outcome Data) is judged to be at **High risk of bias**, the overall risk of bias for this result is **High**, per RoB 2 guidance (a "High" judgement in any single domain drives the overall judgement to "High," regardless of other domains).
