# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Compliance with Mammogram Screening

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation (random?):** Yes. "by using a computerized random number generator program, 500 women were randomly selected" and "A stratified randomization method blocking by age (50–59; 60–70) was used to allocate women to each group using a computerized random generator program." This supports a "Yes" response to randomness of sequence generation.
- **Allocation concealment:** Unclear/No information. The randomization and allocation were performed at a separate coordination center ("A complete data base with 500 eligible women interviewed at baseline was sent to the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center in the US...to proceed with a random allocation"), which suggests some separation between enrollment and allocation, supporting adequate concealment. However, no explicit description of the concealment mechanism (e.g., sequentially numbered envelopes, central randomization protocol details) is given. This leans toward "Probably yes" given the remote, third-party generation of allocation, but details are sparse.
- **Baseline imbalance:** No concerning imbalance reported. "No significant differences were observed between groups in the demographic characteristics of the participants." This is compatible with successful randomization.

Given: allocation sequence random (Yes), concealment probably adequate but not fully detailed (Probably Yes/No information), and no baseline imbalance concerns (No) — this pattern maps to **"Some concerns"** per the algorithm (particularly if concealment is judged as "no information," combined with a "No" on imbalance, still gives "Some concerns" only if other answers are uncertain; here since sequence is clearly random, and imbalance is fine, but concealment detail is minimal, we lean toward Some concerns due to lack of explicit concealment description).

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- **Participant awareness:** Yes — "Blinding participants to their intervention group was not possible in this study." Participants clearly knew whether they received mailed materials, phone calls, or home visits versus standard care.
- **Personnel awareness:** Yes — the lay health educators delivering the high-intensity intervention were aware of the intervention content and delivered it directly; clinical staff at the clinic were blinded to allocation ("Staff at the clinic and at the radiology unit...were blinded"), but the interventionists delivering mail/phone/home visits were necessarily aware.
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** The authors explicitly acknowledge a likely deviation: "Lack of blinding in this study could have produced a certain level of cross contamination given that women, especially in the control arm, could have demanded more information than usual." This is a deviation from intended intervention (women in the control arm potentially receiving intervention-like information) that plausibly arose because of the trial context (participant awareness of study purposes, contact with primary care providers).
- **Likely affected the outcome:** Yes — this deviation (contamination in the control arm demanding more information) directly relates to the outcome (screening compliance), and the authors state that this would bias results, although they argue it would bias **toward the null** (favoring no difference) rather than exaggerating the effect. Note that ~14-15% of all groups (including control) received "opportunistic advice" — a component of the low/high intensity interventions — indicating this co-intervention was NOT confined to the intervention arms alone, contaminating the control arm.
- **Balance between groups:** The opportunistic advice component was reported as similarly distributed across groups (~14-15%), suggesting at least this specific co-intervention was balanced. However, the potential additional "demanding more information" contamination effect in the control group described by the authors was not measured or quantified, so its balance across groups is unknown.
- **Analysis:** An appropriate ITT analysis was used: "An intention to treat analysis was conducted using electronic clinical records...compliance was estimated using the baseline denominators of each group."

Because participants and personnel were aware of assignment, unblinding likely enabled deviations (contamination in the control group) that arose from trial context, that plausibly affected the outcome, and whose magnitude/balance across groups was not adequately measured or controlled — this leads to a judgement of **High risk of bias** for this domain, despite the appropriate ITT analysis (Part 1 High overrides Part 2 Low).

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- Outcome data (based on electronic records) were available for essentially all randomized participants for the primary electronic-record-based outcome; the manuscript states percentages calculated using "the baseline denominators of each group" for the ITT analysis based on **electronic clinical records**, which are available for all 500 randomized women (since these are administrative/registry records not dependent on participant follow-up/response).
- The self-report survey follow-up did have some loss to follow-up (~7.6% overall: "92.4% (462/500) of the women completed the final survey"), with similar loss across groups (6.6%, 7.8%, 8.4%), but this is not the primary electronic-record analysis, and this level of loss is small and balanced across arms.
- Reasons for loss to follow-up (refusal, inability to contact, one death) do not appear related to the true value of the mammography outcome itself in a way that would bias the result, and rates were comparable and low across all three groups.

Because the electronic-record ITT outcome data were available for all randomized participants (the primary analysis explicitly used baseline denominators for all 500 women), and self-reported survey attrition was small/balanced with non-informative reasons, this domain is judged as **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** The method (electronic radiology/clinical records of mammogram completion) is an objective, appropriate measure of the outcome, not questionable in validity.
- **Differential measurement between arms:** The measurement method itself (checking electronic records) was applied identically across groups, so ascertainment methods do not differ structurally between arms.
- **Outcome assessor awareness/blinding:** The paper states that "Staff at the clinic and at the radiology unit where participants got their mammograms were blinded to the allocation group" and that "The analysis of the data was conducted by investigators of the coordination center...blinded to the group assignment of participants." This supports low risk from the assessor/analyst standpoint.
- However, this is an observer-reported outcome not involving much subjective judgement (a mammogram either was performed and recorded, or not), which is typically lower risk regardless of blinding.
- The main source of "Some concerns" stems from potential differential ascertainment: because the intervention itself (mail, telephone, home visits) actively prompted and facilitated scheduling of mammograms, and administrative contact with participants differed substantially by arm, there is a slight theoretical concern that engagement/contact intensity could differentially affect whether visits/completions were captured equally well across arms — though this is a minor concern given the outcome is an objective, register-based binary event (having/not having a mammogram) unlikely to be substantially influenced by assessment bias.

Given the objective nature of the outcome and blinding of key personnel/analysts, but with residual minor concern regarding differential engagement/detection opportunities correlated with intervention intensity, this is judged as **Some concerns** rather than clearly low risk.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- The primary outcome (mammography screening compliance) was clearly the pre-specified primary outcome of the study, calculated based on a pre-planned sample size determination targeting "a point difference (δ) in the screening rate for each arm compared to the control arm."
- The registered trial protocol (referenced doc_id) can be used to confirm that mammography screening compliance was the primary/pre-specified outcome and analysis method (ITT via electronic records), and the results reported in the publication (baseline and 6-month screening rates via both self-report and electronic record, for all three groups) appear complete and consistent with what would be expected from the pre-specified design — there is no indication of selective reporting among multiple candidate outcome definitions, timepoints, or analytic approaches for this specific outcome.
- Both self-reported and electronically-recorded outcomes are reported transparently, with the electronic record data emphasized as the primary/ITT analysis, consistent with an a priori analysis plan rather than a post-hoc, results-driven selection.

Based on the consistency between the trial registration/pre-specified design and the reported results for this outcome, this domain is judged as **Low risk of bias**.
