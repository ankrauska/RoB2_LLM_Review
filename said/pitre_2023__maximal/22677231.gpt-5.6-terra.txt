1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study is described as randomized, but neither the method used to generate the allocation sequence nor allocation concealment is adequately reported. The article states that participants “were randomly allocated to the treatment or wait-list group in consecutive order” and that “no stratification was applied,” but does not state whether a computer-generated random sequence, random-number table, or another genuinely random method was used. It also gives no information on whether allocation was concealed from personnel enrolling participants before assignment.

   Baseline groups were numerically equal (105 per group), and Table 1 reports no statistically significant between-group differences in measured demographic, treatment, or baseline clinical characteristics. The article concludes that “Randomization was successful,” and the reported baseline BDI total means were similar (wait-list 30.02; Deprexis 28.81; *p* > .4). Thus, baseline data do not suggest a clear problem with the randomization process. However, absent information on concealment of allocation means that low risk of bias cannot be judged under RoB 2.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew whether they had immediate access to Deprexis or were assigned to the wait-list. The intervention was an unguided online program and the comparator was delayed access, so participant blinding was not feasible. Personnel communicating allocation were also likely aware of assignment.

   However, for the assignment effect, RoB 2 considers only deviations that arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, and were likely to affect the outcome. The report does not provide evidence of such deviations. Receiving other ongoing psychological or pharmacological care appears consistent with the naturalistic design: “being in current psychotherapeutic or pharmacological treatment did not lead to exclusion.” This is therefore not, by itself, evidence of a protocol deviation caused by the trial context.

   The halfway contact was standardized: “At half-time… all participants were individually asked via email if problems had occurred… The email did not contain any therapeutic information.” There is no indication that important non-protocol cointerventions or differential delivery failures arose because participants had been randomized to one group rather than the other.

   The primary analysis intended to estimate the assignment effect was an ITT analysis: “ITT analyses considered data from all subjects with available baseline data.” The investigators used multiple imputation for missing post-intervention scores and analysed participants according to assigned group. Although the paper also reports a per-protocol analysis, the ITT result is available and is the relevant result for the assignment effect. Non-adherence or variation in use of the web program does not itself bias the effect of assignment.

3. **Bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**

   c. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   d. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants. Of 210 randomized participants, 170 completed the post-intervention assessment: 90/105 (85.7%) in the wait-list group and 80/105 (78.1%) in the Deprexis group. Thus, post-intervention BDI data were missing for approximately 19% overall, with a higher proportion missing in the Deprexis group (21.9%) than in the wait-list group (14.3%).

   The authors attempted to address missing data using multiple imputation: “Multiple imputation was adopted to estimate post-scores for non-completers,” with 100 imputed datasets. However, the publication does not provide sufficient information about the imputation model, its predictors, or sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-not-at-random assumptions. Multiple imputation alone does not demonstrate that missing-data bias was removed.

   Missingness could plausibly depend on the true post-intervention depression score in this online depression trial—for example, participants with persistent symptoms, deteriorating symptoms, dissatisfaction, or reduced motivation may be less likely to complete an online follow-up. The report states that non-completers did not differ from completers on baseline psychopathological measures, apart from two quality-of-life subscales, but baseline comparisons cannot rule out dependence of missingness on unobserved post-intervention BDI scores. There are no reported reasons for missing BDI outcomes by trial arm and no robustness/sensitivity analysis. The differential amount of missing data is modest and not statistically significant, so the evidence is insufficient to conclude that missingness was likely dependent on the true outcome; nevertheless, it raises some concerns.

4. **Bias in measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The BDI total score is a validated and appropriate instrument for measuring depressive symptom severity, and the same online BDI questionnaire was administered to both groups at baseline and after eight weeks. Therefore, there is no indication that the measurement instrument itself was inappropriate or that its formal administration differed between arms.

   However, the BDI is a participant-reported outcome, so the participant is the outcome assessor. Participants were aware of whether they had immediate access to Deprexis or were assigned to delayed treatment. The article makes this explicit: participants in the immediate-treatment group received program access, whereas wait-list participants “received notice that they were allocated to the control group and would receive their access code upon completion of the post-survey eight weeks later.”

   Knowledge of assignment is likely to influence self-reported depressive symptoms in an open-label comparison of an active internet therapy with waiting-list control. Expectations, disappointment with allocation to delayed treatment, perceived benefit from receiving a novel intervention, and demand characteristics could all affect BDI responses. This concern is reinforced by the paper’s subjective-appraisal findings among treated completers, including that 64.8% reported that expectations were met or exceeded and 47.9% reported that “Depressive symptoms have decreased due to Deprexis.” Although the registry labels the study as single-masked with the “outcomes assessor” masked, that masking cannot plausibly apply to a self-reported BDI when participants knew their treatment status. Under RoB 2, this makes bias in measurement of this subjective participant-reported outcome likely.

5. **Bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was registered prospectively (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01401296; first posted July 25, 2011) and identified the BDI assessment of depressive-symptom severity as the primary outcome. The registry stated that depressive symptoms would improve more in Deprexis than in the wait-list condition over eight weeks. The article is consistent with this broad outcome specification, stating that “The primary outcome of the study was depression symptomatology as assessed with the BDI,” and reports the BDI total-score result at the eight-week post-intervention assessment.

   There is no clear evidence that the reported BDI total-score result was selected from multiple BDI instruments, time points, or subscales on the basis of the findings. The BDI total score was explicitly designated as primary, while BDI subscales were also reported separately.

   Nevertheless, the registry does not provide a sufficiently detailed, finalized pre-specified statistical analysis plan for the post-intervention BDI total score. In particular, it does not pre-specify the use of ANCOVA, the covariates, the multiple-imputation strategy, or whether final BDI values, change scores, per-protocol analyses, or ITT analyses would be prioritized. The article reports both per-protocol and ITT analyses and describes several analytic choices only in the publication. Because multiple eligible analyses of the BDI data were possible and no detailed pre-unblinding analysis plan is available, there are some concerns about selective reporting, but no direct evidence supports a high-risk judgement.
