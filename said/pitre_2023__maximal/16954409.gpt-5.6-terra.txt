**Outcome assessed:** Clinical pregnancy rate, defined as clinical pregnancy documented by transvaginal ultrasound 6 weeks after IUI. The assessment concerns the effect of assignment at baseline (intention-to-treat effect). The relevant comparison is rFSH (follitropin alfa) versus uFSH or hMG; the domain judgements apply to either comparison.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The sequence-generation method appears random: participants were “**prospectively randomized using computer-generated random numbers**.” Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable across all three groups: age, duration of infertility, BMI, and BMI categories were all reported as non-significantly different. Thus, there is no indication from the reported baseline data that randomization failed.

   However, allocation concealment is insufficiently described. The report states that women “**were allocated randomly … with sealed envelopes by specialist nurse**,” but does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, opened sequentially, and opened only after irrevocable enrolment. Under RoB 2, merely reporting “sealed envelopes” does not establish adequate concealment. Therefore, although random sequence generation and baseline balance are reassuring, the unclear allocation-concealment procedure leads to **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Participants and treating personnel were probably aware of the assigned gonadotrophin preparation: the trial does not describe blinding, placebo preparations, or masking. Nevertheless, for the effect of assignment to intervention, lack of blinding creates bias only if deviations from the assigned intervention occurred because of the trial context and were likely to affect clinical pregnancy.

   The protocolized aspects of treatment were described similarly across groups: stimulation began on day 2 or 3, monitoring used serum estradiol and transvaginal ultrasound, hCG timing was based on follicle size, and IUI was performed 36 hours after hCG. Dose adjustments were “**adjusted individually**,” and cycles with declining estradiol or more than three ≥16-mm follicles were cancelled; these appear to be planned protocol procedures rather than trial-context deviations.

   There is no reported evidence of differential crossover between gonadotrophin groups, non-protocol cointerventions, differential management caused by knowledge of allocation, or exclusions from the analysis due to non-adherence. Clinical-pregnancy results use the randomized denominators—21/81, 11/80, and 10/80—indicating analysis according to assigned group. Thus, despite apparent lack of blinding, there is no evidence of consequential trial-context deviations and the analysis is consistent with an intention-to-treat approach.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data appear available for all randomized participants. The study randomized 241 women: 81 to rFSH, 80 to uFSH, and 80 to hMG. The clinical-pregnancy table reports results using exactly these denominators: “**25.9 (21)**,” “**13.8 (11)**,” and “**12.5 (10)**,” respectively. The report also states that “**only one cycle per patient was performed**.”

   Although the text mentions cycle cancellation for inadequate or excessive response, the pregnancy rates appear to retain all randomized women in the denominators. A cancelled cycle cannot result in a clinical pregnancy from that IUI cycle, so this does not inherently represent missing clinical-pregnancy outcome data. No losses to follow-up, missing pregnancy ascertainment, or differential missingness by treatment group are reported. Therefore, missing data are unlikely to have materially affected the result.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Clinical pregnancy was defined using an appropriate and objective method: “**Clinical pregnancy was documented by TV-US, 6 weeks after the IUI**.” Transvaginal ultrasound confirmation of a clinical pregnancy is a suitable method for this outcome.

   The same outcome definition and assessment timing were specified for all groups. Although the report does not explicitly state that ultrasound assessors were blinded to the gonadotrophin assigned, ultrasound documentation of clinical pregnancy provides limited scope for subjective interpretation compared with participant-reported or clinician-judgement outcomes. Knowledge of treatment allocation is therefore unlikely to influence whether a pregnancy is visualized and documented. There is no indication that surveillance intensity or pregnancy ascertainment differed between groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Clinical pregnancy rate was explicitly identified as the primary outcome: “**The primary outcome measure was clinical pregnancy rate**,” and the report prespecified that clinical pregnancy would be documented by transvaginal ultrasound at 6 weeks after IUI. The publication reports the result for all three randomized groups, with event counts and denominators.

   However, no protocol or statistical analysis plan finalized before access to unblinded outcome data is available in the supplied material. The cited trial-registration document was not available for review here, so it cannot be used to verify whether the reported clinical-pregnancy definition, timing, analysis population, and analysis approach matched prospectively specified plans. While there is no direct evidence that the reported clinical-pregnancy result was selected from multiple outcome definitions, time points, or analyses on the basis of the findings, absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan means selective reporting cannot be excluded. Therefore, **some concerns** are warranted.
