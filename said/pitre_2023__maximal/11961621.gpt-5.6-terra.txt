1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The report describes both an adequately random allocation sequence and allocation concealment:
   - “*Procedure type was assigned by a computer-generated randomization list*.”
   - “*Concealment of randomization was done by telephone inquiry*.”

   A computer-generated list is an appropriate random sequence-generation method, and telephone allocation is consistent with centrally administered allocation concealment, provided that the allocation was disclosed only after enrolment.

   The baseline groups were broadly comparable for major reported characteristics, including age, gravidity, parity, body-mass index, and sonographically estimated uterine weight. Although some numerical differences were present (e.g., median estimated uterine weight, 283 g in LAVH versus 369 g in AH), there is no indication of an imbalance that is clearly incompatible with chance in this small trial (28 versus 20 participants), nor a substantial departure from the intended group sizes.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of assignment at baseline. Participants and care providers could not realistically be blinded to the assigned surgical approach. The authors explicitly acknowledge this limitation: “*the patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment*.”

   The study also reports an imbalance in surgical seniority:
   - LAVH: 20/28 (71.4%) operated on by an attending physician;
   - AH: 8/20 (40%) operated on by an attending physician;
   - this difference was statistically significant (“*p < 0.05*”).

   Surgeon experience could plausibly influence perioperative care, complications, pain management, and consequently length of stay. However, the publication does not establish that this imbalance was a non-protocol deviation arising because of the trial context, rather than reflecting the way the surgical procedures were delivered in routine practice. Nor does it provide enough information about other cointerventions, postoperative-management differences, or protocol adherence to determine whether relevant deviations were balanced between groups.

   On the other hand, the reported hospital-stay analysis appears to include all randomized participants: 28 in the LAVH group and 20 in the AH group are shown in Table 2, and there were “*no conversions from LAVH to AH*.” Thus, there is no clear evidence of an inappropriate as-treated or per-protocol analysis. Nevertheless, because the trial was unblinded and relevant treatment-delivery differences cannot be excluded, there are some concerns.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:** Length of hospital stay is recorded for all participants in Table 2, which reports results for the full randomized groups: LAVH (n = 28) and AH (n = 20). The article does not report exclusions, withdrawals, losses to follow-up, or missing hospital-stay data before discharge.

   Hospital stay is an in-hospital outcome that would ordinarily be available for nearly every randomized participant who underwent surgery. In addition, there were no LAVH-to-AH conversions. Therefore, the amount of missing data, if any, was sufficiently small or absent such that it is unlikely to have materially biased the comparison.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Length of hospital stay is an outcome that reflects a discharge decision. In this study, discharge was not determined using an objective, blinded, standardized discharge protocol. Instead, the authors state: “*Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital.*”

   Both participants and clinical staff were necessarily aware of the allocated procedure. Since the participants themselves made the decision that determined the outcome, awareness of receiving LAVH versus abdominal hysterectomy could plausibly affect perceived recovery, readiness for discharge, expectations, and willingness to remain hospitalized. The authors themselves acknowledge that expectations could affect recovery-related outcomes: “*the patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment; thus, their expectations may have influenced the duration of their recuperation or their satisfaction with the procedure.*”

   Although the nominal method of recording hospital stay was the same in both groups, the outcome was inherently susceptible to influence by knowledge of the surgical intervention. This is particularly important because the study’s primary outcome was hospital stay and because the intervention groups had different levels of postoperative pain. Thus, assessment/ascertainment of the outcome was likely influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies length of hospital stay as the primary outcome:
   - “*We defined the primary outcome measure as length of hospital stay.*”
   - The sample-size calculation was explicitly based on this outcome: “*We hoped to reduce the median hospital stay, for example, at least from 9 to 7 days. For this purpose, we calculated that we would need a sample size of 20 cases per group.*”

   The reported result for hospital stay is clearly presented in Table 2: median 6.5 days for LAVH and 10 days for AH, with a reported statistically significant difference.

   However, no prospectively dated protocol, statistical analysis plan, or usable trial-registration record is available in the supplied materials to confirm that the outcome definition, timing, analytical method, and comparison were finalized before outcome data were examined. In particular, it is unclear whether alternative analyses of hospital stay—such as mean versus median, adjusted versus unadjusted analyses, or analyses accounting for surgeon experience—were conducted and selectively unreported.

   There is no direct evidence that the hospital-stay result was selected from multiple measurements or analyses because it was favourable. Nonetheless, in the absence of a pre-specified, dated analysis plan or sufficient registration information, low risk of bias cannot be established.
