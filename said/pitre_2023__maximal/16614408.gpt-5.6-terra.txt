**Effect assessed:** assignment to fish-oil supplementation [3.6 g/day (n-3) fatty acids] versus placebo (olive-oil capsules), regardless of the assigned background diet (SAFA or MUFA), on change from baseline to 3 months in plasma **8-iso-PGF2α** and **15-keto-dihydro-PGF2α**.

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The article states that participants were “randomly assigned” first to the SAFA or MUFA diet and then underwent “a 2nd random assignment” to fish-oil or placebo capsules. However, it does not describe how either allocation sequence was generated or whether allocation was concealed until enrolment and assignment. A statement that a study was randomized, without details of a random component, is insufficient under RoB 2 to establish that sequence generation was random. Similarly, no central randomization, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes are described.

There is no strong indication that randomization failed. The authors state that: “The clinical characteristics of the participants at baseline did not differ among the groups,” and Table 1 shows broadly similar age, BMI, lipid concentrations, glucose, and blood pressure across the four groups. Group sizes were also close to the intended balanced allocation (42, 41, 40, and 39 participants). These findings do not suggest an important randomization problem, but they cannot compensate for the absence of information on sequence generation and concealment.

Therefore, the randomization process raises concerns because allocation concealment is not reported.

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
This judgement addresses the **effect of assignment** to fish oil versus placebo at baseline.

The trial used fish-oil capsules and placebo capsules containing olive oil: participants were assigned to “supplements of capsules containing fish-oil” or “placebo capsules with the same amount of olive oil.” However, the report does not explicitly state that participants, dietitians, carers, or personnel delivering the intervention were blinded. Use of the term “placebo” alone does not establish who was blinded or that the capsules were indistinguishable.

There is evidence that the intended nutritional intervention was received: “The increased intake of EPA and DHA during the study was reflected in the fatty acid composition of the serum phospholipids.” Participants were instructed not to alter lifestyle or physical activity and to avoid fish and fish products. No important protocol deviations, contamination, differential cointerventions, or differential adherence are reported.

The reported analysis appears compatible with estimating an assignment effect. Treatment effects were estimated using models including the diet assignment, fish-oil assignment, their interaction, treatment centre, age, sex, and baseline outcome value. The four randomized group sizes reported in the tables remain those initially assigned, and the report does not describe exclusion of participants because of non-adherence or treatment received.

Nevertheless, because blinding status and information on deviations arising specifically from the trial context are insufficiently reported, it cannot be confidently concluded that differential trial-context deviations did not occur. Thus, this domain has **some concerns**, rather than high risk, since no evidence indicates that such deviations occurred or affected these biochemical outcomes.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
For **15-keto-dihydro-PGF2α**, plasma samples from all centres were analysed, and the report presents results for the randomized groups without describing participant-level loss to follow-up or exclusions from this outcome analysis. The group sample sizes shown in Table 3 correspond to the original randomized group sizes. There is therefore no indication of important missing outcome data for this outcome.

For **8-iso-PGF2α**, samples from two centres—Denmark and Italy—could not be analysed: “Plasma samples from 2 centers (Denmark and Italy) were unfortunately inadequate to use for analysis of isoprostanes due to inappropriate sample handling and/or storage.” Thus, 8-iso-PGF2α was measured only at the Finland, Australia, and Sweden centres.

This missingness is substantial in amount, but its stated cause was sample handling/storage at entire centres rather than participant prognosis, treatment response, or the true value of the outcome. Such laboratory/sample-storage failure is not plausibly dependent on a participant’s true 8-iso-PGF2α value. The analysis also included treatment centre as a covariate. There is no evidence that missingness differed according to fish-oil versus placebo assignment within the centres contributing data, or that it depended on the true outcome value.

Accordingly, despite the reduced availability of 8-iso-PGF2α data, the available information supports low risk of bias from missing outcome data for these outcomes. This conclusion concerns internal validity; the absence of data from two centres may reduce precision and generalizability, but is not, on the stated information, likely to introduce missing-data bias.

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Both outcomes were measured in plasma using described and validated radioimmunoassays:

- “The plasma levels of 8-iso-prostaglandin F2a … were analyzed … by using a validated RIA developed by Basu.”
- “The levels of 15-keto-dihydro-PGF2a were analyzed in plasma from all centers using a validated RIA developed by Basu.”

The article reports assay detection limits and antibody cross-reactivity for both assays, supporting that the measurement methods were appropriate for the biochemical outcomes. Blood was collected under comparable conditions—“after an overnight fast at baseline and after 3 mo”—and plasma/serum samples were frozen until analysis.

There is no indication that outcome measurement procedures, thresholds, or timing differed systematically between fish-oil and placebo groups. Although the report does not explicitly state whether laboratory personnel were blinded to intervention assignment, these are objective laboratory-based biochemical assays with little or no scope for assessor judgement to influence the measured concentration. Knowledge of intervention assignment would therefore be unlikely to affect outcome measurement materially.

The inadequate handling/storage of samples at two centres affects availability of 8-iso-PGF2α data and is considered under missing outcome data, rather than indicating differential measurement bias between randomized groups.

### 5. Selective reporting: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The publication identifies changes in 8-iso-PGF2α and 15-keto-dihydro-PGF2α as the “main outcome variables” and describes the statistical model used to estimate treatment effects. It reports results for both biomarkers: fish-oil supplementation was associated with a statistically significant between-group difference in change in 8-iso-PGF2α (*P* = 0.015), whereas no effect was found for 15-keto-dihydro-PGF2α.

However, there is no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan available in the presented material that establishes, before availability of unblinded outcome data:

- the exact outcome measurements and time point to be analysed;
- the intended primary comparison in the factorial design;
- whether change scores, final values, or another analysis metric would be used;
- the covariates to be included;
- handling of the interaction between background diet and fish-oil assignment;
- handling of the substantial centre-level absence of 8-iso-PGF2α data.

The article reports several biochemical outcomes and uses adjusted models, with interaction terms removed when non-significant: “For outcome variables in which the interaction between the treatment factors was not significant … a limited model was used excluding that term.” This approach may be reasonable, but without a prospectively finalized analysis plan it is not possible to exclude selection among alternative eligible analyses.

There is no direct evidence that the reported results were selected because they were statistically significant or favourable to fish oil, and both a significant and a null lipid-peroxidation result were reported. Therefore, high risk of bias is not warranted. Nonetheless, the absence of verifiable prespecified analysis intentions for these specific outcomes leads to **some concerns** about selective reporting.
