**Assessment scope:** Individually randomized, three-arm parallel-group trial. This assessment concerns smoking abstinence measured objectively as a breath carbon monoxide (CO) sample **<3 ppm**, focusing on the comparison of the percentile versus fixed-criterion incentive schedules. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to the intervention at baseline**.

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The study describes a restricted random allocation process: “*Random assignment to one of the three groups was accomplished by randomly assigning 2 participants to the percentile group, 2 to the fixed criterion group and 1 to the random incentives group from each group of 5 participants completing intake.*” This indicates a random component in allocation, albeit with planned unequal allocation to the random-incentive group.

However, the report does not state how the allocation sequence was generated in practice (for example, computer-generated random numbers) or how allocation was concealed from staff enrolling participants. There is no indication of central allocation, an independent allocator, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. Thus, it is unclear whether recruiters could foresee upcoming allocations within each set of five participants.

Baseline characteristics were broadly similar, although the article reports one statistically significant imbalance: “*A higher proportion of percentile incentive participants achieved [Vocational/Technical School/Community College] level of education, relative to fixed criterion participants (χ² = 8.46, p < 0.01).*” This single difference among numerous baseline variables does not by itself clearly establish a failed randomization process, particularly because education is not necessarily a key prognostic factor for the objectively measured CO outcome. Nevertheless, the absence of information on allocation concealment means low risk of bias cannot be assigned.

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Participants and personnel were necessarily aware of the allocated incentive schedule. For example, immediately after randomization participants received descriptions of their assigned contingency:

- Percentile participants were told that “*the value of the breath CO changed to help them smoke less*.”
- Fixed-criterion participants were told they “*could only earn incentives for not smoking*.”
- Random-incentive participants were told that incentives were independent of smoking.

Thus, both participants and intervention personnel were aware of intervention assignment.

There is no clear evidence that non-protocol cointerventions or failures in delivery arose because of the trial context. The different payment contingencies were the intended interventions, rather than deviations from them.

However, for the abstinence-maintenance findings, the analysis was not conducted for all randomized participants in their allocated groups. Several principal analyses were restricted to participants who first achieved abstinence. For example:

- “*For those participants who were able to meet the breath CO criterion at least once, the number of visits before they produced a breath CO < 3 ppm differed between groups.*”
- “*For those participants that submitted at least one breath CO sample < 3 ppm, the total number of criterion breath CO samples submitted was not statistically different between the three groups.*”
- The comparison of sustained abstinence was also based on participants who had already produced at least one abstinent sample.

Achieving at least one CO <3 ppm sample is a post-randomization event that can be affected by the allocated intervention. Restricting analyses to this subgroup breaks the randomized comparison and can introduce selection bias. In particular, the reported finding that fixed-criterion participants maintained abstinence for longer is based on a selected subset of randomized participants rather than a full intention-to-treat comparison.

The study did report the proportion of all randomized participants providing at least one abstinent sample (23/36 versus 25/40 in the percentile and fixed-criterion groups), but its more substantive abstinence-maintenance analyses were conditional on post-randomization abstinence. Therefore, for the reported abstinence-maintenance result, the analysis is not appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment at baseline and could have a substantial effect on the result.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Outcome data were not available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants. Participants were expected to attend 60 weekday visits, but the mean numbers of submitted samples were:

- Percentile: 42/60 visits (SD 22);
- Fixed criterion: 40/60 visits (SD 24);
- Random incentives: 52/60 visits (SD 19).

Thus, on average, approximately one-third of planned CO measurements were missing in the percentile and fixed-criterion groups. The event-record figure also explicitly distinguishes missed visits from observed CO samples: “*White areas represent missed visits.*”

No imputation, sensitivity analysis, or other analysis was presented to show that the abstinence results were robust to the missing CO data. The analyses appear to treat attendance and observed CO samples separately rather than classifying missed visits as smoking or otherwise addressing the absent abstinence measurements.

For smoking-cessation outcomes, missing CO assessments could plausibly depend on the true smoking status: participants who resumed smoking, struggled to abstain, lost motivation, or disengaged from the incentive programme may have been less likely to attend. This is particularly plausible in this trial because incentives were conditional on attendance and, in the percentile and fixed-criterion groups, on CO performance. Missingness could therefore be related to both true abstinence and assigned schedule.

Although average attendance was similar between the percentile and fixed-criterion groups, the amount of missing outcome data was substantial and there is no evidence that it could not be related to smoking status. This creates a high risk that missing CO outcomes affected the estimated abstinence results.

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Smoking abstinence was assessed using an objective biochemical measure: breath CO <3 ppm. The study used the same equipment and measurement procedure for all groups:

> “*Vitalograph CO monitors … were used to take breath CO samples.*”

> “*The peak breath CO reading was taken as the participant’s breath CO level.*”

> “*We used an abstinence breath CO criterion of < 3 ppm based on our previous smoking cessation studies.*”

This is an appropriate method for the specified outcome—abstinence as verified by breath CO below the prespecified threshold. The threshold was applied identically across the intervention groups, and the outcome was generated by an instrument reading rather than by subjective participant or staff judgement.

Participants and staff knew the assigned incentive schedule, but such knowledge is unlikely to influence the monitor’s recorded CO value directly. While participants might alter smoking behaviour before a visit, that is part of the behavioural effect of the intervention and not differential measurement bias. Therefore, there is no important concern that outcome ascertainment differed between groups or was biased by assessor knowledge of allocation.

### 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The article reports several possible abstinence-related outcomes and analyses, including:

- the proportion with at least one CO <3 ppm sample;
- time to first CO <3 ppm;
- total number of CO <3 ppm samples among participants who achieved abstinence;
- duration of consecutive CO <3 ppm samples;
- probability of a second consecutive CO <3 ppm sample after the first;
- incentives earned and attendance.

The report also uses multiple analytical approaches, including Kruskal–Wallis tests, Mann–Whitney comparisons, chi-square tests, analyses adjusted for sex, analyses among all randomized participants, and analyses restricted to participants who achieved at least one abstinent sample.

The publication does not identify a clearly prespecified primary abstinence endpoint or a finalized statistical analysis plan. In particular, the selective emphasis on analyses among participants who first attained abstinence raises concern, because this subgroup was identified after randomization and multiple related abstinence metrics were available.

The trial-registration document was referenced in the request but its substantive registry information and any prespecified outcome definitions or analysis plan were not available in the supplied materials. Accordingly, it cannot be verified that the reported CO <3 ppm result, time point, analysis population, and statistical method were all specified before unblinded data were available.

There is insufficient evidence to conclude that a specific result was definitely selected because it was statistically significant; therefore, high risk is not assigned. However, in the absence of a verifiable prespecified analysis plan and given the multiple eligible abstinence outcomes and analyses, there are some concerns about selective reporting.
