**Outcome assessed:** performance, operationalized as the **sum score of correct answers to the 10 spinal-fracture questions** (with the 3Dp teaching module compared with the CT module; the considerations also apply to the 3D comparison). The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to the teaching module at baseline**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence was computer generated, and the reported baseline characteristics do not suggest a problem with randomization, but allocation concealment was not described.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   * The study states: “**Students were randomized into three groups using a computer program**.” This supports a random allocation sequence.  
   * However, there is no information on who generated the sequence, who enrolled students, or whether allocation was concealed until students had been enrolled and assigned. There is no description of central allocation, opaque sequential envelopes, or another concealment mechanism. Under RoB 2, absence of information on allocation concealment generally leads to **some concerns**, unless the other circumstances provide strong reassurance.  
   * Baseline data are broadly compatible with chance: group sizes were equal (40 per group), sex distribution was similar (22/18, 21/19, and 22/18 female/male in CT, 3D, and 3Dp groups), and age/school-age values were broadly similar across groups. The article reports “**no significant sex-related differences**” and “**no statistically significant difference**” in school age. There is no apparent major imbalance in a key prognostic factor.  
   * Thus, there is no indication that randomization itself was manipulated or unsuccessful, but concealment remains insufficiently reported.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   * Participants and teaching personnel were necessarily aware of the assigned teaching modality: CT images, interactive 3D images, or physical 3D-printed models. This is an unblinded educational intervention.  
   * However, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding alone does not establish bias. RoB 2 requires evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose because of the trial context, were likely to affect the outcome, and were unbalanced between groups. Such evidence is not reported.  
   * The planned teaching approaches appear to have been delivered as assigned: “**The TM of the first group … was based on the CT images**”; “**The TM of the second group … was based on the 3D images**”; and “**In the last group … each student received the two fracture models**.” A teacher provided “**a detailed interpretation of the fracture in each group**.”  
   * Differences inherent to the assigned interventions—for example, the ability to manipulate a printed model—are intended features of the intervention, not deviations from it.  
   * All 120 randomized students contributed outcome data: “**120 medical students … participated, and no one dropped out**.” The analysis included the students in their randomized groups using two-way ANOVA. There is no evidence of an as-treated analysis, crossover, or post-randomization exclusion. Therefore, the analysis is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   * The report explicitly states: “**a total of 120 medical students … participated, and no one dropped out**.”  
   * Each randomized group retained 40 students, and Table 3 presents performance scores and response times for all students: CT 22 female/18 male; 3D 21 female/19 male; and 3Dp 22 female/18 male.  
   * Therefore, outcome data for the performance outcome appear to be available for all randomized participants. There is no indication of differential missingness, imputation, or exclusion of participants with measured outcomes.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   * Performance was assessed using a common examination administered after the class: “**each student answered 10 questions regarding spinal fractures**” and the responses were “**assessed by an examination paper**.” The score was the number of correct answers from a defined set of multiple-choice or binary items.  
   * The same examination procedure appears to have been used in all three groups, so there is no evidence that measurement or ascertainment differed between intervention arms.  
   * Although students knew which teaching module they received, the outcome consisted of objective, prespecified answers (e.g., identification of vertebral structures, fracture type, and whether particular anatomical features were present). Knowledge of assignment should not affect whether an answer is scored correct, provided scoring followed the stated answer key.  
   * No subjective assessor judgement, participant self-rating, or treatment-provider decision was involved in the primary performance score. The article states that “**the answers to each question and the required time were recorded**,” and an invigilator recorded completion time.  
   * A possible limitation is that the examination “**contained many ‘3D images’**.” This may affect how well the test captures broader learning from the respective modalities, but it was applied uniformly and does not demonstrate differential outcome measurement or measurement bias under RoB 2.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   * The supplied report does not describe a prespecified statistical analysis plan finalized before outcome data were available. It does not state that the reported analysis of total correct answers was specified in advance.  
   * The study had multiple potentially eligible performance-related results: the total score from 10 separate anatomical questions, completion time, sex-stratified results, and four evaluative questionnaire outcomes. The article reports multiple analyses, including two-way ANOVA, Tukey comparisons, and Sidak sex comparisons.  
   * There is no direct evidence that the reported total-score result was selected because it was favourable, nor is there evidence that other eligible performance scales or analyses were withheld. Therefore, a high-risk judgement is not justified.  
   * However, in the absence of a dated, sufficiently detailed prespecified analysis plan—or accessible registration information specifying the performance measure, time point, and statistical analysis—it cannot be confirmed that the result was selected without reference to the findings. This leads to **some concerns** under RoB 2.
