# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Intentions to Consume RDIFV

## Preliminary Considerations
- **Design:** Individually-randomized parallel-group trial
- **Experimental:** Persuasive communication + implementation intentions leaflet
- **Comparator:** Control task (questions about the questionnaire)
- **Outcome:** Intentions to consume RDIFV (post-intervention, immediate)
- **Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (as specified)

---

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The trial used a true random number generator to determine allocation order, and the process appears to have been implemented in a way that limited opportunity for foreknowledge (booklets pre-ordered into a single pile before distribution). Baseline cognition and behavioural measures showed no significant differences between groups, consistent with successful randomization.

**b. Rationale:** "To ensure randomization, intervention and control booklets were placed in a single pile in an order determined by a sequence of random integers generated by a true random number generator (Haahr, 2004)." This satisfies the random sequence generation criterion. Regarding concealment, because booklets were pre-assembled in a fixed, randomly-determined order and then distributed in bulk to a lecture theatre/lab class, there is limited opportunity for selective enrolment based on foreknowledge of allocation—though this is not explicitly confirmed with formal concealment procedures (e.g., sequentially numbered/sealed materials), the mass, simultaneous distribution method makes manipulation implausible. Baseline balance was confirmed: "MANOVA performed on pre-intervention behaviour...and cognition measures...revealed no significant differences between the control and experimental groups," and no differences in age or gender by condition. There is no evidence of a problem with the randomization process.

---

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Participants were necessarily aware of which booklet/task they received (not blinded, as this is a leaflet-type intervention), but there is no evidence that trial-context-driven deviations (e.g., contamination, non-protocol interventions) occurred that would bias the intention outcome, and appropriate analysis (ANCOVA controlling for pre-intervention scores, including all randomized participants with available data) was used.

**b. Rationale:** Participants and personnel could not be blinded to allocation given the nature of the intervention (a leaflet with persuasive messages and planning tasks vs. a neutral questionnaire task): "The control task consisted of questions about the questionnaire itself…The task took approximately the same time to complete as the intervention." Awareness of assignment is thus certain (Y to signalling question 2.1/2.2). However, for the *intention* outcome, which was measured **immediately** after the intervention/control task, within the same testing session, there is minimal opportunity for trial-context-driven deviations (e.g., contamination between groups) to occur before this measurement. Authors explicitly minimized contamination risk for the later (1-week) behavioural outcome ("participants were explicitly requested not to discuss the contents of their booklet with other participants over the following week"), but this concern is essentially irrelevant for the immediate intention measure, which was recorded before participants left the room. There is no indication that non-protocol interventions or implementation failures affected the immediate intention outcome. The analysis of intention appears to include all randomized participants who completed the immediate post-test (an appropriate, intention-to-treat-consistent approach for this within-session outcome), with ANCOVA controlling for pre-intervention scores: "Univariate ANCOVAs were used to test the difference between post-intervention means between conditions, controlling for pre-intervention scores... the post-intervention difference for intention was significant, F(1,215)=5.33, p<.05." This n (215) closely matches the full randomized sample (218), indicating minimal exclusion at this stage.

---

### 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** For the post-intervention intention outcome, data were available for nearly all randomized participants.

**b. Rationale:** The intention outcome analyzed here is the **immediate post-intervention** measure (collected in the same session as randomization and intervention/control task), not the 1-week follow-up. The degrees of freedom reported for the ANCOVA (F(1,215)) indicate that approximately 217-218 of the 218 randomized participants contributed data to this analysis, i.e., "Univariate ANOVAs showed that there were no significant pre-intervention differences on intention, self-efficacy, attitudes, norms, or anticipated regret" and post-test ANCOVA used nearly the full sample. The substantial attrition described elsewhere ("At follow-up, 146 people responded...with an overall attrition rate of 33%") pertains to the 1-week behavioural follow-up, not the immediate post-intervention cognitive measures, which were collected before participants left the testing session. Therefore, missingness for this particular outcome (intention) is minimal and unlikely to bias the result.

---

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome (intention) is a participant-reported outcome, participants were not blinded, and knowledge of intervention assignment could plausibly influence self-reported intention scores (e.g., demand characteristics, participants recognizing the persuasive intent of the leaflet). However, there is no strong evidence that this specific outcome was flagrantly manipulated to be favorable, and the measure itself (a validated multi-item Likert scale with strong reported reliability, α = .90/.93) was appropriate.

**b. Rationale:** Per RoB 2 guidance, "for participant-reported outcomes, the assessment of outcome is potentially influenced by knowledge of intervention received, leading to a judgement of at least 'Some concerns.'" Here, the outcome assessor is the participant, and participants in the intervention arm clearly knew they had received persuasive messages explicitly urging them to "make a firm decision now that you will eat the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables." This creates a plausible pathway by which participants' self-reported intention could be influenced by demand characteristics/social desirability rather than a genuine attitude shift, especially because the outcome was measured immediately after exposure to the persuasive content, using the same instrument pre- and post-intervention. The instructions attempted to mitigate this ("Respond as you are feeling now... Please do not look back at your previous answers"), and the method of measurement (validated TPB-based scale) did not differ between groups. Given the immediacy of assessment following overt persuasive messaging, we judge this as "Some concerns" rather than "High risk," as there is no definitive evidence (e.g., extreme/implausible effect sizes) that assessment was strongly and deliberately distorted, but the potential for influence is clearly present and cannot be ruled out.

---

### 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** There is no clear evidence that the reported intention result was selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses on the basis of the results.

**b. Rationale:** The trial registration document (doc_id: 4dab5cab) and the article together indicate that intention was one of several pre-specified TPB-based cognitive outcomes (attitude, norms, PBC, self-efficacy, intention, anticipated regret), each measured using a single fixed multi-item scale both pre- and post-intervention, with consistent Cronbach's alphas reported for both time points. The analysis for intention is straightforward — a single, pre-post ANCOVA — with no evidence of multiple alternative operationalizations or analytic models being tried and selectively reported for this specific outcome. All the main cognitive outcomes (including intention) are reported comprehensively in Table 4, including both significant and non-significant results (e.g., self-efficacy showed no significant intervention effect and this null result was reported, arguing against selective reporting: "The intervention appears to have promoted intentions to eat the RDIFV and anticipated regret...but did not have significant effects on self-efficacy"). This transparent reporting of a non-significant finding alongside significant ones for closely related constructs, measured with the same methodology, supports a low risk of selective outcome reporting for the intention result.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias Judgement (for Intentions to Consume RDIFV): **Some concerns**

**Rationale:** All domains were judged as "Low risk of bias" except for "Measurement of the outcome," which was judged "Some concerns" due to the unblinded, participant-reported nature of the intention outcome, measured immediately following explicit persuasive messaging, creating plausible risk of the outcome being influenced by participants' awareness of their assigned condition. Per RoB 2 guidance, a judgement of "Some concerns" in any single domain, with no domain at "High risk," yields an overall judgement of "Some concerns" for this outcome.
