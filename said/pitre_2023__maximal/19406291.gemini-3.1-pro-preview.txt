**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** The allocation sequence was random, adequately concealed, and baseline imbalances were minor and compatible with chance.
**b. Rationale:** The trial publication states that "random assignment to treatment conditions (sertraline or placebo) was balanced by using a block design (blocks of 8) with computer-generated random number sequences in individual presealed envelopes." Furthermore, "The pharmacy implemented masking and random assignment of participants... Thus, both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received." Although there was a baseline difference in income levels between the groups, it is compatible with chance in a small sample size (n=52) and does not suggest a fundamental flaw in the randomization process. 

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** Participants and personnel were blinded, and an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis approach was used.
**b. Rationale:** The trial was a "controlled, double-blind randomized controlled trial." Medications and placebos had "identical markings," and "both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received." Regarding the analysis, the authors conducted an intent-to-treat analysis utilizing a last-observation-carried-forward (LOCF) approach to include all randomized participants who completed at least some of the follow-up, which is considered appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to the intervention.

**3. missing outcome data: some concerns**
**a. Judgement:** Approximately 20% of the sample dropped out, and there is evidence that missingness may be related to the true value of the outcome.
**b. Rationale:** Out of 52 randomized participants, 11 (21%) dropped out. The authors note that "the intent-to-treat analyses found differences between the last assessment HAM-D score for those who dropped out of treatment as compared with the assessment HAM-D score of those participants who remained in treatment... with the dropouts tending to have higher HAM-D scores." This suggests that missingness in the outcome could depend on its true value (i.e., those with more severe depression were more likely to drop out). While the missingness rates were relatively balanced between groups (5 in sertraline, 6 in placebo), the reliance on single imputation (LOCF) does not fully protect against bias when data are missing not at random. 

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** The outcome was measured using an appropriate, standard tool by assessors who were blinded to the intervention assignment.
**b. Rationale:** The HAM-D is a widely accepted and validated clinician-rated tool for measuring depression severity. The outcome assessors were blinded to the assigned intervention: "The pharmacy implemented masking... both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received." Therefore, the assessment of the outcome is unlikely to have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention.

**5. selective reporting: low risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** The reported results correspond to the pre-specified primary outcome measure and analysis plan documented in the trial registry.
**b. Rationale:** The trial was prospectively registered (NCT00233103). The clinical trial protocol specifies the primary outcome as "Depression at End of Treatment" assessed via the HAM-D score at 10 weeks. The published article reports this exact outcome measure at the pre-specified time point, and the data analysis matches the intended comparisons, leaving little concern for selection of the reported result based on statistical significance.
