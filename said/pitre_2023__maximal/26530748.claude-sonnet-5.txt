# Risk of Bias Assessment: PORTEC-2 Trial — EORTC QLQ-C30 Global Health Outcome

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (EBRT vs. VBT)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**a. Rationale for judgement:** PORTEC-2 was a multicenter randomized trial (427 patients) comparing EBRT and VBT. Although this specific HRQL publication does not repeat full randomization methodological detail (referring instead to the primary trial reports, e.g., Nout et al. 2010, 2009), PORTEC trials are well-documented, cooperative-group studies that used centrally administered, stratified randomization in the original protocol. There is no indication in this report of problems with allocation concealment.

**b. Rationale/evidence:** Table 1 in the article shows that the HRQL responder population (7-year cohort) was "slightly younger and had fewer comorbidities" compared with the full PORTEC-2 cohort, but no baseline imbalances between the EBRT and VBT randomized arms themselves are reported that would suggest a randomization failure ("no other significant differences were found" between arms). The observed age/comorbidity difference relates to selective response over long-term follow-up (addressed under Domain 3), not to a flawed randomization process. No evidence of a substantial excess of baseline imbalances between the randomized groups is presented.

---

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention)
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**a. Rationale for judgement:** This was an open-label trial (EBRT vs. VBT); participants and treating personnel were necessarily aware of the assigned intervention, since the treatments are physically and logistically distinct. However, there is no evidence that deviations from intended treatment allocation (e.g., non-protocol interventions, crossovers) occurred because of the trial context that would bias the assignment effect. The HRQL outcome was analyzed according to randomized allocation (intention-to-treat framework) using linear mixed models comparing groups "as randomized."

**b. Rationale/evidence:** The article states "Patients and Methods…randomly allocated to EBRT or VBT" and reports outcomes by assigned arm throughout (e.g., "EBRT patients reported more bowel symptoms…without impact on overall quality of life"). No mention is made of crossover between EBRT and VBT arms or of non-protocol radiotherapy interventions. The exclusion of patients with disease recurrence/second cancers from the longitudinal symptom analysis was done for both arms symmetrically and does not reflect an intervention-related deviation. Analyses appear consistent with an ITT-type approach for the randomized comparison.

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data
**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**a. Rationale for judgement:** A substantial proportion of originally randomized/eligible patients did not contribute Global Health data at the 7- and 10-year assessments, and there is evidence that the missingness/selection into the long-term analysis was related to participant characteristics (age, comorbidity, survival status) that plausibly correlate with the true value of the HRQL outcome.

**b. Rationale/evidence:** 
- Only patients "alive and disease-free" were eligible for follow-up assessment — a substantial post-randomization selection.
- "Response rate at 7 years was 205 of 265 (77%)" and at 10 years "80 (67%)" — meaningful proportions of missing data.
- The article explicitly acknowledges a systematic difference between responders and the full trial cohort: "Responders at 7 years were slightly younger and had fewer comorbidities compared with the whole PORTEC-2 cohort" and speculates that "older patients with more comorbidities were at higher risk to die of intercurrent disease" or "were not able to respond owing to other reasons, such as vision problems or cognitive disorders."
- No sensitivity analysis was performed to demonstrate that the Global Health results were robust to these patterns of missingness.
- Because age, comorbidity, and survival status are plausible correlates of global health/quality-of-life scores, this raises real concern that missingness depended on the (unmeasured) true outcome value, particularly for the long-term (7- to 10-year) timepoints.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome
**Judgement: Some concerns**

**a. Rationale for judgement:** The EORTC QLQ-C30 is an appropriate, validated instrument for measuring global health-related quality of life, and it was used identically in both arms (same instrument, same administration schedule), so there is no differential measurement method between groups. However, this is a participant-reported outcome, and because the trial was unblinded (open-label EBRT vs. VBT), the outcome assessor (the patient) was aware of which treatment they received. There is no clear evidence that self-report of Global Health was actually influenced by treatment knowledge, but the possibility cannot be excluded, particularly given that other subjective symptom scores (e.g., "sex to be enjoyable") differed between arms in ways not obviously explained by objective symptom differences.

**b. Rationale/evidence:** "Cancer-specific general HRQL was measured with the EORTC…QLQ-C30" — an appropriate, standard instrument, applied identically to both groups. The trial is open label ("PORTEC-2…randomised trial" comparing two distinct modalities of radiotherapy), so blinding of participants (who are the outcome assessors for this patient-reported measure) was not possible. Because global health scores did not significantly differ between arms ("Scores on the QLQ-C30 functioning and global health scales did not significantly differ between the 2 treatment groups"), there is no strong signal of differential reporting bias, but the inherent lack of blinding for a subjective, self-reported outcome still generates residual concern.

---

## 5. Selective reporting
**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**a. Rationale for judgement:** The EORTC QLQ-C30 Global Health scale is a standard, prespecified component of the EORTC core questionnaire used consistently across all PORTEC-2 HRQL analyses (baseline, 5-year, and this 7-/10-year report), analyzed according to established EORTC Quality of Life Group guidelines with a prespecified statistical approach (linear mixed models, prespecified significance threshold of P<.01, and prespecified clinically relevant change guidelines). There is no indication that Global Health results were selected from multiple alternative analyses or outcome definitions based on the results obtained.

**b. Rationale/evidence:** "Analysis of HRQL was done according to EORTC Quality of Life Group guidelines" and "a two-sided P value <.01 was considered statistically significant" — indicating a consistent, prespecified analytic approach applied uniformly across timepoints and outcomes, rather than a plan modified post hoc. The Global Health scale results are reported consistently with the same methodology used in the prior 5-year publication (reference 7), and the trial registration information indicates HRQL/QLQ-C30 was a predefined secondary outcome of the PORTEC-2 trial rather than an outcome introduced or altered opportunistically at this later analysis. No evidence of selective analysis (e.g., selective time points, alternative scoring algorithms, or selective covariate adjustment) is apparent for this specific outcome.

---

# Overall Risk-of-Bias Judgement: **High risk of bias**

**Summary rationale:** The trial is judged to be at high risk of bias overall because of a high risk of bias due to missing outcome data (Domain 3) — driven by substantial loss to long-term follow-up combined with plausible informative missingness related to age, comorbidity, and survival — which is sufficient on its own to generate an overall "High risk" judgement for the EORTC QLQ-C30 Global Health outcome, per RoB 2 rules (a "High" judgement in any single domain results in an overall "High" risk of bias, irrespective of the largely low/some-concerns judgements in the other four domains).
