**Outcome assessed:** immediate post-intervention *intentions to consume the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables (RDIFV)*, comparing the leaflet-like motivational/planning intervention with the control questionnaire task. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The allocation sequence appears genuinely random: “*intervention and control booklets were placed in a single pile in an order determined by a sequence of random integers generated by a true random number generator*.” This supports a judgement of *Yes* for random sequence generation.

   However, the report does not describe measures ensuring that the allocation sequence was concealed until participants had been enrolled and assigned. Distribution from a single pre-arranged pile in a lecture theatre/laboratory could be adequately concealed in practice, but no information is given about whether booklets were externally indistinguishable, sealed, numbered, or distributed by someone unaware of their allocation. Therefore, allocation concealment is uncertain.

   Reported baseline comparisons did not identify statistically significant differences between intervention and control groups in pre-intervention behaviours or cognitions: “*MANOVA … revealed no significant differences between the control and experimental groups*,” with no differences in age or gender. This does not suggest a clear failure of randomization. However, the relevant baseline analyses appear to have been undertaken in the follow-up/analysed sample rather than unequivocally among all 218 randomized participants, limiting reassurance.

   Thus, random sequence generation was adequate and there is no evident major baseline imbalance, but uncertainty about allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Participants necessarily became aware of their assigned condition because they either received the intervention messages and planning exercises or completed a control task. Personnel distributing booklets may also have been aware, although this is not fully described.

   For the effect of assignment, RoB 2 considers only deviations that arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, and could have affected the outcome. The authors took a precaution against cross-condition contamination: “*To reduce the risk of cross condition contamination, participants were explicitly requested not to discuss the contents of their booklet with other participants over the following week.*” There is no report of contamination, switching between conditions, differential co-interventions, or failure to deliver the assigned booklet/task.

   The immediate post-intervention intention analysis appears to retain all participants who completed the initial booklet. The ANCOVA for intention has \(F(1,215)\), consistent with analysis of 218 participants, the number reported as having completed the booklet. Participants were analysed according to their allocated intervention/control condition, with baseline intention included as a covariate. This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment, subject to the separate considerations for missing data.

   Consequently, although awareness of allocation was inherent to this behavioural intervention, there is no evidence of trial-context deviations from intended intervention that would bias the assignment effect.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The outcome of interest—immediate post-intervention intentions—was measured in the same booklet session as randomization, intervention/control delivery, and baseline measurement. The article reports that “*218 people completed the booklet*.” The intention ANCOVA was reported as \(F(1,215)=5.33\), which corresponds to an analysis including 218 observations with one covariate and two groups. This strongly indicates that immediate post-intervention intention data were available for all randomized participants.

   The reported 33% attrition occurred at the separate one-week behavioural follow-up: “*At follow-up, 146 people responded … with an overall attrition rate of 33%.*” That loss is relevant to the one-week behavioural outcomes, but not to the immediate post-intervention intentions result assessed here.

   Therefore, there is no indication of important missing immediate post-intervention intention data, and missingness is unlikely to have materially biased this result.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Intentions were measured using a participant-reported five-item self-report scale, for example: “*I intend to eat the RDIFV every day over the next week*.” The scale had high internal consistency (post-intervention Cronbach’s \(\alpha=.93\)), so there is no clear reason to regard the measurement instrument itself as inappropriate.

   However, participants were aware of their assigned condition. The intervention explicitly encouraged a change in intention, including messages such as: “*You can do it, so do it this week. Make a firm decision now that you will eat the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables each day this week.*” Immediately afterwards, participants were instructed to complete the cognition measures based on how they felt “*now*.”

   For this participant-reported outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor. Knowledge of receiving an intervention designed explicitly to increase intention could readily influence reported intentions through expectations, demand characteristics, social desirability, or the immediate persuasive effect of the leaflet/task. This concern is especially strong because the outcome was measured immediately after the intervention and closely reflects the intervention’s stated target.

   Accordingly, it is likely that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of the assigned intervention, leading to **high risk of bias** in measurement of the outcome.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The article clearly identifies intention as a planned target and hypothesis: “*compared with (no leaflet) controls, students who received the intervention will have higher scores on intention, perceived behavioural control, self-efficacy and anticipated regret*.” It also describes the intention scale and reports the intended adjusted comparison: “*Univariate ANCOVAs were used to test the difference between post-intervention means between conditions, controlling for pre-intervention scores.*” The result for intention is reported, including the ANCOVA statistic, \(F(1,215)=5.33, p<.05\).

   Nevertheless, no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied materials. The trial-registration record referred to in the request was not available for review here; therefore, it cannot be verified whether the immediate post-intervention intention outcome, its five-item composite measure, its time point, and the ANCOVA approach were pre-specified before unblinded data were available.

   The study assessed several cognition constructs and multiple behavioural outcomes, and potentially alternative analyses could have been conducted (for example, final-value analyses, change-score analyses, or alternative covariate specifications). There is no direct evidence that the reported intention result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable, so high risk is not justified. However, absence of a verifiable prospective analysis plan means selection from multiple measurements or analyses cannot be excluded.

   Therefore, the judgement is **some concerns**.
