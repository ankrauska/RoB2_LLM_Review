1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The allocation sequence appears to have contained a random component: “*268 three-digit random numbers from the table were written in the patient registers in advance. Patients with even numbers were given PB-MDT and those with odd numbers were given ROM.*” Use of a random-number table and allocation according to parity would ordinarily generate a random sequence.

   However, allocation concealment was not adequately described. Numbers were entered in patient registers “*in advance*,” but the report does not state that the registers were inaccessible to recruiters, that allocations were revealed only after irreversible enrolment, or that central randomization, opaque sealed envelopes, or another concealment mechanism was used. Thus, it is unclear whether investigators enrolling patients could foresee the next allocation.

   Baseline characteristics do not strongly suggest a failure of randomization. The arms were equal in size (134 each), and most baseline differences were modest. Mean age was somewhat higher in the ROM group (40.2 versus 36.4 years; *P* = 0.059), and disease-duration distributions differed slightly, but these differences are compatible with chance and do not constitute compelling evidence of manipulation or a problematic randomization process.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   This assessment concerns the effect of assignment at baseline. Participants and personnel were not blinded: “*Blinding to treatment was not undertaken.*” The two regimens also differed substantially in delivery: ROM doses were fully supervised monthly, whereas PB-MDT included self-administered daily dapsone between monthly supervised doses.

   Although the report does not establish that treatment discontinuation or other deviations arose specifically because of the trial context, the analysis used for the 2-year cure result was not an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis. The reported cure rates were explicitly based on participants who completed treatment and were followed up: “*The cure rate at 2 years was 99% in ROM-6 and 97.0% in PB-MDT group, of those who completed treatment*.” At 2 years, outcome data were reported for only 100/134 participants assigned to PB-MDT and 103/134 assigned to ROM-6.

   Post-randomization exclusions were substantial. Treatment was completed by 107/134 (79.9%) in the PB-MDT group and 115/134 (85.8%) in the ROM-6 group; 27 PB-MDT participants and 19 ROM-6 participants defaulted. The reasons for discontinuation included intolerance/weakness, swelling, vomiting/diarrhoea, pregnancy, feeling cured, stigma, and loss to treatment. Excluding these participants from the cure analysis has clear potential to materially affect the effect estimate, especially because treatment completion differed between groups and discontinuation may be related to prognosis or treatment response.

   Therefore, the result does not estimate the effect of assignment to PB-MDT versus ROM-6 in all randomized participants.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Two-year cure data were not available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants. At 2 years, the analysis included 100/134 (74.6%) participants in the PB-MDT group and 103/134 (76.9%) in the ROM-6 group. Thus, outcome data were unavailable for approximately one quarter of each randomized group.

   The study provides no sensitivity analysis or other analysis demonstrating that the 2-year cure comparison was robust to plausible outcomes among missing participants. Instead, the authors acknowledge an important follow-up limitation: “*A number of patients were lost to follow up after release from treatment*.”

   Missingness could plausibly depend on the true clinical outcome. Reasons for default included “*feeling that they were cured*,” drug intolerance/weakness, swelling, vomiting/diarrhoea, and being “*lost to treatment*.” A participant who believed they were cured may have been less likely to remain in follow-up, whereas participants with adverse effects, persisting active disease, or poor clinical response may also have been less likely to continue. The authors’ later tracing of defaulters supports the possibility that their outcomes differed: among traced defaulters, some remained clinically active and one ROM-6 defaulter had developed grade 2 disability.

   Because missing data were substantial, were not adequately handled, and were plausibly related to cure status, this domain is at high risk of bias.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Cure was assessed clinically using lesion resolution, flattening, hypopigmentation, reduction in lesion size, and/or regained sensation: “*Cure of the disease was defined as complete resolution of the lesion or patch becoming flat, hypopigmented and with decrease in size of the lesion and/or regaining of sensation.*” These are clinically relevant measures for paucibacillary leprosy, and the same stated clinical criteria and follow-up schedule were used in both groups.

   However, treatment was unblinded, and there is no indication that outcome assessment was performed by assessors blinded to treatment allocation. The article states: “*Blinding to treatment was not undertaken.*” The outcome involved clinical judgement, particularly in judging lesion activity, flattening, infiltration, lesion size, and recovery of sensation. Therefore, knowledge of assigned treatment could have influenced assessment.

   There is no direct evidence that assessors systematically favoured either regimen, and both were active treatment regimens; therefore, a high-risk judgement is not compelled. Nevertheless, the absence of blinded assessment for a partly subjective clinical outcome raises some concerns.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The trial was registered only after recruitment, treatment, and follow-up had occurred: “*This study is now registered on Clinical Trial Registry of India vide REF/2013/11/005995 dated 22 November, 2013.*” Recruitment ran from June 2001 to July 2004, and the study was conducted through November 2010. Consequently, the registry record could not provide a prospectively finalized analysis plan for the 2-year cure result.

   The publication reports cure at several time points—release from treatment, 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months, and later follow-up—and reports both cure and relapse outcomes. Thus, there were multiple potential outcome time points and potentially multiple ways to summarize cure. The article does identify an “*interim observation at 2 years after release from treatment*,” but it does not provide a pre-specified statistical analysis plan stating that the reported 2-year cure analysis, its denominator, handling of defaulters, and statistical methods were fixed before unblinded outcome data were available.

   There is no clear evidence that the reported 2-year result was selected because it was favourable, so high risk of bias is not justified. However, in the absence of prospective registration or a pre-specified analysis plan, the risk of selection among time points and analyses cannot be excluded.
