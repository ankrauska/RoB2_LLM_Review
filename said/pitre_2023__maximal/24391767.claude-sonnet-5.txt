# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Image Quality Parameters

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation:** The article explicitly states "Eligible patients were randomly assigned (computer-generated sequence)," satisfying the criterion for a truly random allocation process.
- **Allocation concealment:** "Randomization was not stratified and allocation was concealed (using sealed envelopes)." This is an adequate (if minimal) method of concealment, and there is no indication that the envelope system was compromised (e.g., no report of unsealed/translucent envelopes or predictable block sizes).
- **Baseline imbalance:** Table 1 shows patient characteristics (sex, age, height, weight, BMI, body circumference, claustrophobia score, state anxiety, region of imaging) were well balanced between groups, with all P values > 0.05 (e.g., sex P=.8, age P=.1, BMI P=.3, region P=.9). The authors explicitly state groups "were well matched between both groups." There is no evidence of imbalance suggestive of a failure of randomization.

All three signalling questions point toward low risk: allocation concealed = Yes; sequence random = Yes; baseline imbalance suggestive of problem = No.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Awareness of intervention:** Both participants and personnel were necessarily aware of the assigned intervention, as the study explicitly states: "Patients could not be blinded to the assigned study group due to the MR imaging setting."
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** A substantial number of participants did not receive their randomly assigned intervention. Per Figure 1 and the Methods: "If patients did not complete imaging in their assigned MR scanner due to claustrophobia, they were cross-referred to imaging in the other scanner." Table 2 indicates 25 of 93 examinations (~27%) were performed after cross-referral. This is a deviation directly caused by the trial context (i.e., the intervention itself provoking claustrophobia, an issue intrinsically related to open vs. closed-bore design) and inconsistent with the original random allocation.
- **Effect on outcome:** This deviation plausibly affects the image-quality outcome, since patients unable to tolerate their assigned scanner (who may be more prone to motion, anxiety-related artifacts) were re-allocated to and imaged on the alternate scanner, and the resulting image was analyzed as if reflecting that scanner's inherent performance.
- **Balance between groups:** The cross-referral was not necessarily balanced — claustrophobic events (and thus cross-over) plausibly differed by initial assignment (open vs. short-bore), as the companion trial's central hypothesis concerned differential claustrophobia rates between the two scanner types.
- **Appropriateness of analysis (Q2.6/2.7):** Critically, the statement "Examinations performed after cross-referral, with or without sedation, were included in the primary analysis" indicates that the primary analysis of image quality was based on the scanner actually used to complete the scan rather than the scanner to which the patient was originally randomized. This is analogous to an "as-treated" analysis rather than a strict intention-to-treat analysis of assignment. Given that this affected ~27% of examinations, the potential impact on the result is substantial.

Because participants/personnel were unblinded, deviations arose from trial context, likely affected the outcome, and the analysis effectively regrouped participants by intervention received rather than assigned (with a substantial potential impact), this domain is judged **high risk of bias** for the effect of assignment to intervention.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Availability of outcome data:** Of 106 patients with a clinical indication for spinal MRI, only 93 (87.7%) underwent MR imaging and were included in the image-quality analysis; 13 patients (12.3%) never completed imaging on either scanner and therefore have no outcome data at all ("Only patients who could not undergo MR imaging in either of the two scanners received conscious sedation... Ninety-three of the 106 patients... underwent an MR examination").
- **Could missingness depend on the true value of the outcome?** The reason for these missing data was claustrophobia preventing scan completion. Because the central research question of the parent trial concerns differential claustrophobia by scanner type, and claustrophobia/anxiety-driven motion is plausibly related to image quality (e.g., more motion artifacts, poorer contour sharpness), it is plausible that the propensity for a participant to be entirely excluded (due to intolerance of scanning) correlates with what their image quality would have been, and this propensity likely differs by originally assigned arm.
- **Evidence the result is not biased:** No sensitivity analyses addressing this specific exclusion (13 patients with no data at all) were presented; subgroup analyses reported in the paper address cross-referral and sedation, not the fully un-scanned patients.
- Given the moderate proportion of missing outcome data (12.3%), the plausible (but not clearly demonstrated) relationship between missingness and the true outcome value, and lack of explicit reasons/breakdown by arm for these specific 13 exclusions, this is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "High risk," since there is not definitive evidence that missingness was related to outcome value in a strongly differential way between arms.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** The measurement methods (qualitative 1–5 grading scales for contrast, contour sharpness, overall quality, artifacts, noise; quantitative SNR, CNR, and ImageJ-based contour sharpness measurement) are standard, validated approaches for MR image quality assessment and appropriate for the outcome domain.
- **Comparability of measurement across groups:** The same rating scales, ROI placement rules, and quantitative formulas were applied identically to images from both scanners: "The optimal viewing window-level parameters for each image were adjusted automatically by the examiners..." and sequence parameters were deliberately matched ("the primary aim of the sequence setup was to obtain a voxel size and imaging time that is as similar as possible on both scanners"). Thus the *method* of measurement itself did not differ between arms.
- **Blinding of assessors:** The authors report: "At the time of the analysis the examiners were blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity." This is a strength.
- **Potential for influence despite blinding:** However, true blinding is difficult to achieve in this comparison because open- and short-bore MR images have intrinsically different visual characteristics (different coil geometry, noise texture, field-of-view, and artifact patterns), which could allow assessors to infer which scanner produced an image despite the stated blinding. Several of the outcome measures (overall quality, contrast, contour sharpness) involve subjective judgment, which is potentially influenced by such inference, especially given the study's underlying hypothesis that one scanner would outperform the other.
- Because this is an outcome involving observer judgment, blinding was attempted but its success is questionable given the visibly distinct image characteristics inherent to the two scanner types, this domain is judged "Some concerns" (rather than "Low," which would require confidence that assessment could not be influenced by awareness of intervention, or "High," for which there would need to be clear evidence that assessment was, in fact, influenced).

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- A detailed trial protocol was published in advance describing the methods and design for image quality assessment (Enders J et al., BMC Med Imaging 2011, cited as Protocol S1), specifying qualitative rating scales, quantitative SNR/CNR calculation, and contour sharpness methodology, which appear to have been followed exactly as pre-specified in the results (Tables 3 and 4 match the described qualitative/quantitative parameters).
- All pre-specified sequences (sagittal T1, sagittal T2, axial T2) and all outcome measures (overall image quality, contrast, contour sharpness, noise, artifacts, SNR, CNR, quantitative contour sharpness) are reported comprehensively and consistently across both anatomical regions (cervicothoracic and thoracolumbar spine), with no indication that measures were selectively omitted based on results.
- Optional sequences (STIR, post-contrast T1) were explicitly excluded a priori "due to the small number," a reasonable, non-results-based justification rather than selective reporting.
- The trial registry (NCT00715806) primary outcome concerns claustrophobia reduction, and this image-quality analysis is a pre-planned secondary/companion analysis with its own dedicated published protocol; there is no evidence of selection among multiple analytic approaches or outcome definitions based on the direction/significance of results.

## Overall Risk of Bias Summary for "Image Quality Parameters"

| Domain | Judgement |
|---|---|
| 1. Randomization | Low risk |
| 2. Deviations from intended intervention | **High risk** |
| 3. Missing outcome data | Some concerns |
| 4. Measurement of the outcome | Some concerns |
| 5. Selective reporting | Low risk |

**Overall judgement: High risk of bias**, driven primarily by Domain 2 (the substantial cross-referral of participants between scanners, contrary to original random assignment, analyzed in the arm of the scanner actually used rather than assigned), compounded by "Some concerns" in the missing outcome data and outcome measurement domains.
