**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
- **a. Judgement:** The trial used block randomization, but it is unclear if the allocation sequence was adequately concealed. There was a statistically significant baseline imbalance in age between the groups. 
- **b. Rationale:** The trial reports that "Study patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio by using the mixed block randomization (4 and 6 blocks) method," indicating a random sequence generation. However, the method of allocation concealment (e.g., central randomization, sealed opaque envelopes) is not described. Additionally, the study notes a baseline imbalance: "the mean age in the candesartan-HCT group (51.0 [10.2] years) was about 3.2 years higher than that in the candesartan monotherapy group (47.8 [10.7] years; P = 0.02) despite random allocation." While this could be due to chance, the lack of information on allocation concealment coupled with the baseline imbalance warrants some concerns.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
- **a. Judgement:** Although the trial was open-label, an appropriate modified intention-to-treat (mITT) analysis was used to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention, and there is no evidence that deviations from intended interventions arose due to the trial context. 
- **b. Rationale:** The trial was open-label, meaning participants and personnel were aware of the assigned intervention (the protocol confirms "Open-label, Randomised"). However, to estimate the effect of assignment to intervention, the investigators used an appropriate analysis strategy: "Efficacy data were evaluated in the intent-to-treat (ITT) population. The ITT population consisted of patients who had baseline BP values and >=1 postrandomization BP values and who had taken >=1 dose of the study drug." According to RoB 2 guidance, this modified ITT approach is considered appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment, and there is no clear indication that clinically significant deviations from the protocol occurred due to the trial context.

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
- **a. Judgement:** Outcome data were available for all or nearly all participants randomized who were included in the ITT population.
- **b. Rationale:** Out of 233 randomized patients, 227 took at least one dose of the study medication, and the ITT set consisted of 224 patients (who had baseline and at least one post-randomization BP value). Table II shows that data for the 4-week DBP outcome were analyzed for 111 patients in the combination group and 113 in the monotherapy group, accounting for all 224 participants in the ITT set. Therefore, missing outcome data is minimal and unlikely to bias the results.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
- **a. Judgement:** The outcome was measured using an automated device, which limits the potential for the assessor's knowledge of the intervention to influence the measurement.
- **b. Rationale:** Although the trial was open-label and investigators were aware of the assigned interventions, blood pressure was measured using an objective, automated method: "BPs were measured in each patient’s arm by the same investigator at each center with a fully automated device produced by Omron." Because the measurement is largely automated and requires minimal subjective judgment by the observer, the assessment of the outcome is unlikely to be substantially influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**5. selective reporting: low risk of bias**
- **a. Judgement:** The reported result corresponds exactly to the pre-specified primary outcome in the trial registration, and the data were analyzed according to a pre-specified plan.
- **b. Rationale:** The trial registry (NCT00621153) lists the primary outcome as "Changes in Mean Sitting DBP From Baseline After 4 Weeks of Therapy." The published article reports exactly this outcome as its primary endpoint and presents the results completely. There is no evidence of selection of the reported result from multiple outcome measurements or multiple analyses.
