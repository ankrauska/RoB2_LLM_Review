**Outcome assessed:** Motor Assessment Scale item 6—Upper Arm Function (MAS6), measured at baseline (0 weeks), post-training (4 weeks), and follow-up (12 weeks), comparing active tRNS plus reaching training with sham tRNS plus reaching training. The assessment concerns the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The report and prospectively registered protocol describe an adequately random sequence and reasonably adequate allocation concealment.

* The article states: “**An offsite investigator prepared the concealed randomization using a computer generated random number sequence (1:1).**”
* It further states: “**Consecutively numbered opaque envelopes containing group allocation were collected from the offsite investigator by the tRNS intervener after initial assessment.**”
* The trial registry similarly pre-specified that “**an independent person will prepare the concealed randomisation using a computer generated random number sequence**” and that allocation would be placed in consecutively numbered opaque envelopes.

Thus, the allocation sequence was random, and the envelopes were apparently held independently and accessed only after baseline assessment. Although the report does not explicitly state that envelopes were sealed/tamper-proof, the offsite preparation, opaque consecutively numbered envelopes, and post-baseline release provide reasonable assurance of concealment.

There is no important evidence that baseline differences indicate failure of randomization. The groups were very small (two participants per arm), so some differences are expected by chance. Baseline MAS6 scores were 1 and 0 in the active group and 2 and 1 in the sham group; this does not constitute a clear or implausible imbalance indicating a compromised randomization process.

---

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
Participants, reaching trainers, assessors, and analysts were intended to be blinded, reducing the likelihood of trial-context deviations that differed systematically by group.

* The article reports: “**Study personnel involved in assessment and training, along with all participants were blinded to group allocation for the duration of the study.**”
* The registry specifies that participants, those administering treatment, outcome assessors, and data analysts were masked.
* The tRNS intervener alone was unblinded, with stimulation parameters recorded separately and stored in a separate locked cabinet.

The intervention appears to have been delivered as planned. All participants completed the assigned treatment schedule: “**All four participants completed the 12 training sessions over 4-weeks, with no training sessions missed.**” There is no reported crossover between active and sham tRNS, and average reaching repetitions were broadly similar across groups overall. There is no reported evidence of important differential cointerventions or departures arising because of the trial context.

However, there are **some concerns about the analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment**. The registry prespecified an intention-to-treat, two-group-by-three-phase mixed-effects analysis with between-group comparisons. In contrast, the publication presents the trial primarily as a set of four individual cases and reports individual MAS6 trajectories. It does not present the prespecified comparative group analysis for MAS6. The authors state that “**the amount of improvement varied across individuals and appeared to be independent of group allocation**,” but do not provide a formal between-group effect estimate.

All four reported participants were retained and reported according to their allocated group, so there is no evidence that participants were analysed in the wrong arm or excluded because of non-adherence. Nonetheless, the absence of the planned comparative intention-to-treat analysis raises some concern regarding whether the reported MAS6 result appropriately estimates the effect of assignment to active versus sham tRNS.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
For the four participants described in the publication, outcome data appear complete: all completed 12 training sessions and MAS6 values are reported at baseline, post-training, and follow-up. The article states: “**All four participants completed the 12 training sessions over 4-weeks, with no training sessions missed.**”

However, there is a material, unexplained discrepancy between the published report and the trial registry regarding the number of recruited participants:

* The article states: “**Four participants were recruited**.”
* The ANZCTR record reports a **final sample size of 8**.
* The registry also gives an actual recruitment period ending on 3 November 2014, whereas the publication gives no participant flow diagram or explanation of whether additional recruited/randomized participants were excluded, withdrew, had missing data, or were otherwise not reported.

If eight participants were enrolled/randomized, outcome data are reported for only four—potentially half of the randomized sample. The report provides no CONSORT-style flow diagram, no accounting of the discrepancy, no reasons for the apparent non-reporting of four participants, and no sensitivity analysis. This prevents confidence that data were available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants.

The absence of data and reasons for the potentially unreported participants means that missingness could plausibly be related to outcome or intervention experience. Given the possible magnitude of unreported data and lack of explanation, this is judged at **high risk of bias** due to missing outcome data. This judgement is conditional on the registry’s final sample size of eight being correct; clarification from the investigators or registry would be needed to resolve the discrepancy.

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
MAS6 is an appropriate, prespecified clinical measure of upper-arm function after stroke. The study used the same outcome measure and the same scheduled assessment time points in both groups.

* The report identifies MAS6 as “**the primary clinical outcome measure**.”
* The scale was measured at baseline, post-training, and follow-up for both active and sham groups.
* The assessors were blinded: “**Study personnel involved in assessment and training, along with all participants were blinded to group allocation for the duration of the study.**”

MAS6 involves observer assessment and therefore includes some professional judgement. However, the stated blinding of the assessor means that differential assessment caused by knowledge of whether active or sham tRNS was received is unlikely. There is also no indication that measurement procedures, timing, or thresholds differed between groups.

---

### 5. Selective reporting: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The trial was prospectively registered before enrolment, but the published MAS6 reporting differs importantly from the registered outcome and analysis plans.

The ANZCTR record prespecified:

* a **primary outcome** of the “**Composite score of Motor Assessment Scale upper limb items 6–8**”; and
* an intention-to-treat, two-group-by-three-timepoint mixed-effects analysis, followed by between-group comparisons.

In contrast, the publication states: “**The primary clinical outcome measure was Motor Assessment Scale item 6 (Upper Arm Function, MAS6).**” It reports MAS6 in the main results table, but does not present the registered composite MAS items 6–8 score. Although MAS7 and MAS8 are mentioned as secondary outcomes in the methods, their numerical findings are not presented in Table 3 or elsewhere in the supplied results.

Therefore, the publication appears to have selected an individual component—MAS6—from the registered composite primary outcome and re-designated it as the primary clinical outcome. Further, the prespecified comparative mixed-effects/between-group analysis was not reported; instead, the published presentation is descriptive and participant-specific.

Because the prespecified composite outcome and planned comparative analysis were not reported, while a component outcome was emphasized, the MAS6 result is likely to have been selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements and/or analyses. This creates a substantial risk that the reported MAS6 result was selected after knowledge of the data.
