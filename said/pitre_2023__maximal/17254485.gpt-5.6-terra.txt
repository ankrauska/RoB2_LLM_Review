1. **Bias due to randomization: Some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The allocation sequence appears to have included a random component: “*A random number table was produced through Microsoft Excel. The patients were divided into a control group and a therapy group following the random number table.*” This supports a judgement that the sequence was random.

   However, the report does not describe any method of **allocation concealment**. There is no information on central randomization, independently held allocation, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another procedure preventing recruiters from knowing the next allocation before enrolment. Under RoB 2, absence of information on concealment leads to some concerns when baseline data do not otherwise indicate a problem.

   Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable between groups. The authors state that “*the sex, age, degree of cirrhosis and varices were not significantly different between the two groups*.” The table reports similar distributions of active oesophageal bleeding, red signs, Child–Pugh score, portal-vein width, numbers of oesophageal varices, and GOV subtype. Although there are some numerical differences (e.g., F3 varices: 42/90 versus 51/82), these are not clearly implausible by chance and do not demonstrate a failure of randomization.

   Thus, random sequence generation is adequately described and baseline imbalance does not clearly suggest a problem, but lack of information about allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: Low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to concurrent embolization plus EVL versus embolization followed by EVL two months later, regardless of whether participants adhered perfectly to treatment.

   Participants and treating clinicians were necessarily aware of the assigned strategy, since the interventions were endoscopic procedures delivered either concurrently or at different times. However, lack of blinding alone does not establish bias for the effect of assignment. RoB 2 requires evidence that deviations from the intended strategy arose because of the trial context, affected emergency hemostasis, and were unbalanced between groups.

   The intervention procedures were described clearly:
   - Therapy group: “*Embolization and ligation were performed at the same time*.”
   - Control group: “*embolization was performed and then EVL was performed two months later*.”
   - “*Somatostatin was administered before and after the operation*” in both groups.

   In the control group, EVL was used as rescue treatment in patients in whom hemorrhage was not controlled after embolization: “*EVL was performed for the patients whose hemorrhage was not controllable. Hemorrhaging was controlled after EVL.*” This appears to be a clinical response to persistent bleeding rather than a non-protocol deviation caused by trial participation or personnel undermining the randomized comparison. Importantly, the reported emergency-hemostasis outcome retained the initial control-group failures (72/82 controlled, 10/82 not controlled), rather than reclassifying rescue-treated patients as initial successes.

   The outcome data appear to have been analysed according to randomized group: the reported emergency-hemostasis rates use all 90 therapy-group participants and all 82 control-group participants. There is no indication of an as-treated or per-protocol analysis, nor of post-randomization exclusions. Therefore, there is no evidence that deviations arising from the trial context biased the intention-to-treat effect.

3. **Missing outcome data: Low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Emergency-hemostasis data were reported for all randomized participants:

   - Therapy group: “*Hemorrhaging was controlled in all 90 patients in the therapy group, the rate of emergency hemostasis was 100.0%.*”
   - Control group: “*hemorrhaging was controlled in 72 out of 82 patients and the rate of emergency hemostasis was 87.8%.*”

   Thus, the numerical outcome includes all 172 randomized participants (90 + 82). The 10 control-group patients whose bleeding was not controlled were counted as failures for the emergency-hemostasis result, even though they subsequently received rescue EVL.

   There is no report of loss to follow-up, withdrawal, missing emergency-hemostasis assessments, or imputation for this acute in-hospital outcome. Consequently, missing data could not plausibly have materially changed the estimated effect.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: Some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Emergency hemostasis is a clinically important and reasonably appropriate outcome for acute variceal bleeding. The report provides endoscopic illustration that bleeding stopped following treatment and states that “*active gastric fundus variceal bleeding was controlled after embolization*” and “*active esophageal variceal bleeding was controlled after EVL*.” There is no indication that different formal outcome-measurement methods or thresholds were deliberately used between groups.

   Nevertheless, the outcome definition is insufficiently explicit. The article reports that “*hemorrhaging was controlled*,” but does not prespecify objective criteria for emergency hemostasis—for example, a defined observation period without hematemesis/melena, stable hemoglobin, hemodynamic criteria, or blinded endoscopic confirmation. In contrast, the authors gave a more detailed definition for early rebleeding.

   Outcome assessors were almost certainly aware of allocated intervention, because clinicians both delivered the procedures and evaluated whether bleeding had been controlled. The assessment of hemostasis may involve some clinical judgement, particularly where the initial bleeding source included both gastric and oesophageal varices and where rescue EVL could be given for persistent bleeding. Knowledge of treatment assignment could therefore potentially influence the determination or timing of “controlled” bleeding.

   There is no direct evidence that assessors actually classified outcomes differently because of this knowledge, and the outcome is more objective than a patient-reported symptom. However, the open-label design combined with an inadequately specified assessment criterion raises some concerns rather than supporting a low-risk judgement.

5. **Selective reporting: Some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The publication reports an emergency-hemostasis result for the full randomized groups and gives the corresponding counts and percentages: 90/90 (100.0%) versus 72/82 (87.8%). It also reports early rebleeding and complications. There is no clear internal evidence that the emergency-hemostasis result was selected from multiple reported analyses.

   However, a low-risk RoB 2 judgement requires confidence that the result was analysed in accordance with a prespecified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available, and that it was not selected from multiple eligible measurements or analyses. The article does not provide a protocol, statistical analysis plan, or detailed prespecified definition of emergency hemostasis. It only states that enumeration data were analysed using a chi-square test and that statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

   The requested trial-registration record is referred to in the question, but its contents are not included in the material available here. Therefore, it is not possible to verify whether emergency hemostasis, its measurement time point/definition, and its analysis were prospectively specified, or whether other eligible analyses were planned but not reported.

   In the absence of a dated prespecified analysis plan or accessible registry information sufficient to verify the outcome and analysis, RoB 2 indicates **some concerns** for selective reporting.
