# Risk of Bias Assessment: BDI Total Score (Post-Intervention)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation:** The article states "Participants who left their email addresses at the end of the baseline survey were randomly allocated to the treatment or wait-list group in consecutive order. No stratification was applied." No further detail on the method used to generate the random sequence (e.g., computer-generated randomization, block size) is provided. This is insufficient to confirm the sequence was truly random (Probably Yes, given this is a formally registered trial, but lacking detail).
- **Allocation concealment:** No information is provided about concealment mechanisms. Since allocation occurred automatically via online consecutive assignment after survey completion, and participants had already provided baseline data before assignment, there is limited opportunity for foreknowledge to bias enrollment; however, no explicit description of a concealment mechanism (e.g., independent randomization module, computer-generated allocation independent of investigators) is given. This is "No information."
- **Baseline balance:** Table 1 shows no significant differences between groups on demographic or clinical variables (age, gender, education, medication, therapy status, BDI severity distribution). The baseline BDI scores were also very similar (30.02 vs 28.81, p>.4). This supports that randomization was not compromised.

Given random sequence generation is not clearly described (No information) but no evidence of baseline imbalance, per the RoB2 algorithm (NI on concealment + No baseline imbalance problem) → **Some concerns**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Awareness of assignment:** Participants were not blinded — they knew whether they received immediate access to Deprexis or were wait-listed ("Those in the wait-list (i.e., delayed-treatment) group were informed that they would receive a voucher for Deprexis subsequent to completion of the post-survey eight weeks later"). Personnel/carers were minimal (self-help, unguided), but participants themselves were clearly aware of allocation.
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** This is a naturalistic/pragmatic design permitting co-interventions (concurrent psychotherapy/medication) in both arms — the authors explicitly note "being in current psychotherapeutic or pharmacological treatment did not lead to exclusion." The wait-list group showed an unexpectedly large improvement (d=.58), which the authors speculate may be attributable to "patients' use of treatments other than Deprexis" or "spontaneous remission." This raises the possibility that awareness of non-allocation to immediate treatment led to compensatory care-seeking (a trial-context-driven deviation), though the discussion frames this as speculative rather than confirmed.
- **Balance:** Table 1 shows treatment status distribution (no/single therapist/in-or-outpatient) is not significantly different between groups at baseline (χ²=4.32, p>.1), and medication use was balanced. There is no clear evidence that non-protocol co-interventions were systematically unbalanced between groups, though this was not tracked longitudinally (only at baseline).
- **Analysis:** An appropriate intention-to-treat analysis was conducted using multiple imputation for missing data ("ITT analyses were performed with multiple imputation using SAS PROC MI"), consistent with recommended methods for estimating the assignment effect.

Because participants were aware of assignment, and there is some indication (though not definitive) that deviations related to trial context (unblinded wait-list feeling "unlucky" and seeking alternative care) may have influenced outcomes, but it's unclear whether these deviations were substantially unbalanced or definitively affected the outcome → **Some concerns** (not "high," since the appropriate ITT analysis was used and no clear evidence of an unbalanced, outcome-affecting deviation was established).

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- Completion rate was high at 81.9%, with no significant difference in completion rates between groups (85.7% wait-list vs. 78.1% Deprexis; χ²=2.06, p>.1).
- The authors explicitly assessed whether non-completers differed from completers: "Participants who did not complete the trial were indistinguishable from non-completers on any of the background, medical or baseline psychopathological and psychological variables except for the QoL subscales tapping social relationships... and environment... where completers achieved slightly higher baseline scores." This provides evidence against a strong relationship between missingness and outcome.
- Additionally, the study conducted intention-to-treat analyses using multiple imputation via SAS PROC MI stratified by treatment group, which is an appropriate method attempting to address missing outcome data, and the ITT results were highly consistent with the per-protocol/completer results (Table 2), providing evidence that the result is robust to the missing data.
- Given: high completion rate, similar/balanced attrition between groups, minimal baseline differences between completers/non-completers, and consistent ITT vs. completer results, missingness is unlikely to have biased the result.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** The BDI is a well-validated, appropriate self-report instrument for measuring depression severity, with good psychometric properties reported ("correlation coefficients between BDI and the HDRS ranging from r=.61 to .81"). Method not inappropriate.
- **Comparability across groups:** The same self-report questionnaire was administered identically to both groups online at the same 8-week timepoint, so measurement method did not differ between arms.
- **Blinding of outcome assessor:** Because the BDI is a participant-reported outcome, the "outcome assessor" is the participant themselves. Participants were not blinded to their intervention assignment (they knew if they received immediate access to Deprexis or were on the wait-list).
- **Potential for influence:** For participant-reported outcomes, knowledge of intervention assignment could plausibly influence self-report of depressive symptoms (e.g., demand characteristics, disappointment in wait-list group, or a sense of receiving "help" in the treatment group could influence responses). This aligns with RoB2 guidance that participant-reported outcomes assessed without blinding default to at least "Some concerns."
- There is no strong specific evidence (e.g., extreme placebo-like expectancy effects documented) that assessment was clearly and strongly influenced in a way that would elevate this to "high risk," but the structural potential for bias from unblinded self-report is clear and unavoidable given the study design (wait-list, unblinded, self-help intervention). This corresponds to "Some concerns" rather than "High," since there is no definitive evidence the outcome assessment was in fact strongly influenced (versus merely being susceptible to influence).

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial was prospectively registered (NCT01401296) with BDI listed as the sole primary outcome and WHOQOL-BREF as a secondary outcome, with a timeframe of "7 Days" specified (note: discrepancy between the registered timeframe and the actual 8-week reported outcome, suggesting some imprecision in prospective specification).
- The BDI total score, as the pre-specified primary outcome, was reported in the article in a manner consistent with the registration (both PP and ITT analyses reported), supporting low concern for this specific analysis.
- However, several additional outcomes not listed in the registry (DAS, RSE, SBQ-R) and additional BDI subscales (negative attitude toward self, performance impairment, somatic symptoms) were reported in the published article without being pre-specified in the trial registration as secondary outcomes. This suggests some analyses/outcomes may have been added post hoc.
- No full pre-registered statistical analysis plan (SAP) was available to verify whether the specific ANCOVA approach, choice of covariates, or multiple imputation procedure was pre-specified prior to unblinded data being available. The registration provides only minimal outcome description without detail on planned analysis methods.
- For the BDI total score specifically (the outcome being assessed here), there is no clear evidence that this particular result was selected from multiple eligible analyses/measurements based on the result (i.e., it appears to be reported straightforwardly as specified), but the absence of a detailed prospectively-registered SAP prevents a "Low risk" judgement.

Given the primary outcome (BDs total score) itself appears reported in line with what was registered, but a full pre-specified SAP is unavailable to confirm the analytic approach was locked in before unblinded data access → **Some concerns**.

**Overall judgement: High risk of bias** — Per RoB 2 guidance, "Some concerns" in multiple domains (here, four domains) can lead to an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" when this substantially lowers confidence in the result. The accumulation of concerns regarding unclear allocation concealment, unblinded participant-reported outcome measurement, potential unbalanced trial-context deviations (particularly the unexplained large wait-list improvement), and incomplete verification of a pre-specified analysis plan collectively undermine confidence in the BDI result.
