1. **Bias due to randomization: Some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The study is described as a “controlled, randomised, open study with two parallel groups,” but the report provides no information on how the allocation sequence was generated or concealed. In particular, it does not state whether computer-generated randomization, random-number tables, sealed opaque envelopes, central allocation, or another adequately concealed process was used.

   * Allocation sequence random: **No information.** The report states only that subjects “were randomised to receive a booster dose of either Avaxim™ or Vaqta™.”
   * Allocation concealment: **No information.** No method preventing recruiters from knowing the next assignment is described.
   * Baseline imbalance: **Probably no evidence of a problem.** The groups were closely similar in reported demographic variables: mean age was 38.4 versus 38.2 years; 55% versus 52% were male; and mean weight and height were also very similar. Pre-booster antibody GMTs differed numerically (496 versus 325 mIU/mL), but this difference was reported as not statistically significant (*p* = 0.171), and there is no indication of an imbalance implausible by chance.

   Thus, there is no evidence that randomization failed, but the lack of information about both sequence generation and allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: Some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   This assessment concerns the **effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline**. The trial was explicitly open-label: “This was a single centre, controlled, randomised, open study.” Therefore, participants and personnel administering the vaccines were aware of assigned intervention.

   * Participants aware of intervention: **Yes.**
   * Carers/personnel aware of intervention: **Yes.**
   * Deviations arising because of the trial context: **Probably no / no evidence reported.** There is no indication that knowledge of allocation led to differential use of cointerventions, altered vaccine administration, or other non-protocol interventions likely to influence anti-HAV antibody titres at 1 month. Both vaccines were administered intramuscularly in the deltoid region, and the laboratory outcome was measured at the same scheduled post-vaccination interval.
   * Analysis appropriate for assignment effect: **Probably no.** Although the authors state that both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses were undertaken, they report only the per-protocol immunogenicity result: “The results of both analyses were close enough to allow only the per-protocol results to be reported.” The reported GMT result therefore excludes six randomized participants from the immunogenicity analysis.
   * Potential impact of failure to report the randomized-group analysis: **Probably no substantial impact, but uncertain.** Six of 127 randomized participants were excluded from the per-protocol immunogenicity analysis. This is not a large proportion, and the authors state that ITT and per-protocol findings were “close,” but numerical ITT results were not provided for verification.

   Open-label conduct alone does not create bias for the assignment effect unless trial-context deviations occurred and affected the outcome. No such deviations are identified. However, the selected reported result is per-protocol rather than a fully reported ITT/mITT analysis, resulting in some concerns.

3. **Missing outcome data: Low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data appear available for nearly all randomized participants. Of 127 randomized participants, 121 were included in the per-protocol immunogenicity analysis (59 Vaqta™ and 62 Avaxim™).

   The report states that six participants were excluded from the per-protocol analysis:

   > “one subject had previously received a hepatitis A booster, one subject travelled to India between visits and four subjects did not have pre- and post-vaccination serological results available.”

   The relevant missing outcome data are the four participants lacking paired serological measurements, approximately 3% of randomized participants. For a continuous laboratory outcome such as anti-HAV GMT, this amount of missing data is unlikely to be sufficient, by itself, to materially change the estimated comparative effect.

   There is no evidence that missing serology was related to the true 1-month antibody titre, and no differential reasons for missing outcome data by intervention group are reported. Although the numbers of participants in the per-protocol groups differ slightly from those randomized (64 versus 63 randomized; 59 versus 62 analysed), the report does not establish that the difference was due specifically to missing outcome measurements or that missingness depended on outcome values. Therefore, the outcome data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: Low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The outcome was anti-HAV antibody titre measured approximately 1 month after booster vaccination, using the same laboratory method for both intervention groups.

   * Appropriateness of measurement: **Yes, appropriate.** The study used a quantitative modified HAVAB™ assay, calibrated using WHO standard immune globulin:

     > “Test samples were compared with the standard curve and the level of anti-HAV in mIU/mL was determined. Levels were measured down to 6 mIU/mL.”

   * Measurement method differing between groups: **No.** Serological evaluations for both groups were performed by Merck Research Laboratories, and the same assay and timing were specified for all participants.
   * Assessor awareness: **No information.** The report does not explicitly state whether laboratory personnel were blinded to vaccine allocation.
   * Potential influence of knowledge of intervention: **Probably no.** Anti-HAV titre is an objective quantitative laboratory outcome, based on a standardized assay rather than participant or clinician judgement. Even if laboratory staff had known allocation, there is little plausible scope for knowledge of the assigned vaccine to affect the assay result.

   Accordingly, the absence of explicit laboratory blinding does not create a material risk of measurement bias for this objective outcome.

5. **Selective reporting: Some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The publication identifies the 1-month post-vaccination anti-HAV GMT comparison as the primary evaluation criterion:

   > “The primary evaluation criterion was the comparison of the anti-HAV antibody geometric mean titre (GMT) for both vaccines 1 month post-vaccination.”

   The publication reports the prespecified outcome measurement and time point, including group-specific post-booster GMTs and confidence intervals. There is therefore no direct evidence that the reported result was selected from multiple unreported time points or antibody assays.

   However, the report also states:

   > “Intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses were performed. The results of both analyses were close enough to allow only the per-protocol results to be reported.”

   Thus, more than one eligible analysis was conducted, but only the per-protocol analysis was fully reported. The report does not provide a statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available, nor does it provide the numerical ITT result. The available information does not establish that the per-protocol result was selected because it was more favourable, but it also does not allow this possibility to be excluded.

   Consequently, there are some concerns about selection among multiple analyses, rather than high risk of bias, because no clear evidence demonstrates results-based selection.
