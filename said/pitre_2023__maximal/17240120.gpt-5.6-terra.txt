**Outcome assessed:** time to relapse from the start of double-blind treatment (placebo withdrawal/switch versus continued escitalopram), analysed using the reported log-rank/Cox time-to-event analysis. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The double-blind phase used a computer-generated allocation sequence, with concealment from investigators and participants. The reported group sizes and baseline characteristics do not suggest a problem with randomization.

* The report states that eligible responders were assigned “according to a **computer-generated randomisation list**.”
* For allocation concealment: “The details of the randomisation series were **unknown to any of the investigators** and were contained in a set of **sealed opaque envelopes**.” Patients were sequentially assigned the lowest available randomization number.
* The allocation ratio was intended to be 1:1 and the resulting randomized numbers were close to balanced: 163 assigned to escitalopram and 157 to placebo. This small difference is compatible with chance, including the stated use of blocks of four across many centres.
* The authors report: “There were **no differences in demographics and clinical parameters** between the two treatment groups in the randomisation period.” Table 1 shows generally similar distributions of sex, age, race, age at onset, duration of illness and duration of current symptoms.

Although the report does not give every operational detail recommended for envelope-based concealment (for example, explicit confirmation that envelopes were opened sequentially only after irreversible enrolment), the computer-generated sequence, opaque envelopes, investigators’ lack of knowledge of the sequence, and balanced baseline data provide sufficient reassurance that allocation was adequately concealed and random.

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

**b. Rationale:**  
Participants, investigators, and other study personnel were blinded, the interventions were designed to preserve masking, and the principal analysis was conducted according to randomized assignment. There is no indication of important deviations caused by the trial context that would have biased the assignment effect.

* “All study personnel and participants were **blinded to treatment assignment for the entire duration of the study**.”
* Escitalopram and placebo tablets were described as having “**identical appearance, taste or smell**.”
* The placebo group underwent a protocol-specified taper if previously receiving escitalopram 20 mg/day: 10 mg/day for one week, followed by placebo. This was an intended component of the placebo-assignment strategy, rather than an unplanned deviation from the protocol.
* The investigators examined whether early discontinuation symptoms affected the relapse result. They report that analyses censoring relapses in the first 7 or 14 days after randomization still found escitalopram superior to placebo.
* The primary analysis was described as using the ITT population and compared groups according to their randomized treatment: “The primary efficacy analysis used a two-tailed log-rank test to compare the time to relapse for patients treated with escitalopram versus placebo.”

There is a minor reporting inconsistency: the text defines the ITT population as randomized patients who took at least one dose and had at least one post-randomization efficacy assessment, and refers to two randomized patients who did not meet the prior response criterion. However, it also states that the ITT population comprised 320 patients—the full randomized population. Even if two randomized participants had been excluded, this is too small to plausibly have a substantial effect on the time-to-relapse estimate. Therefore, it does not raise material concern for this domain.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **some concerns**

**c. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**d. Rationale:**  
The amount of non-relapse withdrawal was relatively low and similar between groups, but the report does not provide enough detail to establish that censoring was non-informative for the time-to-relapse outcome.

* During the double-blind period, withdrawal excluding relapse occurred in 13/163 (7.9%) escitalopram participants and 14/157 (8.9%) placebo participants. Thus, non-relapse withdrawal was reasonably balanced between groups.
* Relapse-related withdrawal should be counted as an outcome event rather than missing outcome data, because the protocol required that patients who relapsed be withdrawn. The relevant missing-data issue is therefore participants who withdrew or were censored for other reasons before relapse or week 24.
* The report states that sample-size calculations assumed non-relapse withdrawals would occur at the same rate in both groups, but it does not fully report the reasons for all non-relapse withdrawals or provide a sensitivity analysis specifically addressing informative censoring.
* For a time-to-event outcome, participants censored because they leave follow-up may introduce bias if their subsequent risk of relapse differs from that of participants who remain under observation. In an OCD relapse-prevention trial, withdrawal could plausibly be related to clinical status, adverse effects, perceived lack of benefit, or treatment discontinuation.

The balanced and modest withdrawal rates reduce concern, and there is no direct evidence that censoring differed materially by group. However, the absence of sufficiently detailed reasons for censoring and of analyses demonstrating robustness to informative censoring means that low risk of bias cannot be confidently assigned.

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Relapse was prospectively defined, assessed at scheduled visits using established OCD measures, and outcome assessors were blinded to treatment allocation.

* Relapse was prospectively defined as either “an increase in the Y-BOCS total score of ≥5 points from the time of randomisation, or an unsatisfactory treatment effect (lack of efficacy) as judged by the investigator.”
* The primary relapse definition was driven almost entirely by the standardized Y-BOCS criterion: “The Y-BOCS relapse criterion accounted for almost all of the relapses (116 out of 119 patients, 97%).”
* The Y-BOCS is an established clinician-rated measure for OCD symptom severity, and ratings were undertaken at prespecified, comparable intervals in both randomized groups.
* To improve reliability, “only those investigators who had actively participated in rater training sessions” could rate patients, and “patient ratings were assessed by the same investigator at each visit, whenever possible.”
* Most importantly, “all study personnel and participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire duration of the study.”

The investigator-judgement component of relapse could in principle be subjective; however, it accounted for only three of 119 relapses, and the clinicians conducting the assessments were blinded. The method of outcome assessment was comparable across treatment arms and appropriate for the outcome.

### 5. Selective reporting: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The published primary outcome and analysis are consistent with the stated prospectively defined outcome, but there is insufficient information to confirm that the reported time-to-relapse result was analysed according to a detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

* The publication explicitly identifies the outcome as prospectively defined: “The prospectively defined primary analysis of efficacy was the **time to relapse from the start date of double-blind treatment**.”
* The report also prespecifies the principal method: “The primary efficacy analysis used a **two-tailed log-rank test** to compare the time to relapse.”
* The reported primary result corresponds to this stated analysis: a statistically significant log-rank comparison and Cox-model hazard ratio of 2.74 for placebo versus escitalopram.
* The article distinguishes this primary ITT analysis from secondary or post-hoc analyses, including the per-protocol analysis, analyses censoring early relapses, and a post-hoc analysis restricted to the Y-BOCS relapse criterion. Importantly, the primary ITT result—not one of the post-hoc analyses—is emphasized as the primary result.

However, no dated, detailed statistical analysis plan is available in the provided material, and the supplied registration information is not reproduced in sufficient detail to verify that the outcome definition, time point, censoring approach, Cox analysis, and handling of protocol deviations were all finalized before access to unblinded data. Since time-to-relapse data can be analysed using alternative censoring rules, analysis populations, and relapse definitions, there remains some concern regarding selection among analyses, despite no clear evidence that such selection occurred.
