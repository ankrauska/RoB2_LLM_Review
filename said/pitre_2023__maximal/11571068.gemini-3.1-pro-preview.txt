Based on the provided RoB 2.0 guidance and the trial publication, here is the risk of bias assessment for the outcome of women’s burden and distress (measured via the Relative Stress Scale and General Health Questionnaire):

1. **Bias due to randomization**: **Some concerns**
   a. **Judgement**: The sequence generation was random, but there is no information about whether allocation was concealed, leading to some concerns.
   b. **Rationale**: The trial states, "Participants were assigned randomly via random number tables to one of three treatment conditions." This indicates a random sequence generation (low risk). However, there is no mention of how this sequence was concealed from the personnel enrolling participants (e.g., use of opaque sealed envelopes or central randomization). Therefore, allocation concealment is unclear ("No information"). Baseline characteristics were reported to not differ significantly across conditions, but the lack of information on allocation concealment defaults this domain to "Some concerns."

2. **Bias due to deviations from intended intervention** (effect of assignment): **Low risk of bias**
   a. **Judgement**: Blinding was likely not possible for these psychosocial interventions, but there is no evidence that deviations from intended interventions arose because of the trial context. Analyses were appropriate. 
   b. **Rationale**: Due to the nature of the interventions (supportive counselling, stress management, alcohol-focused couples therapy), participants and personnel were aware of the assigned intervention. However, there is no indication that non-protocol interventions or deviations arose because of the trial context. The analysis appears to have analyzed participants in the groups to which they were randomized, though some attrition occurred.

3. **Bias due to missing outcome data**: **Some concerns**
   a. **Judgement**: There was a moderate amount of missing outcome data (attrition), and it is unclear if missingness was related to the true value of the outcome.
   b. **Rationale**: The authors report, "Forty-seven participants completed treatment and 44 of these participants provided assessed data at 6-month follow-up. This represents an overall attrition of 22% of participants from the treatment phase of the study." The paper notes, "Many women who dropped out told us that the sessions they attended assisted them to leave a relationship in which they had been very unhappy for many years... Unfortunately, all the women who dropped out from treatment declined further assessment." Because leaving the relationship may profoundly impact stress and distress (the outcomes of interest), missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome, raising some concerns.

4. **Bias in measurement of the outcome**: **Some concerns**
   a. **Judgement**: The outcomes were self-reported, and participants were not blinded to the intervention, meaning knowledge of the intervention could have influenced the outcome assessment.
   b. **Rationale**: The outcomes (women’s burden and distress) were measured using the Relative Stress Scale (RSS) and the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ), which are participant-reported outcomes. As participants were aware of the intervention they received (due to the nature of the psychological therapies), their assessment of their own stress and psychological symptoms could have been influenced by this knowledge. 

5. **Bias in selective reporting**: **Some concerns**
   a. **Judgement**: There is no trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan provided to confirm whether the data were analyzed according to a pre-specified plan.
   b. **Rationale**: The article does not reference a clinical trial registry or an available protocol. Although multiple measures for burden/distress were used (RSS, GHQ) and reported clearly, the lack of a pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available leaves some concerns about whether the reported analyses were selected from multiple potential eligible analyses.
