# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Three-Dimensional Printing Models for Spinal Fracture Understanding

**Preliminary considerations:**
- Design: Individually-randomized parallel-group trial (3 arms: CT, 3D, 3Dp)
- Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention
- Outcome assessed: Performance (sum score of correct answers on 10 anatomical questions)
- Sources: Journal article; trial registration record

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, primarily due to lack of information about allocation concealment.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation:** The article states "Students were randomized into three groups using a computer program," which supports a "Probably yes" for random sequence generation.
- **Allocation concealment:** No information is provided about whether the allocation sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants until assignment. There is no mention of central randomization, sealed envelopes, or any other concealment mechanism. This warrants "No information," which given the question is expected to be reported, leads to concern.
- **Baseline imbalance:** Table 2 shows well-balanced age, sex, and school-age characteristics between groups ("indicating no significant sex-related differences" and no statistically significant differences in school age), suggesting no evidence of a problem with randomization from the baseline data itself.

Applying the algorithm: random sequence = Probably yes; concealment = No information; baseline imbalance = No → this maps to "Some concerns" (per Table 4: Y/PY sequence + NI concealment + N/PN/NI imbalance = Some concerns).

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- Participants (students) and personnel (teachers) were necessarily aware of which teaching module (CT, 3D, or 3Dp) they were assigned to, since this is an educational intervention that cannot be blinded ("A teacher provided a detailed interpretation of the fracture in each group").
- However, for this domain, the key question is whether deviations from intended intervention arose *because of the trial context* (e.g., recruitment/engagement effects) that are inconsistent with the protocol and affect the outcome. There is no evidence of any such deviations — students in each arm received exactly the intended teaching module (CT images, 3D images, or 3Dp models) as designed, with a teacher present in each group. There is no report of any contamination, co-intervention, or switching between arms.
- Analysis: All 120 randomized students were included and answered the outcome questions ("no one dropped out"), and results appear to be analyzed according to group assignment (an ITT-consistent analysis).
- Because there is no indication that deviations occurred due to trial context, and an appropriate analysis (all randomized participants analyzed in assigned groups) was used, this domain is judged as low risk of bias, despite lack of blinding, since lack of blinding alone is not sufficient to cause bias for this outcome (performance test administered immediately after the same-format lecture/exam) — awareness of group assignment would not plausibly distort the anatomical test-taking itself in a manner inconsistent with the protocol.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- The article explicitly states: "a total of 120 medical students (54.2% females) participated, and no one dropped out."
- Outcome data (sum scores of correct answers) were available for all 120 randomized participants, with complete breakdowns by group and sex reported in Table 3 (N = 22 and 18 for CT; 21 and 19 for 3D; 22 and 18 for 3Dp, matching the total randomized).
- Since outcome data were available for all randomized participants, there is no risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** The method of measuring the outcome (a written examination with 10 key anatomical questions, multiple-choice/binary format, Table 1) appears appropriate for assessing anatomical understanding.
- **Comparability of measurement across groups:** The same 10 questions and examination paper were used for all three groups ("each student answered 10 questions regarding spinal fractures... assessed by an examination paper"), so the method of measurement did not differ between arms.
- **Outcome assessors:** The outcome here is essentially an objective, "observer-reported outcome not involving judgement" for most items (multiple-choice/binary correct-or-incorrect answers), and grading of such questions would not be expected to require subjective judgement. However, there is no explicit statement about whether the person grading the exams was blinded to group assignment, and no information is given about whether exam scoring involved any judgement calls.
- Because the article does not report whether outcome assessors (graders) were blinded, and objectivity of grading, while likely high given the multiple-choice format, is not explicitly confirmed, and no information is provided to rule out awareness influencing scoring (e.g., a non-blinded scorer aware of group allocation, given each exam paper recorded the "randomization code" and likely group), there is some uncertainty. Since the correct-answer format leaves little room for subjective bias, but this is not explicitly confirmed and the exam papers were linked to a randomization code (implying assessors could potentially discern group), we judge this domain as "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk," reflecting the incomplete reporting of blinding of assessors/scorers.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registration (Chinese ethics approval, Binzhou Medical College 20140108) and the reported outcomes correspond directly with what is described in the Methods section: 10 anatomical questions and 4 evaluative questions, with all being reported in Table 1, Table 3, and the Results section.
- There is no evidence of selective reporting: all pre-specified questions/outcomes described in the methods (10 key anatomical questions comprising the "sum score of correct answers," plus the 4 evaluation questions) are fully reported with complete data (means, SDs) by group and by sex.
- There is no indication of multiple alternative analyses, scales, or time points from which the reported result could have been selectively chosen — a single composite sum score and single time-to-completion metric were pre-specified and reported for all groups.
- Although the study was seemingly not formally pre-registered with a detailed statistical analysis plan available for comparison (limiting certainty), there is no evidence of inconsistency, unusual composite construction, or omitted outcomes based on the information available, supporting a judgement of low risk rather than "some concerns."

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement for this Outcome (Performance): **Some concerns**

**Rationale:** No domain was judged to be at high risk of bias, but "Some concerns" were identified for two domains (bias arising from the randomization process, due to lack of information about allocation concealment; and measurement of the outcome, due to lack of information about blinding/objectivity of outcome assessors). Per RoB 2.0 guidance, this results in an overall judgement of "Some concerns" for the performance outcome.
