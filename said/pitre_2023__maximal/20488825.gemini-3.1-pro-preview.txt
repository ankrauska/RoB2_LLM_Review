**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The allocation sequence was random, allocation was adequately concealed, and there were no significant baseline imbalances suggesting a problem with the randomization process.
b. Rationale: The trial publication states that "A computer-based blocking method (four blocks) was used for the randomization" and "separate physicians were assigned to randomly allocate patients." Allocation concealment was maintained as "The patients were unaware of their group assignment and all medications were given in similar packages." Furthermore, Table 1 shows no significant differences between groups in baseline characteristics such as age, sex, EDSS, and previous relapse number.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Participants and personnel were unaware of the assigned interventions, and there were no indications of deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context.
b. Rationale: The study was a "double-blind randomized controlled trial" using a placebo comparator. The publication explicitly notes, "The patients were unaware of their group assignment and all medications were given in similar packages." Neurologists evaluating the patients and radiologists were also blinded to the treatment groups. Therefore, deviations influenced by knowledge of the intervention are unlikely.

**3. missing outcome data: some concerns**
a. Judgement: Outcome data were not available for all randomized participants, and there are some concerns that missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome, though statistical methods (LOCF) were used to include some dropouts.
b. Rationale: The trial randomized 85 patients, but "Fourteen patients withdrew from the study." Five patients withdrew in the first two months and were entirely excluded. Nine other patients dropped out later, six of whom specifically withdrew due to having two relapses. Because some participants withdrew directly due to experiencing exacerbations (the outcome of interest), missingness is related to the outcome. Although the authors state that they used "last observation carried forward (LOCF) methods" for the nine later dropouts, excluding the five early dropouts and using LOCF (which may underestimate total events in a 1-year period for those who withdrew early) raises some concerns for bias due to missing outcome data. 

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The method of measuring the outcome (relapse/attack number) was appropriate, comparable across groups, and assessors were blinded to the intervention.
b. Rationale: A relapse was consistently defined ("any new or worsening neurological deficit in the absence of high body temperature that lasted for more than 24 h after 1-month stability"). Furthermore, the neurologists assessing relapses and the patients themselves were blinded to the treatment allocation, minimizing the risk of bias in outcome ascertainment.

**5. selective reporting: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The reported results correspond to the primary outcome pre-specified in the trial registration, with no evidence of selection from multiple analyses or outcome measurements.
b. Rationale: The trial registration (NCT00668343) pre-specifies the primary outcome as "attack number" with a timeframe of "1 year". The published manuscript reports this exact outcome ("total relapse rate" over a 1-year follow-up) as intended. There is no evidence that the results were selected from multiple eligible analyses or measurements based on statistical significance.
