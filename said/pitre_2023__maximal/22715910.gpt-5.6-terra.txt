**Outcome assessed:** Inhaler use technique, assessed using a standardized nine-step observational checklist at the 3-month post-intervention follow-up. The assessment concerns the effect of assignment to the educational-material groups at baseline (rather than adherence to the assigned material).

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The report states that eligible participants were “**randomly assigned**” to three video groups or a pictorial-pamphlet group, but it does not describe how the random allocation sequence was generated. There is also no description of allocation concealment—for example, central randomization, an independently administered allocation system, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes.

   The allocated group sizes were reasonably similar (community video, n=22; knowledge video, n=21; both videos, n=20; pamphlet, n=22), which does not suggest an obvious failure of randomization. The authors also state that “**initial pretest analyses showed no statistically significant differences among the four study groups**” for outcomes of interest. However, full baseline characteristics by randomized group, including baseline inhaler-technique scores and key prognostic variables, are not presented. Thus, there is no affirmative evidence of a serious randomization failure, but both sequence generation and allocation concealment remain insufficiently reported.

   Under RoB 2, lack of information on concealment, despite no clear problematic baseline imbalance, leads to **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Participants necessarily knew which educational format they received: they watched one or both videos or read the pamphlet. Personnel delivering the intervention and conducting the interviews were also likely aware of the assigned format. Thus, blinding was not feasible and was not maintained.

   However, for the effect of **assignment** to intervention, lack of blinding alone does not establish bias. RoB 2 requires evidence that deviations inconsistent with the protocol arose because of the trial context, affected inhaler technique, and were imbalanced between groups. No such deviations are reported. All groups received an educational intervention and underwent comparable interview and follow-up procedures. The report describes the intervention as follows: “**the patient watched the videos or read the pamphlet based on the study group they belonged to**.”

   The analysis appears to retain participants according to their assigned study group. Although seven of 92 enrolled participants did not complete the study, exclusions attributable to missing outcome data are addressed primarily in the missing-outcome-data domain. There is no indication that participants were analysed according to an intervention other than that to which they were randomized, nor that a naïve per-protocol or as-treated analysis was used.

   Therefore, despite awareness of allocation, there is no reported evidence that trial-context deviations from the intended intervention materially biased the inhaler-technique comparison.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Of the 92 participants enrolled in the intervention, 85 completed the study. The participant flow diagram reports that “**3 were excluded and 4 discontinued intervention**.” Thus, outcome data were not available for all randomized participants. Moreover, the degrees of freedom in the reported inhaler analyses (e.g., df = 1 and 71; df = 1 and 76) suggest that some analyses may have involved fewer than the 85 completers.

   The amount of missing data is not extreme, but it is greater than the level at which it can safely be assumed that missing outcomes could not have affected a continuous outcome such as the inhaler-technique score. The report does not provide:

   * the number with observed inhaler-technique outcomes in each randomized group;
   * group-specific reasons for exclusion or discontinuation;
   * an analysis accounting for missing data;
   * sensitivity analyses examining whether missing data could change the result.

   Missingness could plausibly relate to true inhaler technique or engagement with asthma self-management; for example, participants with poorer skills, lower motivation, or difficulty attending follow-up could be more likely not to provide follow-up measurements. However, the available report does not establish that missingness was likely related to outcome values, or that reasons for missingness differed between trial arms. Accordingly, this is best judged as **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The method of assessing inhaler technique was appropriate in principle. The study used a “**standardized nine-step observational checklist**” for different inhaler devices, with scores from 0 to 9. This is a direct and relevant method for measuring the outcome. The same checklist appears to have been used across intervention groups and time points.

   The report also states that inhaler technique during face-to-face interviews was assessed by “**two observers (the interviewer and the research coordinator)**,” which may improve reliability. In addition, the community facilitators were described as “**not aware of the study hypothesis**.”

   Nevertheless, outcome assessors were not explicitly reported to be blinded to intervention assignment. Because the outcome was assessed in face-to-face sessions shortly after participants had watched a video or read a pamphlet, the interviewer and/or coordinator could likely know the participant’s assigned educational format. Assessment using a stepwise checklist reduces, but does not eliminate, judgement in scoring inhaler use. Knowledge of allocation could also affect participant performance during an observed assessment through expectancy or social-desirability effects.

   There is no evidence that outcome measurement methods differed systematically between groups, and no clear evidence that assessors’ knowledge actually influenced ratings. Therefore, the concern is not sufficient for high risk of bias, but the lack of clear assessor blinding supports **some concerns**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The trial was registered as NCT01474928, but the registry record indicates that recruitment began in August 2009 whereas the study was first submitted to ClinicalTrials.gov in November 2011. Therefore, the registration was retrospective and cannot demonstrate that the reported inhaler-technique analysis was prespecified before unblinded outcome data became available.

   In addition, the registry’s primary outcome is “**medication adherence**,” while inhaler-use technique is not specifically listed as a primary or secondary registered outcome. The registry refers broadly to evaluating educational materials and asthma self-management, but it does not prespecify:

   * the nine-step inhaler checklist;
   * the inhaler devices or method for combining results among participants using more than one inhaler;
   * the target follow-up time point for the inhaler-technique result;
   * the exact statistical analysis;
   * planned covariate adjustment or handling of missing data.

   The article states that inhaler technique was measured at baseline, immediate post-test, and 3-month follow-up, with a later telephone follow-up for selected self-management practices. However, the article summarizes “**pretest and 3-month post-intervention follow-up data**,” rather than fully reporting results for all measured time points. It also reports multiple related outcomes and subgroup analyses.

   No statistical analysis plan finalized before outcome data were available was identified. There is insufficient evidence to conclude that the reported inhaler-technique result was definitely selected because it was favourable, so a high-risk judgement is not warranted. However, the absence of a prospectively finalized detailed analysis plan, retrospective registration, and multiple possible time points and analyses justify **some concerns**.
