1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that, following the pre-test, participants “were randomly assigned to three experimental groups.” However, it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers) or whether allocation was concealed until participants had been enrolled.

   * Allocation sequence random: **No information.** The statement “randomly assigned” alone does not establish that a genuinely random sequence was used.
   * Allocation concealed: **No information.** There is no information on central randomization, sealed opaque envelopes, or another concealment mechanism.
   * Baseline imbalance: **No information.** No baseline characteristics or pre-test checklist scores by randomized group are reported in a way that permits assessment of whether there were important imbalances.

   In accordance with RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment, in the absence of evidence of baseline imbalance, leads to **some concerns**, rather than high risk, for this domain.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of **assignment** to the practice schedule at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and the faculty member delivering feedback were necessarily likely aware of the allocated practice schedule: functional-order practice, random practice, or blocked practice. Nevertheless, awareness alone does not imply bias for the effect of assignment. For this RoB 2 estimand, the relevant question is whether deviations from the assigned schedule arose because of the trial context and were likely to affect the outcome.

   The intervention procedures appear standardized and implemented as intended:

   * The functional-order group practiced the whole task in “three 20-minute practice sessions.”
   * The random-practice group practiced the component skills “in a random order during three 20-minute practice sessions.”
   * The blocked-practice group practiced skills in “five 12-minute practice sessions,” with three consecutive trials of each skill.
   * “All participants in all groups practice[d] each skill 3 times for 2 minutes and 10 seconds.”
   * Feedback provision was standardized: “an orthopedic faculty member circulated within the group systematically to provide feedback to the trainees.”

   There is no report of crossover between practice schedules, unequal additional training, differential feedback, or other departures from the protocol that arose because participants or personnel knew the allocated schedule. The outcome was analysed according to randomized group using mixed ANOVA models with group as the between-participant factor. This is consistent with estimating the effect of assignment to the allocated practice schedule.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:** The report identifies 28 enrolled medical students and describes outcome assessment at pre-test, immediate post-test, and delayed post-test for the participants. It does not report withdrawals, exclusions, missing checklist assessments, or unequal numbers analysed between groups.

   The report states that “the participants’ performances were assessed 3 times” and that individual pre- to post-test difference scores were calculated “for each of the measures.” The reported group means and standard errors for change in checklist score are presented without indication that they are based on an incomplete subset of randomized participants.

   Although a participant flow diagram and group-specific denominators would have provided stronger assurance, there is no indication that outcome data were missing or that missingness was related to participants’ true checklist performance. The available information therefore supports a judgement of low risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was assessed using an operation-specific checklist developed by expert orthopedic surgeons. The method is relevant and plausible for assessing performance of the bone-plating procedure:

   > “A panel of 3 orthopedic surgeons identified the actions necessary to perform the operative task effectively and thus selected these items for the checklist.”

   The checklist was used during the same standardized full-task assessments at pre-test and both post-tests for every group. There is no evidence that the checklist, scoring criteria, or assessment time points differed by randomized group.

   However, two issues leave some concern:

   * The report does not state whether the two experienced orthopedic-surgeon outcome assessors were blinded to the participants’ allocated practice schedule.
   * Checklist scoring of operative performance, even when structured, entails at least some assessor judgement. Knowledge that a participant had received whole-task, random, or blocked practice could potentially influence scoring, particularly given the investigators’ hypothesis that practice schedule would affect learning.

   The article states only that:

   > “Two experienced orthopedic surgeons used 4 separate methods of evaluation during the pre-test and the 2 post-tests.”

   It does not state that videos were coded, that assessors were independent of training delivery, that participants were assessed in a manner concealing their group, or that inter-rater procedures prevented knowledge of allocation. Because influence of assessor awareness is possible but not clearly demonstrated to be likely, **some concerns** is appropriate rather than high risk.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The target result—change in operation-specific checklist scores—is reported, including the group comparison and the absence of a difference between immediate and delayed post-tests:

   > “The change in the operation-specific checklist scores showed a main effect for group (P < .05).”

   The report also gives group-specific mean change scores and standard errors:

   > “The Functional-order-practice group improved their scores significantly more than did the Blocked-practice group (M = 5.72, SE = .83; M = 2.22, SE = .84, respectively)… The Random-practice group was not different from the other 2 groups (M = 4.55, SE = .79).”

   Nevertheless, no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the material provided, and the publication does not identify a prespecified primary outcome, primary analysis, or a protocol finalized before outcome data were examined. The trial used several outcome measures—operation-specific checklist, final-product analysis, global rating score, drilling time during tests, and drilling time during acquisition—and assessed outcomes at more than one post-practice time point. The choice to emphasize change scores and a mixed ANOVA model might therefore have involved analytic choices among multiple potentially eligible approaches (for example, final scores versus change scores, separate immediate and delayed analyses, or differing approaches to repeated measurements).

   There is no direct evidence that the checklist result was selected because it was favourable, and the authors report both statistically significant and non-significant outcomes. Thus, high risk is not justified. However, in the absence of a dated prespecified analysis plan or sufficiently detailed trial-registration information demonstrating that this specific outcome measurement and analysis were prespecified, the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is **some concerns**.
