Outcome assessed: **reoperation rate within 6 months of randomization**, including repeat wide local excision, further mastectomy, and pathologically avoidable initial mastectomy. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment** to MRI plus triple assessment versus triple assessment alone.

1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation process used a centrally administered telephone system and a minimisation algorithm with a random element. These features provide adequate sequence generation and allocation concealment.

   * The trial reports: “Patients were randomised … on a 1:1 basis, with a minimisation algorithm incorporating a random element.”
   * Allocation was administered through “the University of Leeds Clinical Trials Research Unit’s automated 24-hour telephone randomisation system,” by the research nurse or consultant surgeon. This is an appropriate central allocation mechanism, preventing recruiters from knowing the next allocation before enrolment.
   * The minimisation variables were clinically relevant: surgeon, age, and breast density.
   * Baseline characteristics were closely balanced. For example, the proportions aged under 50 years were identical (23% in each group), median age was 57 years in both groups, and breast-density categories were very similar. The authors state that “Characteristics were well balanced between the groups.”
   * There is no indication of a meaningful baseline imbalance, unexpectedly unequal group sizes, or another feature suggesting a problem with randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:** This was an open-label diagnostic-imaging trial, so participants and clinical personnel were necessarily aware of allocation. However, the primary analysis was intention-to-treat, including all randomized participants in their assigned groups. There is no evidence that deviations arising specifically because of the trial context were both unbalanced and likely to have materially affected reoperation rates.

   * The study was explicitly an “open-label, parallel group trial”; therefore, participants, surgeons, radiologists, and other personnel were aware of whether MRI had been assigned.
   * Some participants did not receive their allocated imaging strategy:
     * In the MRI group, 761/816 received MRI; reasons for not receiving MRI included technical failure, MRI unavailable before surgery, refusal, non-attendance, unsuitability, claustrophobia, and staffing issues.
     * In the no-MRI group, 9/807 received MRI, mainly at patient or clinician request.
   * These departures were relatively limited and largely reflected practical clinical circumstances, patient choice, technical limitations, or access problems. The report does not indicate that they were caused by differential trial-context behaviour intended to undermine the randomized comparison.
   * Importantly, the primary endpoint was analysed according to assignment: “Analysis was by intention to treat.” The report further states that all 816 MRI-assigned and all 807 no-MRI-assigned participants were analysed for the primary endpoint.
   * Changes in surgical management prompted by MRI findings are part of the intended effect of being assigned to the MRI strategy, rather than deviations from it. For example, MRI-related identification of additional disease and consequent changes from wide local excision to mastectomy are mechanisms through which the diagnostic strategy might affect reoperation.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:** Primary-outcome data were available for nearly all randomized participants. Missing or unavailable primary-endpoint data were very few relative to the sample size and, especially, to the 309 primary-endpoint events. It is implausible that the small amount of missing information could materially alter the estimated effect.

   * The primary analysis included 816/816 participants assigned to MRI and 807/807 assigned to no MRI.
   * The flow diagram identifies very small numbers with unavailable primary-endpoint information:
     * MRI: 4 participants lost to follow-up or with unavailable primary-endpoint data.
     * No MRI: 6 participants lost to follow-up or with unavailable primary-endpoint data.
   * The article states: “Patients lost to follow-up [were] regarded as not having a primary endpoint event.”
   * Although classifying missing participants as having no event is an assumption, there were only about ten such participants in total (<1% of the randomized population), compared with 309 observed primary-endpoint events. Even extreme alternative assumptions about these few individuals would be very unlikely to have materially changed the odds ratio of 0.96.
   * The imbalance in missing data was minimal (approximately 0.5% versus 0.7%), and no evidence is reported that missingness was related to the true reoperation outcome differently between groups.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Reoperation is a largely objective, routinely documented surgical event, measured over the same prespecified 6-month period in both groups. Although the trial was open-label and surgeons knew imaging allocation, there is little scope for knowledge of allocation to bias ascertainment of whether a repeat operation occurred.

   * The endpoint was explicitly defined as “the proportion of patients undergoing a repeat operation—either further wide local excision or mastectomy within 6 months of random assignment, or a pathologically avoidable mastectomy at initial operation.”
   * Repeat surgery and mastectomy are concrete clinical events that can be ascertained from surgical and hospital records. The same outcome definition and 6-month assessment window were applied to both groups.
   * Histopathological assessment was conducted according to NHS Breast Screening Programme/Royal College of Pathologists guidance. The trial also describes defined criteria for “pathologically avoidable mastectomy.”
   * An independent Data Monitoring and Ethics Committee reviewed imaging and histopathology for initial mastectomies to identify potentially avoidable mastectomies. This adds assurance for the less straightforward component of the composite endpoint.
   * Surgeons were aware of MRI allocation, and surgical decisions may legitimately have been informed by MRI results. However, this is an intended consequence of assignment to a preoperative MRI strategy—not differential measurement of the factual occurrence of reoperation. There is no evidence that the occurrence or recording of repeat operations was assessed differently between groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article and registry identification indicate that reoperation rate was the primary endpoint, and the publication reports a clear intention-to-treat comparison consistent with the stated outcome. However, there is insufficient evidence of a fully specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available; therefore, low risk cannot be confirmed under RoB 2.

   * The article identifies the trial registration number: “This study is registered, ISRCTN number 57474502.”
   * The publication clearly identifies reoperation rate as the primary clinical outcome and reports it for both randomized groups: 153/816 (19%) in the MRI group and 156/807 (19%) in the no-MRI group; adjusted OR 0.96 (95% CI 0.75–1.24).
   * The methods pre-specify the primary endpoint definition, including repeat surgery within 6 months and pathologically avoidable initial mastectomy. They also specify the primary analytic approach: logistic regression adjusting for minimisation factors, with analysis “by intention to treat.”
   * The report distinguishes “prespecified exploratory subgroup analyses” from “ad-hoc exploratory analyses,” which is relatively transparent.
   * Nevertheless, the available information does not provide a dated, sufficiently detailed statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the precise analysis of reoperation was finalized before access to unblinded trial data. There is also no complete pre-unblinding documentation allowing confirmation that no alternative eligible outcome definitions, time points, or analyses were selectively reported.
   * There is no direct evidence that the reported reoperation result was selected because of its magnitude, direction, or statistical significance. Thus, the appropriate judgement is **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.
