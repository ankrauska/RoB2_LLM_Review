**Outcome assessed:** delayed diagnostic test performance (including the reported condition-by-time interaction across the four randomized instructional conditions: science-based causal explanations [SC], basic science then clinical features [BC], clinical features then basic science [CB], and clinical-feature list [FL]). The assessment concerns the **effect of assignment** to the instructional condition at baseline.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants “were randomly allocated to one of four study conditions,” but does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated. Therefore, it cannot be established that a genuinely random sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers) was used.

   Allocation concealment is also not described. In particular, there is no indication that allocation was implemented centrally, by an independent party, or using sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. The study was conducted individually using a computer program, which may make concealment plausible, but this is not reported sufficiently to support a low-risk judgement.

   The initially reported group sizes were reasonably similar (SC = 26; BC = 25; CB = 25; FL = 25), which does not itself suggest manipulation or a serious randomization failure. The report also provides some post-randomization professional-group information (e.g., two physician assistants per group), but no full baseline characteristics table or baseline pretest was reported. The authors acknowledge that they “cannot conclusively rule out an imbalance between the conditions or professional groups.”

   Thus, there is no direct evidence of a flawed randomization process, but the absence of information on both sequence generation and allocation concealment raises **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew the instructional material to which they had been assigned. The interventions consisted of visibly different educational materials, so participant blinding was not feasible. Personnel administering the computer-based study may also have been aware of condition, although this is not explicitly stated.

   However, there is no evidence that important deviations from the assigned instructional condition arose because of the trial context. The intervention appears to have been delivered in a standardized individual computer-based format:

   > “Participants completed this study individually using a custom-designed computer program.”

   Participants were instructed to read assigned explanations and underwent the same testing structure. There is no reported evidence of differential cointerventions, contamination between conditions, or systematic failures in delivery that would have affected delayed diagnostic performance. Therefore, lack of blinding alone does not establish bias for the effect of assignment.

   There are, nevertheless, concerns regarding the analysis population. Five participants who did not return for delayed testing were excluded, and two additional participants were excluded after inspection of their delayed diagnostic-test performance because they had spent less than five minutes on the test and scored extremely poorly:

   > “One participant from the BC and CB group each spent less than five minutes on the delayed diagnostic test. Upon inspection, these participants’ performance was three standard deviations below the mean performance for their respective groups… This left a final sample size of 94 participants.”

   Excluding participants post-randomization because of behavior and extremely low observed outcome performance is not fully consistent with an analysis of the effect of assignment to intervention. The exclusion involved only two participants, so its potential impact may not be substantial by itself; nevertheless, outcome-related exclusions create some concern. The reported analysis is consequently closer to a modified intention-to-treat/complete-case analysis than a full intention-to-treat analysis.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:** Delayed diagnostic-performance data were not available for all randomized participants. Initially, 101 participants were randomized (SC = 26, BC = 25, CB = 25, FL = 25), whereas the final analysis included 94 participants. The report states:

   > “Five enrolled participants failed to complete the delayed session of the study … and were excluded from the analysis.”

   Attrition was not balanced according to the narrative: three non-completers were from CB and two from BC, with none reported from FL or SC. The delayed-test flow figure also indicates changes in group sizes, although the figure and text are not fully internally consistent. Such differential attrition is important because the delayed diagnostic outcome is the outcome under assessment.

   No reasons for failure to complete the delayed session are reported. Therefore, it is plausible that return for delayed testing was related to participants’ engagement, performance, motivation, or other characteristics related to the true delayed diagnostic score. The uneven distribution of missing delayed-session data across groups further increases concern that missingness may be related to both assigned condition and outcome.

   In addition, two participants with observed very low delayed-test scores were excluded from the analysis. Although these exclusions are principally considered under deviations from intended intervention rather than missing-data bias, they reinforce concern about the robustness of the delayed-performance result.

   The study does not report sensitivity analyses, imputation, inverse-probability weighting, or other analyses demonstrating that the interaction result was robust to plausible assumptions about missing delayed outcomes. Given differential missingness, absent reasons for dropout, and no sensitivity analysis, missingness was likely capable of biasing the delayed diagnostic-performance findings.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Delayed diagnostic performance was measured using a structured diagnostic test comprising clinical vignettes with four possible diagnoses:

   > “The diagnostic test consisted of 12 clinical vignettes for which students had to identify the correct diagnosis from a list of four possible disorders.”

   This is an appropriate and objective method for measuring performance on the experimental diagnostic-reasoning task. Scores are based on correct answers rather than subjective assessor judgement. The authors also report that the test materials were developed by a clinician, independently verified by another clinical expert, used in prior studies, and had demonstrated adequate measurement properties for the intended experimental purpose:

   > “These assessments have previously demonstrated adequate measurement properties for the purposes of studying medical students’ learning and diagnostic reasoning processes.”

   Participants were aware of their instructional condition, but the outcome was a scored multiple-choice diagnostic test. Awareness of instructional assignment would not reasonably allow participants or assessors to manipulate whether a selected diagnosis was correct. There is no indication that scoring methods, answer keys, testing procedures, or diagnostic-vignette ascertainment differed between conditions. Alternate test versions were counterbalanced between immediate and delayed testing.

   Although the test may have limited generalizability to routine clinical performance, this concerns applicability rather than bias in measurement of the stated trial outcome. The measurement domain is therefore at **low risk of bias**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies diagnostic-test performance as the primary analysis:

   > “The primary analysis was performance on the diagnostic test using a mixed, repeated-measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with group … as the between-subjects factor and test … as the repeated factor.”

   It also reports results for the memory test, time measures, immediate and delayed diagnostic performance, the time effect, and the condition-by-time interaction. The reported diagnostic-analysis result is compatible with the study’s stated objectives and methods.

   However, the article does not report a prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan, and the supplied article text does not provide enough information from a registration record to verify that the exact delayed diagnostic-performance analysis—including the selected covariates, interaction model, exclusions, post hoc least-significant-difference comparisons, and choice of reported time-point contrasts—was prespecified before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The report states that:

   > “Appropriate covariates chosen were based on the correlations between performances on the diagnostic and memory test as well as between performance and time per vignette.”

   This wording suggests that covariate selection may have been informed by observed data rather than a fully prespecified analysis plan. Further, the use of post hoc comparisons after finding an interaction introduces multiple analytic options. There is no clear evidence that the reported result was selected from multiple analyses because it was favorable, but the absence of a dated, detailed prespecified analysis plan means that selective analysis reporting cannot be excluded.

   Therefore, this domain is judged as raising **some concerns**, rather than high risk, because the article is broadly consistent with its stated primary outcome and no direct evidence of result-driven selective reporting is presented.
