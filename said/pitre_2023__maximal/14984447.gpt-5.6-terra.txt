**Effect of interest:** effect of assignment at baseline to free glucometer test strips (no-cost group) versus having to purchase further strips (control group), irrespective of subsequent adherence.  
**Outcomes assessed:** SBGM frequency (glucometer-memory downloaded tests/day) and HbA1c over 12 months.

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

### a. Judgement
There are some concerns because the report states that patients were “randomly assigned” after income stratification, but does not describe how the random sequence was generated or how allocation was concealed before enrolment/assignment. Baseline characteristics appear well balanced and do not themselves suggest a failure of randomization.

### b. Rationale
- The study reports that patients “were stratified in three categories of income … and they were **randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion** to two groups of 31 patients each.”
- However, it gives no information on:
  - the method used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table); or
  - allocation concealment (e.g., central randomization or appropriately prepared sequential opaque sealed envelopes).
- Under RoB 2, a statement that allocation was “random” alone is insufficient to establish that the sequence was random, and lack of information about allocation concealment ordinarily gives **some concerns** where baseline data do not indicate a problem.
- Baseline Table 1 shows broadly comparable groups for the main prognostic characteristics and both outcome-related baseline measures:
  - HbA1c: 9.1% in no-cost versus 9.2% in control;
  - weekly SBGM frequency: 8.4 versus 8.5;
  - diabetes type: 17:14 versus 18:13 (type 1:type 2);
  - insurance: 13:18 in both groups.
- The equal group sizes (31 per group) and lack of conspicuous baseline imbalance do not suggest that randomization was compromised. Nevertheless, they cannot compensate for absent reporting of allocation concealment.

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **Low risk of bias**

### a. Judgement
For the effect of assignment to free strips versus usual purchase of strips, the risk of bias due to deviations from intended intervention is low. Participants and personnel were likely aware of the practical intervention received, but there is no evidence that trial-context deviations from the protocol occurred, affected SBGM frequency or HbA1c, and differed between groups. The primary analysis was described as intention-to-treat, with last observation carried forward for missing data.

### b. Rationale
- The intervention could not realistically remain fully blinded throughout follow-up:
  - the no-cost group received “a supply of reagent strips, **100 strips per month**”;
  - the control group received a one-off package of 50 strips and “were instructed to **purchase additional reagent strips as needed**.”
  Participants would therefore be expected to know, or infer, their assigned strategy.
- The study attempted to minimize additional intervention differences:
  - both groups received a glucometer;
  - participants were told to perform SBGM as previously advised through the Diabetes Education Centre;
  - “the frequency of visits to their physician was not altered”;
  - the research nurse “did not provide any feedback” concerning glucometer results, random glucose, or HbA1c.
- The report does not identify non-protocol cointerventions, differential clinical management, or failures to deliver the allocated strip-access strategy that arose because of the trial context.
- Changes in insulin dose occurred, particularly in the control group. However, insulin adjustment is part of routine diabetes management and is not shown to have been a non-protocol, trial-context deviation introduced differentially by study personnel. It therefore does not establish bias in the effect of assignment.
- The authors state: “**Efficacy data from the intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward, were analysed** by General Linear Model with baseline values as co-variate.”
- For this RoB 2 domain, an ITT or modified ITT analysis that preserves randomized group assignment is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment. The suitability and potential bias of last-observation-carried-forward in the presence of dropout is considered under missing outcome data, rather than in this domain.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

### a. Judgement
There is high risk of bias due to missing outcome data for both SBGM frequency and HbA1c. Attrition was substantial and strongly differential between groups, particularly in the control group. The reasons for missing outcome data were not adequately reported by randomized group, and last-observation-carried-forward does not establish that the results are robust to missing data.

### b. Rationale
- Outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants at 12 months:
  - no-cost group: 25/31 remained, corresponding to 19% dropout;
  - control group: 16/31 remained, corresponding to 48% dropout.
- The report explicitly states: “At the end of the study, there were **25 patients remaining in the NC group and 16 patients in the C group**, i.e. a dropout rate of **19%** in the NC group and **48%** in the C group.”
- This is a large absolute difference in attrition (29 percentage points). Such differential missingness is especially important because the intervention itself concerns affordability of self-monitoring materials, and lack of strips or dissatisfaction with the assigned strategy could plausibly be related to both continued participation and glycaemic control/SBGM behaviour.
- The authors themselves acknowledge a plausible outcome-related mechanism: “We cannot exclude the possibility that patients with intrinsically poorer glycaemic control became discouraged and therefore did less testing or dropped out of the study altogether.”
- Although they argue this “seems unlikely,” they do not provide participant-level dropout reasons by group, comparative outcome data immediately before dropout, or sensitivity analyses demonstrating robustness under plausible missing-data assumptions.
- The stated analytic approach was “intention-to-treat … using the **last observation carried forward**.” LOCF does not reliably address bias where missingness may depend on the true, unobserved SBGM frequency or HbA1c, and it may distort longitudinal changes and underestimate uncertainty.
- The report presents a completer analysis as well: “In subjects that completed the entire duration of the study, a repeated measure analysis of variance was also used.” Given the much greater loss in the control group, completer analyses are particularly vulnerable to selection bias.
- Thus, missingness could—and, given the differential attrition and plausible relation to diabetes self-management and glycaemic outcomes, is likely to—depend on the true values of SBGM frequency and HbA1c.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

### a. Judgement
Measurement bias is at low risk for both outcomes. SBGM frequency was obtained objectively from glucometer memory and HbA1c was measured using a specified analyser. The same measurement methods and schedule were used in both groups, and knowledge of assignment was unlikely to influence these measurements materially.

### b. Rationale
**SBGM frequency**
- SBGM frequency was measured as “the frequency of SBGM … determined by **downloading the glucometer memory**.”
- This is more objective and less vulnerable to reporting bias than participant self-report of testing frequency.
- Both groups were supplied with the same glucometer model, and glucometer memory was downloaded at the same scheduled visits.
- Although the research nurse may have known assignment, the recorded number of tests in device memory is not plausibly susceptible to assessor judgement or differential interpretation.

**HbA1c**
- HbA1c is an objective biochemical outcome, measured using the same method in both groups: “HbA1c was measured using the **DCA 2000 Analyser**.”
- Measurements were made initially and at each subsequent 2-monthly visit in both groups.
- No evidence suggests that assay methods, timing, thresholds, or laboratory procedures differed by intervention group.
- Even if laboratory personnel or the nurse were aware of group assignment, knowledge of allocation would not plausibly influence an instrument-generated HbA1c result in a way likely to cause differential measurement error.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

### a. Judgement
There are some concerns about selective reporting for both SBGM frequency and HbA1c. The report identifies these outcomes and gives a general description of analyses, but there is no available prospective, detailed, date-stamped statistical analysis plan showing that the reported outcome measurements, time points, handling of missing data, and analyses were all specified before unblinded outcome data became available.

### b. Rationale
- The publication states that the measured endpoints included “the frequency of SBGM (mean number of tests per day) … the average daily blood glucose over the past 2 months, and the HbA1c.”
- It reports multiple time points over 12 months and multiple analytic approaches:
  - a General Linear Model including group, time, and group-by-time interaction;
  - an ITT analysis using LOCF;
  - a repeated-measures ANOVA among participants completing the full study;
  - analyses of correlations between HbA1c and SBGM frequency.
- The publication emphasizes selected time-specific findings, for example:
  - SBGM frequency was higher during “the first 4 months”;
  - HbA1c differences were “most apparent at the 6th month of the study.”
  These statements do not prove selective reporting, but they demonstrate opportunities to select among several follow-up occasions and analyses.
- No full prespecified statistical analysis plan is reported, and the supplied materials do not provide sufficient information to establish that the reported analyses—including LOCF, covariate adjustment, repeated-measures analysis, or selected time-point claims—were finalized before unblinded data were available.
- There is no clear evidence that results were selected from multiple measurements or analyses because of their statistical significance; therefore, a **high-risk** judgement is not warranted. However, without a sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan, RoB 2 indicates **some concerns**.

---

### Overall implication
For both SBGM frequency and HbA1c, the study has **high overall risk of bias**, driven by the high risk of bias due to missing outcome data.
