1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants “were randomly selected for insertion of the 0.014 or 0.016 inch wires,” which supports that allocation was intended to be random. However, it does not describe how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence or random-number table) and, importantly, does not report allocation concealment. There is no indication that a central service, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes were used. Therefore, personnel enrolling patients could potentially have foreseen assignments.

   Baseline characteristics relevant to pain prognosis were not presented by randomized group. The reported group sizes (56 versus 53) are reasonably close and do not themselves suggest an important failure of randomization, but the absence of baseline-comparability data limits assessment.

   * Supporting text: “The patients were randomly selected for insertion of the 0.014 or 0.016 inch wires.”
   * The publication does not report sequence-generation methods, allocation concealment, or a baseline table by intervention group.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   *Effect of assignment to intervention at baseline*

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Blinding of participants and clinical personnel is not reported, so they were probably aware of the wire used. However, for the effect of assignment, lack of blinding creates bias only if deviations from the protocol arose because of the trial context, affected the outcome, and were imbalanced between groups.

   The interventions appear to have been implemented as assigned: “Following placement of the archwires they were ligated to all teeth.” The study also reports that no extra-oral appliances, palatal arches, or quad-helix appliances were used during the experimental period. Although participants could use pain medication when they considered it necessary, analgesic use was an observed consequence of treatment rather than evidence of a non-protocol cointervention caused by the trial context. There is no evidence that participants crossed over between wire groups, that the assigned wires were replaced, or that trial-specific deviations affecting initial pain occurred differentially between groups.

   The analysis compared participants according to their assigned wire group, and all 109 participants completed and returned the questionnaire. Thus, there is no indication of an as-treated or per-protocol analysis that would be inappropriate for the effect of assignment.

   * Supporting text: “Following placement of the archwires they were ligated to all teeth.”
   * “No extra-oral appliances, palatal arches or quad-helix appliances were used during the experimental period.”
   * “The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned at the following appointment.”

3. **Bias due to missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:** Data on initial pain appear available for all randomized participants. The authors state that all 109 participants completed and returned the questionnaire. Table 1 accounts for the initial-pain responses: among the 0.014-inch group, 50 reported an onset time and six reported no pain (56 total); among the 0.016-inch group, 47 reported an onset time and six reported no pain (53 total). Thus, the initial-pain result appears to include the complete randomized sample, with “no pain” explicitly represented rather than treated as missing data.

   There is consequently no evidence of differential or outcome-dependent missingness for the initial-pain outcome.

   * Supporting text: “The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned at the following appointment.”
   * For initial pain, “No pain was reported by 10.7 per cent (six patients) in the 0.014 inch group and 11.4 per cent (six patients) in the 0.016 inch group.”

4. **Bias in measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Initial pain was measured using a patient-completed questionnaire. A patient-reported measure is appropriate in principle for pain, and the same questionnaire was apparently used in both intervention groups. The question directly asked when pain was first perceived after archwire insertion, which is relevant to the outcome.

   However, pain is a subjective, participant-reported outcome, and participants were likely aware that they had received orthodontic treatment and may have known, or inferred, aspects of the wire intervention. The publication does not report blinding or masking of participants, nor does it demonstrate that participants were unaware of wire size. Therefore, knowledge of the intervention could potentially affect reported pain onset. The report does not provide a strong basis for concluding that such knowledge was likely to have influenced reporting—particularly because the two initial NiTi wire sizes are similar and both groups received fixed appliances—so the concern is not judged high.

   There is also limited information about the interval between wire placement and questionnaire return. Since the initial-pain time was apparently recalled at the subsequent appointment, recall error is possible, although this would likely affect both groups similarly.

   * Supporting text: “Question 1 asked the time at which pain was first perceived after archwire insertion.”
   * “Following archwire insertion the patients were given previously prepared questionnaires and instructed on how they should be completed.”
   * No blinding or masking procedures are reported.

5. **Bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Initial pain was clearly one of the stated study aims, and the paper reports the distribution of the time to initial pain for both wire groups. This reduces concern that the outcome was omitted entirely. The publication also reports the no-pain category and reports that there was no statistically significant difference between groups, which does not suggest obvious selective emphasis on a favourable result.

   Nevertheless, there is no available dated protocol or statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the exact initial-pain outcome definition, time categories, and analysis were prespecified before unblinded outcome data were available. The trial-registration information referenced in the request was not available in the material provided here, so it cannot be used to verify prespecification. Pain could be measured and analysed in multiple ways—for example, onset time, proportion with pain, VAS intensity, pain location, and repeated follow-up time points. Although the reported initial-pain distribution appears complete, it is not possible to exclude selection among potential pain measurements or analyses.

   * Supporting text: the stated aims included determining “the time at which pain starts.”
   * “They described the time of initial pain in the first question.”
   * “There were no statistically significant differences between the groups.”
   * No protocol, prespecified statistical analysis plan, or verifiable registration record was available in the supplied material.
