## RoB 2.0 Assessment: Outcome = Muscle Damage (CK/AST) — Allopurinol vs Placebo (Racing Period B)

**Effect of interest specified:** Effect of assignment to intervention (allopurinol vs. placebo), per instructions.

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process — **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Sequence generation:* The paper states, “crew members were divided into two groups according to random numbers,” which is sufficient to answer “Probably yes” to randomness of sequence generation.
- *Allocation concealment:* No information is provided about how/when allocation was concealed from investigators or participants prior to enrolment. The text notes only that “Sailors were blind regarding the group assignment,” which pertains to blinding after allocation, not concealment of the sequence itself. This warrants “No information” for signalling question 1.2.
- *Baseline imbalance:* The two groups were markedly imbalanced with respect to a key prognostic factor — physical work-intensity category, which the study itself demonstrates is strongly associated with CK/AST levels (Fig. 2; HIW group had roughly double the CK of MIW/LIW at baseline). The allopurinol group contained 8 HIW/13 total members (62%), whereas the placebo group contained only 2 HIW/10 (20%). Given that HIW status is a major determinant of the outcome (muscle-damage markers), this imbalance is concerning and could itself explain part of the observed “treatment effect” independent of allopurinol. Although this could be a chance imbalance given the extremely small sample (n=23), the magnitude and its direct relevance to the outcome raises concern about the robustness of randomization in this small, non-centrally-randomized allocation.
- Combining “Probably yes” random sequence + “No information” on concealment + a plausible randomization-related imbalance in a key prognostic factor leads to a judgement of **Some concerns** rather than Low risk.

---

### 2. Bias due to deviations from the intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention) — **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- Participants were blinded to allocation: “Sailors were blind regarding the group assignment.” The study was placebo-controlled, implying that personnel administering the allopurinol/placebo were also likely unaware of assignment (double-blind design implied, though not explicitly stated for personnel).
- There is no evidence of any additional non-protocol interventions, co-interventions, or trial-context-driven behavior changes reported that would differentially affect the two groups' muscle-damage outcomes.
- Analysis appears consistent with intention-to-treat principles for participants included (all who provided pre/post samples were analysed by assigned group); no evidence of switching between allopurinol and placebo groups or of exclusion based on outcomes.
- Because both participants and (implicitly) those administering treatment were blinded, and there is no indication that deviations from protocol occurred due to trial context, this domain is judged as **Low risk**.

---

### 3. Bias due to missing outcome data — **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- The article states: “Four sailors finally declined to take part in this phase of the study,” resulting in the analyzed sample of 23 (13 allopurinol + 10 placebo) out of the original 27-member crew.
- No information is given about which group these four sailors would have been assigned to, their baseline characteristics (e.g., work-intensity category), or the reasons for declining participation.
- Because reasons for dropout are unreported, it cannot be excluded that missingness was related to willingness to take medication, anticipated side effects, or other factors potentially correlated with the true value of the outcome (e.g., sailors more concerned about muscle soreness might have been more or less likely to decline).
- Given the very small sample size (23 total), even a few missing participants could materially influence the effect estimate, and there is no sensitivity analysis or evidence provided that the results are robust to this missingness.
- This leads to a judgement of **Some concerns** (data not available for all/nearly all participants; no evidence provided that missingness did not bias the result; missingness could plausibly depend on outcome-related factors, though not clearly shown to be likely).

---

### 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome — **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- Muscle damage was measured using validated, objective laboratory biochemical assays: “serum CK and AST activities were spectrophotometrically measured using commercially available reagents on an automatic random access analyser… The intra-assay coefficient of variation was 4.5% for CK and 4.2% for AST.” This is an appropriate, objective, automated method with low measurement error.
- The same standardized laboratory method was used for both experimental and placebo groups, with no indication that measurement or ascertainment differed by group.
- CK/AST assays are automated laboratory tests not subject to subjective interpretation, and there is no indication that laboratory personnel processing the samples were aware of, or could be influenced by, treatment allocation. This outcome falls into the category of "observer-reported outcomes not involving judgement" for which lack of blinding of assessors is not expected to bias results.
- Therefore, this domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

---

### 5. Bias in selection of the reported result — **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- No pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial protocol detailing planned outcome measures/analyses for the allopurinol intervention (Period B) was referenced or made available in the article; the RoB2 assessment instructions direct reliance on the trial registration record for this domain, but no explicit registration details (e.g., primary/secondary outcome pre-specification, planned statistical models) are quoted or evident in the text to confirm that CK, AST, and MDA were pre-specified as co-primary outcomes analyzed as reported.
- The result is presented as a single, defined outcome (CK and AST before/after intervention, per-group comparison) with a clear pre/post comparison, and there is no evidence of multiple alternative definitions, cut-points, or subsets of data being selectively reported based on results (e.g., no indication that multiple time points, models, or adjusted vs. unadjusted analyses were tried and only favorable ones shown).
- However, in the absence of confirmed prospective registration/analysis plan predating unblinded data availability, it cannot be confirmed with certainty that the reported CK/AST comparisons were the only ones planned, particularly given the multiple analyses conducted across the paper (correlation analyses, subgroup analyses by work intensity, MDA analyses) that could reflect flexible/exploratory analytic choices.
- This leads to a default judgement of **Some concerns**, reflecting lack of information rather than clear evidence of selective reporting.

### **Overall risk of bias for Muscle Damage outcome: Some concerns**

*Rationale for overall judgement:* No individual domain was rated “High risk,” but three domains (randomization process, missing outcome data, and selection of the reported result) were rated “Some concerns.” Per RoB 2 guidance, multiple “Some concerns” ratings across domains can lower confidence in the result sufficiently to warrant an overall rating of "Some concerns," bordering on "High risk" given the compounding nature of these limitations (small sample size, unclear allocation concealment, unexplained baseline group imbalance in a key prognostic factor, unaccounted dropouts, and unclear pre-specification of analyses). A cautious overall judgement of **Some concerns** is assigned, with a note that the cumulative effect of these concerns should be considered when weighing the reliability of this trial's findings on muscle-damage biomarkers.
