# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0 – SGA Score Outcome

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, primarily due to lack of detail on random sequence generation, though allocation concealment appears adequate and baseline characteristics were generally balanced.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Allocation concealment (Q1.2):* Adequate. "Individuals were randomized by a computer-generated list, which was used for packaging of the tablets and then maintained by a third party that was not involved in the conduction of the study. Marked tablet packs were designated for each patient." This suggests concealment was maintained through third-party control — judged **Yes/Probably yes**.
- *Random sequence generation (Q1.1):* The report states "computer-generated list" but does not specify the method (e.g., random number generator, block randomization details). This is judged **Probably yes**, as computer-generation typically implies randomness, though no further detail on the algorithm is provided.
- *Baseline imbalance (Q1.3):* Table 1 shows generally similar baseline characteristics between groups (age, sex, dialysis duration, Kt/V, comorbidity index). However, some differences are notable though not statistically tested formally: cerebrovascular disease (6 vs 1), congestive heart failure (8 vs 12), peptic ulcer disease (2 vs 7), and diabetes (8 vs 12 with end-organ damage). The trial authors acknowledge: "Although the treatment group had marginally more patients with underlying heart failure and fewer patients with cerebrovascular disease than the control group, the differences were not statistically significant." These differences are plausible given small sample size (n=30/arm) and not judged severe enough to indicate randomization failure, but combined with lack of detail on sequence generation, this supports "Some concerns" rather than "Low."

Applying the algorithm: Sequence random = Probably yes (no detailed elaboration but reasonable to assume "Probably yes" for computer-generated list); allocation concealed = Yes; baseline imbalance = No/Probably no (compatible with chance) → this pathway would normally yield **Low risk**. However, given the absence of explicit description of the randomization method (e.g., simple vs. block, allocation ratio control) and the somewhat imprecise reporting, a more conservative judgment of **Some concerns** is applied.

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, due to lack of blinding assurance details but strong evidence blinding was intended and no cointerventions arising from awareness were reported.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Blinding of participants/personnel (Q2.1/2.2):* Blinding was clearly attempted: "The appearance, packaging, and labeling of the tablets were identical. Both kinds of tablets had artificial mint flavor to give an identical taste... Both patients and investigators were blinded." This is a strong effort at blinding, judged **Probably no** (unaware) for both participants and personnel.
- However, some participants experienced side effects potentially linked to bicarbonate (dyspepsia, dizziness) which *could* unblind some individuals: "Four patients in the treatment group could not tolerate the study medication because of dyspepsia (3 cases) or dizziness (1 case). Study medication was stopped in 3 patients of the placebo group because of dyspepsia (2 cases) or skin rash (1 case)." This raises some possibility of partial unblinding due to symptomatic differences, introducing minor uncertainty.
- *Deviations arising from trial context (Q2.3):* No clear evidence that non-protocol interventions or co-interventions arose because of trial context (e.g., no evidence that physicians or patients changed diet, other medications, or PD prescriptions specifically due to knowledge of allocation). Dialysis prescription changes were protocolized: "Dialysis prescription was changed only when there was clinical evidence of under-dialysis." This suggests **Probably no** deviations from trial context.
- *Analysis (Q2.6):* Appropriate ITT analysis was used: "Analyses were intention to treat irrespective of adherence to treatment regimen." This satisfies Part 2 as **Low risk**.

Given near-complete blinding, protocolized care, and appropriate ITT analysis, but with minor residual uncertainty due to potential unblinding from side effects, the overall domain judgement is **Some concerns** rather than "Low," reflecting the theoretical possibility (not clearly excluded) that assessment or behavior deviations occurred due to detectable side effects in a minority of participants.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias — outcome data (SGA) appear to have been collected for nearly all participants, and the ITT approach was maintained even for those who discontinued medication.

**b. Rationale:**
- The manuscript states that even patients who stopped taking study medication continued to be followed: "Although study medication was stopped in these patients, biochemical tests and nutritional assessment were continued for 52 wk according to the study protocol."
- Mortality was accounted for (2 deaths in treatment, 5 in placebo), and results are presented for all time points (0, 12, 24, 36, 52 weeks) in Table 3, suggesting SGA was assessed in essentially the full randomized cohort at all follow-up points, with only expected attrition due to death, technique failure, or transplantation explicitly documented.
- No indication of substantial differential dropout related to the SGA outcome itself; the study explicitly used intention-to-treat analysis: "Analyses were intention to treat irrespective of adherence to treatment regimen."
- There is no explicit CONSORT flow diagram provided in the text, which introduces minor uncertainty, but the narrative description supports that outcome data were available for all/nearly all participants (with clear accounting of the small number of deaths/transfers).

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, because SGA is a subjective, judgment-based observer-reported outcome, and although outcome assessors were blinded to treatment group, this was a single-center trial where full blinding integrity could be threatened by potential clues (e.g., bicarbonate side effects, biochemical results).

**b. Rationale:**
- *Appropriateness of method (Q4.1):* SGA is an established, appropriately validated method for nutritional assessment. "SGA was performed at 0, 12, 24, 36, and 52 wk by two trained observers." Judged **No** (not inappropriate).
- *Comparability of measurement between groups (Q4.2):* Same method/protocol applied identically to both groups at same timepoints — judged **No**.
- *Blinding of outcome assessors (Q4.3):* The article explicitly states: "SGA was performed... by two trained observers who were blinded from the treatment group allocation and biochemical results of the patients." This is an important methodological strength, explicitly addressing potential detection bias by withholding biochemical data (bicarbonate levels) from assessors as well.
- *Could assessment be influenced by knowledge of intervention (Q4.4/4.5):* Because SGA involves clinical judgment (subjective assessment of muscle mass, fat, weight change, anorexia), there is inherent potential for influence if blinding were broken. Although the investigators took commendable steps (identical tablets, blinding of assessors to both allocation AND biochemical results), some risk remains because:
  - A minority of patients had side effects (dyspepsia, dizziness, skin rash) that could tip off assessors or patients (who self-report anorexia/appetite change, one SGA component) to their treatment allocation.
  - SGA includes patient-reported elements (anorexia, subjective changes) not merely objective clinician assessment, and patients themselves were not perfectly guaranteed to remain unaware given symptomatic differences.
- Given these considerations, there is a plausible, non-negligible chance that blinding could have been compromised for a subset of patients, but no direct evidence that this influenced the outcome assessment. This supports a judgement of **Some concerns** rather than "Low," since the assessment could theoretically have been influenced by unblinding, though it is not likely to have been substantially so given the described precautions.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, due to the absence of a publicly registered pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial registration confirming that SGA (and its subscales/timepoints) was the pre-specified primary outcome and analysis method prior to unblinding.

**b. Rationale:**
- The article states SGA was intended a priori as the primary outcome, used for sample size calculation: "The sample size was estimated before the study... with the SGA as the primary outcome measure." This is reassuring and suggests low risk for this specific outcome selection.
- However, no independently registered trial protocol (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) is cited in the article, and the provided trial registration record (doc_id: 4dab5cab...) was not detailed in the prompt beyond being available for cross-referencing; without explicit protocol/SAP details confirming the pre-specified plan for handling of SGA subscales (anorexia, weight loss, subcutaneous fat, muscle mass) and timepoints (0, 12, 24, 36, 52 weeks) as reported, it is not possible to fully rule out selective emphasis on time points showing significant differences (e.g., 24 week emphasis in the abstract and discussion).
- The four-item subscale results are reported with mixed significance (anorexia and weight loss significant; subcutaneous fat and muscle mass not significant), and all are reported transparently in Table 3, which reduces concern for outcome-level selective reporting within the SGA domain.
- Given that the overall SGA score (the primary, pre-specified outcome) is reported comprehensively across all time points with consistent analysis (repeated measures ANOVA), and there is no evidence of selective suppression of unfavorable timepoints or alternative analyses, the risk is not judged "High." However, absence of a fully accessible, date-stamped, pre-specified statistical analysis plan prevents a "Low risk" judgement.

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

Per RoB 2.0 guidance (Table 1), because the trial is judged to raise "some concerns" in multiple domains (randomization, deviations from intended intervention, measurement of outcome, and selective reporting) but is not judged "High risk" in any single domain, the overall risk of bias for the SGA outcome is **Some concerns**. The cumulative effect of "some concerns" across four of five domains suggests that this could reasonably be elevated to "High risk" as well, given the guidance that "'Some concerns' in multiple domains may lead the review authors to decide on an overall judgement of 'High' risk of bias." Given the moderate/small sample size (n=60), single-center design, and lack of full documentation (e.g., pre-registered SAP), reviewers may reasonably consider downgrading this trial to **High risk of bias** overall, particularly if these concerns are judged collectively to substantially lower confidence in the SGA result.
