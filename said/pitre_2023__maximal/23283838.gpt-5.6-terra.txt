1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants “were randomly assigned to 250mg of DCS or placebo in identical-appearing capsules by a data manager who had no interaction with participants.” This supports that allocation was undertaken independently of participant-facing study staff, but it does not describe:

   - how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, blocking); or
   - how allocation was concealed until a participant had been enrolled.

   Thus, there is no direct evidence that the sequence was genuinely random or that future allocations could not have been foreseen. The fact that a data manager with no participant interaction assigned treatment provides some reassurance, but is insufficient to judge allocation concealment as adequate.

   Baseline characteristics were mostly comparable: “Participants did not differ across group in regard to: gender, ethnicity, use of medication, use of antidepressants, LSAS scores at Visit 1, average SUDS for the speech in Visit 1, or Social Phobia Scale ratings at Visit 1.” Groups differed in age (*p* = .019), but this single imbalance among several examined characteristics, in a small trial, is compatible with chance and does not itself strongly indicate a failed randomization process. Therefore, the principal concern is incomplete reporting of sequence generation and allocation concealment rather than evidence that randomization was actually compromised.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to DCS versus placebo at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and experimenters were blinded to treatment allocation. The study used “identical-appearing capsules,” and the authors assessed blinding: “neither participants nor experimenters were able to discern assignment (ps > .15).” This makes trial-context deviations—such as differential encouragement, cointerventions, or implementation of the exposure procedure because treatment allocation was known—unlikely.

   The exposure task was tailored to participants’ anxiety level, but this was part of the intended study intervention and was implemented before, and independently of, known treatment assignment. The report does not identify differential non-protocol interventions or differential implementation by randomized group.

   The reported analysis excluded four participants who did not return for Visit 2: “Only those participants completing Visit 2 are analyzed.” For the RoB 2 estimand of assignment to intervention, this is treated as a modified intention-to-treat/available-case analysis and is not, in itself, a bias due to deviations from intended intervention. Its implications are addressed under missing outcome data. Among participants with observed outcomes, analyses retained participants in their randomized DCS or placebo groups; there is no indication of an as-treated or per-protocol comparison.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   c. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   d. **Rationale:** Outcome data at Visit 2 were unavailable for 4 of 34 randomized participants (12%): 2/18 in the DCS group and 2/16 in the placebo group. The reasons were balanced numerically across groups: one participant in each group became unreachable and one participant in each group declined to continue. The equal numbers missing in each group reduce concern about strongly differential attrition.

   However, this is not “all, or nearly all” participants for a continuous subjective distress outcome, especially because the primary between-session outcome requires measurements at both visits. The investigators performed a complete-case analysis, explicitly stating that only Visit-2 completers were analyzed, and they did not report sensitivity analyses assessing the effect of the missing data.

   It is plausible that failure to return or declining further participation could relate to the participant’s underlying anxiety, distress after the first speech, or response to the exposure. Nevertheless, the reported reasons do not establish that missingness was likely related to the unobserved outcome, and attrition was not imbalanced between groups. Accordingly, the appropriate judgement is **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Between-session habituation was based on subjective anxiety during the speech exposures, assessed using the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS). The SUDS is a standard, directly relevant measure of subjective anxiety; it ranged from 0 (“no anxiety”) to 100 (“most anxiety imaginable”). The study applied the same measurement method at both visits and in both randomized groups. The authors also report good internal consistency when the ratings were combined: “Cronbach’s αs > .85 in these data.”

   The outcome assessor for SUDS is the participant, since this is a participant-reported outcome. Such outcomes could be influenced by knowledge of treatment assignment. However, participants were blinded through identical-appearing capsules, and the blinding assessment found that participants could not identify their allocation better than chance (“neither participants nor experimenters were able to discern assignment”). Therefore, there is no reason to conclude that participants’ ratings were differentially influenced by awareness of receiving DCS or placebo.

   Although speech parameters were individualized to produce clinically significant anxiety, the procedure was intended to standardize participants’ anxiety experience and the second speech was conducted in an “identical situation.” There is no indication that outcome ascertainment differed systematically between DCS and placebo groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies between-session habituation as the primary outcome: “Our primary measure of DCS’s exposure-facilitating effect was between-session habituation,” operationalized as “a drop of at least 5 points in average SUDS across the exposure across visits.” It reports the corresponding categorical result, as well as continuous SUDS analyses and the Social Phobia Scale. Thus, the published report is reasonably transparent about the primary outcome definition and presents more than one related outcome analysis.

   However, a dated, sufficiently detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan was not available in the materials provided for this assessment. In particular, it cannot be confirmed that the exact ≥5-point SUDS threshold, the use of average SUDS across exposure, the selected timing/aggregation of repeated SUDS observations, and the complete-case analytic approach were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The study involved several potentially eligible outcome measurements and analyses: repeated SUDS observations within each speech, categorical habituation, continuous average SUDS change, and fear-of-scrutiny scores. There is no direct evidence that the reported between-session habituation result was selected based on its magnitude, direction, or statistical significance. Therefore, high risk of bias is not warranted. Nevertheless, without verifiable prospective analysis intentions from the trial registration or a statistical analysis plan, there are **some concerns** about selective reporting.
