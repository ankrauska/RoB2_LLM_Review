1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias
a. Judgement: Low risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The study states that participants were randomly selected and assigned to study conditions stratified by gender and age. Although specific details of the allocation sequence generation and concealment are not extensively described in the text, the computerized nature of the baseline assessment and subsequent random assignment typical of such trials suggests these processes were likely adequate. Furthermore, the authors report, "There were no significant differences between conditions on age, race, gender," indicating that the randomization process successfully achieved baseline balance without evidence of a problem.

2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias
a. Judgement: Low risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The assessment is based on the effect of assignment to the intervention (intention-to-treat). Although participants and therapists were aware of the assigned interventions (as blinding is not feasible for behavioral interventions), there is no evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose because of the trial context. Crucially, the authors explicitly state, "An intent-to-treat approach was used. All randomly assigned participants were included whether the intervention was received or not," and utilized Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) which "use all data available for participants, including those lost to attrition." This constitutes an appropriate analysis for the effect of assignment.

3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias
a. Judgement: Low risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The trial had a high retention rate, with 83.6% (607/726) of the randomized participants completing the 12-month follow-up. The analysis utilized Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE), which includes all available data. While some outcome data are missing, the proportion is relatively small for a 12-month behavioral study in an emergency department setting, and there is no strong evidence to suggest that missingness in the outcome was highly dependent on its true value in a way that would introduce substantial bias.

4. measurement of the outcome: some concerns
a. Judgement: Some concerns.
b. Rationale: The outcome, peer aggression, is a self-reported behavioral measure assessed via a computer survey (conflict tactic scale). Because participants were not blinded to their intervention group, the outcome assessor (the participant) was aware of the intervention received. Given the subjective nature of self-reported aggression and the behavioral focus of the intervention, it is possible that the assessment of the outcome could have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention received, leading to some concerns regarding measurement bias.

5. selective reporting: low risk of bias
a. Judgement: Low risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The trial was prospectively registered (NCT00251212) with violent behaviors and alcohol misuse listed as primary outcomes at 12 months. The outcomes reported in the manuscript align with the pre-specified primary outcomes in the trial registry. There is no indication that the reported results were selectively chosen from multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses based on the direction or significance of the findings.
