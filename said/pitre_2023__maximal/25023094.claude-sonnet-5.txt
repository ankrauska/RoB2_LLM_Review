# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment: Pain VAS Outcome

**Study:** Efficacy of Therapeutic Ultrasound in Pain and Joint Mobility in Whiplash Traumatic Acute and Subacute Phases

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The allocation sequence had a random component (sex-stratified randomization), allocation was concealed using sealed envelopes, and baseline data show no meaningful imbalance in the pain (VAS) variable.

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: "The distribution of subjects to the two treatment groups was performed using sex-stratified randomization" — this indicates a random element (signalling question 1.1 = Probably yes).
- Allocation concealment: "the allocation sequence was blinded through the use of sealed envelopes" — while not described in the extended detail (sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof), there is no indication of failure of concealment, and the trial was blinded to patients and assessor (1.2 = Probably yes).
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows VAS pre-test values were not significantly different between groups (p = 0.421; Control 7.00 [1.90] vs Experimental 7.40 [2.30]). Some borderline differences existed in mobility variables (p = 0.05–0.09), but these are compatible with chance and the authors note they did not affect comparability; critically, for the outcome under assessment (VAS pain), there is no imbalance (1.3 = No).

Combining: random sequence (Yes/Probably yes) + concealed allocation (Yes/Probably yes) + no problematic baseline imbalance for VAS → **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Although the physiotherapist delivering treatment was not blinded, there is no evidence that this awareness led to deviations from the protocol that arose because of trial context and impacted the pain outcome. Analysis was by intention-to-treat, with no participants lost.

**b. Rationale:**
- Participants: "analytical, prospective, experimental study, with blinding of patients and blind assessor" — participants were blinded (2.1 = No).
- Personnel: The authors explicitly state, "The blinding of the physiotherapist who applied the treatment was not possible, which constitutes another limitation of the study" (2.2 = Yes).
- Deviations arising from trial context: There is no reported evidence of non-protocol co-interventions, differential care, or contamination between groups arising from the unblinded therapist. Both groups received an identical structured protocol (25 sessions, identical massage/exercise regimen), differing only in active vs. sham US (2.3 = Probably no/No information).
- Analysis: "The analysis was done by intention to treat" and "During the study no patient was lost or dropped out" — an appropriate ITT analysis was used with complete data (2.6 = Yes).

Because personnel were aware but there is no evidence of trial-context-driven deviations affecting the outcome, and an appropriate ITT analysis was applied, the domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Outcome data were available for all participants; there was no attrition.

**b. Rationale:**
- "During the study no patient was lost or dropped out."
- The flow chart (Figure 1) confirms all 54 randomized participants (27/27) were followed through to discharge with complete VAS data reported at all four time points (pre-test, post-test 5, post-test 10, post-test discharge; see Tables 1–3).

Since outcome data were available for essentially 100% of randomized participants, missingness cannot have biased the result. **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** VAS is an appropriate, standard method for assessing pain; measurement procedures were identical between groups; and both the participants (self-reporting) and outcome assessor were blinded to allocation.

**b. Rationale:**
- Method appropriateness: VAS is a validated, widely used tool for pain measurement (4.1 = No, not inappropriate).
- Comparability: "A physiotherapist appraiser assessed the patients' pain using the VAS and algometer before starting the 1st (pre-test), 6th (post-test 5) and 11th (post-test 10) sessions and on completing the 25th session" — identical measurement schedule and methods for both groups (4.2 = No).
- Blinding: The study explicitly states "blinding of patients and blind assessor." Because VAS is a participant-reported outcome, the participant is effectively the outcome assessor, and the participants were blinded to allocation (active vs. sham US); the independent assessor administering the scale was also blinded (4.3 = No, unaware).
- Because both the participant (assessor of self-reported pain) and the formal outcome assessor were blinded, knowledge of intervention assignment could not have influenced VAS scoring.

This supports a judgement of **Low risk of bias**, with the caveat that no formal check of blinding success (e.g., asking patients to guess allocation) was reported, which is a minor limitation but not sufficient alone to raise concern given the placebo-controlled, blinded design.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** There is no evidence from the article that a pre-specified statistical analysis plan (finalized before unblinded data were available) was followed, and no trial registration information is described in the text to confirm that all pre-specified outcome measurements/analyses for VAS were reported as planned. However, there is no overt evidence of selective reporting from multiple outcome measures or multiple analyses for the VAS outcome itself.

**b. Rationale:**
- The article does not reference a trial protocol, prospectively registered analysis plan, or trial registry entry. There is no statement confirming a pre-specified plan was finalized before unblinding (5.1 = No information).
- VAS results are reported consistently at all pre-specified time points (pre-test, post-test 5, post-test 10, discharge) in Tables 1–3, with no indication of selective omission of specific time points, measurement instruments, or subscales for pain (5.2 = Probably no).
- Similarly, there is no evidence that multiple statistical analyses (e.g., adjusted vs. unadjusted, alternative cut-points) were conducted and only favorable ones reported for VAS; a single, consistent analytic approach (Student's t / Mann-Whitney U with effect sizes) was applied uniformly across all variables and time points (5.3 = Probably no).

Because there is no information available to confirm a pre-specified analysis plan (a required condition for "Low risk of bias"), but no direct evidence of selection based on results either, the domain is judged **Some concerns**, per the RoB 2 decision algorithm (absence of information on pre-specification, combined with no evidence of selection from multiple outcomes/analyses, defaults to "Some concerns" rather than "Low").

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement for VAS Pain Outcome: **Some concerns**

**Rationale:** Four of the five domains (randomization, deviations from intended intervention, missing outcome data, and measurement of the outcome) were judged at low risk of bias. However, the selective reporting domain was judged as "Some concerns" due to the absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan or trial registration confirming the outcome and analysis strategy were fixed prior to unblinding. Per RoB 2 guidance, a judgement of "Some concerns" in any single domain, without any domain being rated "High risk of bias," leads to an overall judgement of **Some concerns** for this outcome.
