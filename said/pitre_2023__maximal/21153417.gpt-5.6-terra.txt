**Outcome assessed:** Muscle damage, measured by serum creatine kinase (CK), in racing period B: allopurinol (300 mg/day before racing; *n*=13 analysed) versus placebo (*n*=10 analysed). The target effect is the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **high risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The report states that, in period B, “*crew members were divided into two groups according to random numbers*,” which supports use of a random allocation sequence. However, allocation concealment is not described. There is no information indicating that assignment was administered centrally, by an independent person, or through secure sequentially numbered opaque envelopes.

More importantly, there are substantial baseline imbalances relevant to CK/muscle-damage risk. The allopurinol group included **8 high-intensity-work (HIW)** sailors among 13 participants, whereas the placebo group included only **2 HIW** sailors among 10 participants:

> “*a first group of 13 participants (8 HIW, 4 MIW and 1 LIW) was given… allopurinol… The other ten-member group received a placebo (2 HIW, 4 MIW and 4 LIW).*”

HIW sailors had much higher CK values in period A than moderate-/low-intensity workers. Consistent with this imbalance, the reported pre-race CK was appreciably higher in the allopurinol group than in the placebo group (445.8 versus 275.8 U/L). This imbalance in an important prognostic characteristic, coupled with absent information about allocation concealment, raises a serious concern that the randomization process did not produce comparable groups.

---

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **high risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Participants were reportedly blinded:

> “*Sailors were blind regarding the group assignment.*”

Thus, there is no direct evidence that participant knowledge caused differential cointerventions or departures from protocol. However, blinding of carers, team staff, personnel administering treatment, and laboratory personnel is not reported.

The more important concern for the effect of assignment is the analysis population. The report states:

> “*Four sailors finally declined to take part in this phase of the study.*”

The study initially involved all 27 crew members, but the period-B analysis included only 23 sailors (13 allopurinol and 10 placebo). The report does not state whether these four withdrawals occurred before or after randomization, which groups they had been assigned to, or their reasons for declining. If they were randomized and then excluded, this is not a full intention-to-treat analysis.

The statistical methods describe comparisons “*within groups*” using t tests, and the principal reported finding is a significant pre–post reduction in CK in the allopurinol group, rather than a clearly specified intention-to-treat comparison of the randomized groups. The analysis therefore does not clearly include all randomized participants in their assigned groups. With approximately 15% of the original crew absent from the intervention-phase analysis and no accounting for those exclusions, there is potential for a substantial effect on the result.

---

### 3. Missing outcome data: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
Period-B CK data appear to be available for the 23 analysed sailors, but four of the 27 original crew members did not participate in this phase:

> “*Four sailors finally declined to take part in this phase of the study.*”

No information is provided on their randomized allocation, timing of withdrawal relative to allocation and treatment, reasons for non-participation, or whether their CK outcomes were measured. Therefore, the outcome data were not clearly available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants.

There is no sensitivity analysis or other analysis demonstrating that exclusion of these four sailors could not have influenced the CK result. Although there is insufficient evidence that missingness was definitely related to the true CK outcome, the absence of reasons and allocation-specific accounting means that this cannot be excluded. The information supports **some concerns**, rather than high risk, because the report does not establish that missingness was likely related to CK values or differed systematically by intervention group.

---

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
CK is an objective biochemical outcome, measured using the same laboratory approach in both groups:

> “*serum CK, and AST activities were spectrophotometrically measured using commercially available reagents on an automatic random access analyser (Autolab 18…).*”

The assay precision was reported:

> “*The intra-assay coefficient of variation was 4.5% for CK.*”

The same blood collection and laboratory procedures appear to have been used for the allopurinol and placebo groups. No evidence suggests differential measurement or ascertainment between intervention groups.

Although blinding of laboratory personnel is not explicitly reported, CK measurement on an automated analyser is unlikely to be materially influenced by assessor knowledge of treatment assignment. Therefore, measurement bias for CK is unlikely.

---

### 5. Selective reporting: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The article does not report a prespecified statistical analysis plan for the period-B CK comparison. The available report does not establish that the CK analysis, time point, analysis model, handling of exclusions, and between-group comparison were specified before unblinded outcome data became available.

The study evaluated several related biomarkers and multiple racing-period measurements, including CK, AST, and MDA. For period B, the article emphasizes a statistically significant within-group decrease in CK among allopurinol-treated sailors:

> “*A significant decrease in CK activity was found among participants who received allopurinol… compared with those taking the placebo…*”

In contrast, AST and MDA showed no statistically significant treatment-related changes. This pattern does not itself prove selective reporting, and no direct evidence shows that the reported CK result was selected from multiple analyses because of its statistical significance. However, without a sufficiently detailed, time-stamped analysis plan or usable registration information confirming the prespecified CK outcome and analysis, selective reporting cannot be ruled out.
