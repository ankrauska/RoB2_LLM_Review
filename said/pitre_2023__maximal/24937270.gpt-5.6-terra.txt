**Outcome assessed:** Interhemispheric functional connectivity, operationalized as change from pre- to post-stimulation in EEG interhemispheric coherence (particularly theta coherence) after random-frequency (1–5 Hz) tPCS versus sham. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

### a. Judgement
**Some concerns** because the trial is described as randomized with blocks of four, and baseline characteristics do not clearly suggest a failure of randomization. However, the report does not state how the allocation sequence was generated or whether allocation was concealed until participants were enrolled.

### b. Rationale
The study reports that participants:

> “were randomized into one of four study arms”

and that:

> “Randomization was completed using blocks of four.”

Blocked randomization can be valid, but the report does not specify that allocation order within blocks was randomly generated (e.g., computer-generated random permuted blocks). Thus, there is insufficient information to conclude confidently that the allocation sequence was random.

There is also no description of allocation concealment—for example, central randomization, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or an independent allocator. The report therefore provides no evidence that recruiters could not foresee upcoming allocations.

Baseline characteristics did not show a clear indication of a flawed randomization process:

> “There were no significant differences in demographic characteristics between groups (Table 1).”

The groups were of broadly similar size: sham, 9; 1 Hz, 9; 100 Hz, 9; random frequency, 11. Although the study is small and the sham group was somewhat older on average (36 years) than the active groups (approximately 27–29 years), this pattern is plausibly compatible with chance and does not, by itself, demonstrate manipulation or failure of randomization.

Accordingly, the absence of reported allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement.

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **Some concerns**

### a. Judgement
**Some concerns** for the effect of assignment to intervention. Participants and the outcome assessor were blinded, and there is no reported evidence of deviations that affected coherence outcomes. However, the report does not clearly state whether the personnel administering/delivering stimulation were blinded, nor does it provide sufficient information to exclude trial-context-related deviations or differential co-interventions.

### b. Rationale
The study states:

> “Both the investigator assessing the outcomes and the subjects were blinded to the intervention.”

This supports blinding of participants and the outcome assessor. The sham procedure was also designed to resemble active stimulation initially:

> “For the sham condition, stimulation was applied for 30 s and then turned off automatically.”

Furthermore, the reported blinding check found no evidence that participants identified their assigned group better than chance:

> “Subjects did not guess correctly the stimulation group to which they were assigned beyond chance (p = 0.679).”

These features reduce the likelihood that participant expectations caused differential behavior or outcome-related deviations.

However, the article does not explicitly state whether the person who applied the stimulation or otherwise delivered the intervention was blinded to allocation. The phrase “double-blinded” is clarified only as blinding of subjects and the outcome assessor; it does not establish blinding of intervention-delivery personnel. The report also does not describe whether protocol deviations, additional interventions, or other trial-context effects occurred differentially across groups.

The analysis appears to preserve randomized groups for the available EEG data: the analysis compared the randomized stimulation groups, and the one participant removed because of excessive EEG artefact was not reassigned to another group. Thus, there is no evidence of an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis that would be inappropriate for the assignment effect.

Nevertheless, incomplete reporting on personnel blinding and deviations arising from the trial context leads to **some concerns**, rather than low risk.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

### a. Judgement
**Low risk of bias** because EEG outcome data were available for nearly all participants who received stimulation, with only one post-randomization exclusion due to excessive EEG artefacts.

### b. Rationale
The participant flow is reported as follows:

> “Out of the 40 subjects that were recruited, 38 completed the single stimulation session.”

The two people who did not participate withdrew before receiving stimulation:

> “Two subjects signed the consent form and decided not to participate before stimulation was given.”

Among the 38 participants who underwent stimulation, one participant was excluded from the EEG analysis:

> “One subject in the Random Frequency group was removed from the analysis due to excessive artifacts in the EEG recording.”

Therefore, coherence data appear to have been analyzed for 37 of 38 participants who received the assigned intervention (approximately 97%). For this continuous EEG outcome, this is generally “nearly all” participants under RoB 2 guidance.

The stated reason—excessive recording artefacts—concerns technical usability of the EEG recording rather than an explicitly outcome-related clinical reason such as deterioration, lack of efficacy, or treatment-related adverse effects. There is no indication that exclusions occurred in multiple groups, that reasons differed systematically by group, or that missingness was likely related to the unobserved true coherence value.

Although the excluded participant was in the random-frequency group, the amount of missing data is small. On the available evidence, it is unlikely that this single missing EEG record materially biased the comparison.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

### a. Judgement
**Low risk of bias** because interhemispheric functional connectivity was measured using the same objective qEEG procedure in all groups, and the investigator assessing outcomes was blinded.

### b. Rationale
Functional connectivity was assessed using EEG interhemispheric coherence:

> “We used a 64-channel Electrical Geodesic Incorporated (EGI) EEG device.”

The outcome was defined using a specified computational method:

> “We used magnitude square coherence as a pair-wise connectivity measure.”

The study described the EEG acquisition and processing procedures, including identical pre- and post-stimulation EEG recordings, standard filtering, and artefact detection/rejection:

> “EEG was recorded for a total of 12 min using two conditions: eyes open and eyes closed. This procedure was completed before and after the experimental condition.”

> “Offline analysis included manual artifact detection and rejection and bandpass filtering.”

There is no evidence that the EEG equipment, recording duration, electrode pairs, frequency bands, or analytic procedures differed between intervention groups. All participants underwent the same qEEG recording procedures before and after the assigned condition.

Importantly, the article reports:

> “Both the investigator assessing the outcomes and the subjects were blinded to the intervention.”

Although artefact handling involved a manual component, blinding of the outcome assessor reduces the risk that judgement during data processing or assessment was influenced by knowledge of assigned stimulation. Coherence is also a quantitatively derived electrophysiological measurement, with less scope for subjective assessment than participant-reported outcomes.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

### a. Judgement
**Some concerns** because no sufficiently detailed, time-stamped prespecified statistical analysis plan is available to confirm that the reported theta-coherence result was selected without reference to the results. The analysis involved multiple frequency bands, sub-bands, electrode pairs, eye conditions, and post-hoc comparisons.

### b. Rationale
The article describes several coherence outcomes and analyses:

> “We measured the effects of tPCS on interhemispheric coherence of two bands (alpha and theta) and four sub-bands (low-alpha, high-alpha, low-beta, and high-beta) using two different electrode pairs.”

Both eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions were analyzed:

> “Two conditions were analyzed for each parameter; eyes open and eyes closed.”

The investigators used a mixed-model ANOVA, followed by planned and additional exploratory comparisons:

> “According to a-priori specifications, we used t-test for post-hoc comparison to assess the differences between (1) random frequency group and sham, (2) 1 Hz and sham, (3) 100 Hz and sham.”

In addition, the report states:

> “Further exploratory analysis showed that the theta interhemispheric coherence was significantly higher for the random frequency group compared to 1 Hz … and 100 Hz stimulation.”

The article labels aspects of the work as exploratory:

> “We conducted an exploratory analysis to detect changes in mean power … and interhemispheric coherence…”

Thus, many eligible outcome measurements and analyses were possible: multiple frequency bands/sub-bands, two electrode pairs, two eye conditions, pre/post change scores, group effects, interaction effects, and several pairwise comparisons. The report provides omnibus findings for the bands and sub-bands, but the detailed emphasis is on statistically significant theta findings and a borderline low-alpha result.

The available material does not provide a dated protocol or statistical analysis plan that prespecifies the exact coherence outcome, electrode-pair aggregation/selection, primary contrast, model, treatment of eyes-open versus eyes-closed data, and multiplicity approach before unblinded data were available. The trial registration information referenced in the request is not sufficient here to establish a fully prespecified analysis plan for this specific numerical theta-coherence result.

There is not enough direct evidence to conclude that the reported result was definitely selected based on statistical significance; therefore, **high risk of bias** is not warranted. However, the multiplicity of possible outcome measurements and analyses, combined with exploratory reporting and lack of an available prespecified analysis plan, gives **some concerns**.
