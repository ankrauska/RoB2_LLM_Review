1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation method appears to contain a random element: participants were allocated by drawing an orthosis name from a bag (“*The randomization procedure was to pick the name of the orthosis from a bag including the names of both orthoses*”). Drawing lots can constitute random sequence generation.

   However, the report does not state whether the bag was opaque, who performed the draw, whether allocation occurred only after irrevocable enrolment, or whether recruiters could foresee or influence assignments. Thus, allocation concealment is insufficiently described. The unequal group sizes (Spinomed, *n* = 10; PTS, *n* = 8) are compatible with chance in such a small study and are explicitly attributed to the allocation procedure. Reported baseline characteristics—including age, weight, height, kyphosis angle, and baseline balance score—did not differ significantly between groups. Therefore, baseline data do not themselves suggest a failure of randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and personnel were almost certainly aware of which orthosis was used, because Spinomed and PTS are visibly and physically different devices. Nevertheless, for the assignment-effect estimand, awareness alone is not sufficient to indicate bias. The relevant question is whether deviations from the intended intervention occurred because of the trial context, affected balance score, and were unbalanced between groups.

   The article describes standardized fitting by a certified orthotist: “*Both of the orthoses were fitted on the participants by a certified orthotist who adjusted the straps with the same tension in all participants.*” Participants in both groups were also given 1.5 hours to adapt to their assigned device. No non-protocol cointerventions, differential treatment, failures of implementation, crossovers, or other trial-context deviations that could plausibly affect balance score were reported.

   The comparison of change in balance score was analysed according to allocated group (Spinomed *n* = 10; PTS *n* = 8), using an independent-samples *t* test. All participants reported as allocated were included in the reported group analyses. This is consistent with an analysis of assignment, notwithstanding that the authors did not explicitly label it an intention-to-treat analysis.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data appear available for all randomized participants. The report states that “*Eighteen participants … completed the study procedure*,” with eight participants allocated to PTS and 10 to Spinomed. The balance-score tables report data for all 10 Spinomed participants and all 8 PTS participants.

   Because the outcome was assessed immediately, before and after wearing the orthosis during the study session, there was little opportunity for substantial loss to follow-up. No missing balance-score observations, exclusions from the balance-score analysis, or differential missingness between intervention groups are reported. Consequently, missing outcome data are unlikely to have materially affected the between-group comparison.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Balance score was measured using the NeuroCom EquiTest sensory organization test according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The outcome is a quantitatively generated score from force-plate measurements: “*The load cells in the force plates transmit force information to the computer 100 times per second*,” and the balance score is calculated from the test configurations on a 0–100 scale.

   This is an appropriate and standardized measurement method for postural balance. The same equipment, protocol, test configurations, and testing procedures were used in both intervention groups. Although the report describes the study as “single blinded” without clearly stating who was blinded, the balance score was generated instrumentally rather than requiring subjective assessor judgement. Thus, knowledge of the orthosis is unlikely to have influenced outcome ascertainment itself. Any influence of wearing an orthosis on how participants performed the test would reflect the intervention effect rather than differential outcome measurement.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies balance score as the main outcome measure: “*The balance score was considered in the role of the main outcome measure*.” It also reports the other stated balance outcomes (directional control and reaction time), and it reports both within-group results and the between-group comparison of change scores. This reduces concern that only a favorable balance-score result was selectively presented.

   However, the available report does not provide a dated statistical analysis plan, and it does not clearly document that the precise analysis of the between-group balance-score change was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The report also does not provide sufficient detail to rule out selection among potentially eligible analysis approaches—for example, analysis of post-orthosis scores, change scores, or baseline-adjusted analyses. Therefore, there is insufficient basis for a low-risk judgement, but no direct evidence establishes that the reported balance-score result was selected because of its statistical significance or direction.

**Overall for balance score: some concerns**, driven by incomplete reporting of allocation concealment and the absence of a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan for the reported result.
