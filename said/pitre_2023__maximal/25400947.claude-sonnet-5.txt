# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Frequency of Delivery After 48 Hours

**Trial:** Celecoxib vs. Magnesium Sulfate for Suppression of Preterm Labor
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:** The trial states participants were "divided into two groups by simple random sampling," but no details are provided about how the actual sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random number tables) or how allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants. There is no mention of sealed envelopes, central randomization, or any other concealment mechanism. This leads to "No information" on both sequence generation and allocation concealment (signalling questions 1.1 and 1.2).

Regarding baseline balance, most variables were well balanced (age, P=0.194; parity, P=0.99; history of preterm labor, P=1; dilatation, P=0.06). However, there was a statistically significant difference in effacement between groups (35.93 vs. 42, P≤0.0001) and in gestational age at delivery. The effacement difference is notable, as greater effacement is a plausible prognostic factor for imminent delivery/failure of tocolysis. This raises some question about whether randomization achieved balance in this important prognostic variable, though the authors argue this did not affect celecoxib's relative effectiveness.

Given: (i) no information on sequence generation/concealment, AND (ii) a baseline imbalance in a plausible prognostic factor (effacement) that is unlikely to be entirely due to chance (P≤0.0001) — per the algorithm, "No information" on concealment/sequence generation combined with evidence of imbalance suggesting a possible problem leads to a judgement of **Some concerns** (leaning toward High, but the extremely low P-value could also reflect within-group heterogeneity rather than a failure of randomization, and other baseline variables were well balanced). We adopt "Some concerns" as the conservative-but-cautious judgement, noting this could be escalated to "High" by reviewers with strong priors about the exclusion criteria affecting comparability.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias

**b. Rationale:** 
- The study is explicitly described as "single blinded." Participants were aware of their assigned intervention: one group received an **intravenous infusion** (MgSO4, with loading and maintenance doses, requiring monitoring for toxicity, deep tendon reflexes, urine output, respiratory rate) while the other group received an **oral capsule** (celecoxib) every 12 hours. This is an inherently unblindable difference in route/mode of administration, meaning participants and personnel/carers were certainly aware of intervention assignment (signalling questions 2.1 and 2.2 = Yes).
- Because participants and personnel were aware of intervention assignment, we must ask whether deviations arose because of the trial context that could have affected the outcome. The paper does not explicitly discuss cointerventions, but the differential nature of care (IV monitoring/hospitalization intensity vs. oral capsule with less intensive monitoring) creates a plausible context for differential care-seeking behavior, differential clinical vigilance, or differential threshold for administering additional treatments/rescue tocolysis between groups. No information is given confirming that such deviations did not occur or were balanced.
- There is no information regarding whether deviations (e.g., co-interventions, differential monitoring intensity, unblinded outcome-related decision-making about delivery timing) were balanced between groups.
- The analysis method: the paper states "if the uterine preterm contractions did not stop with those medications, immediately drug has been stopped" — suggesting a per-protocol-like handling of treatment failures, but the analysis appears to include all randomized participants (300 per group) in the delivery-timing outcome, consistent with an ITT-type analysis.
- Given lack of blinding of participants/personnel, and no information (or reason to believe) that trial-context-driven deviations were absent or balanced, combined with the inherently subjective/care-decision-influenced nature of the outcome (delivery timing, which can be influenced by clinical decisions to intervene), this domain is judged **High risk of bias**, per the algorithm criterion: participants/personnel aware, deviations plausible due to trial context, likely to affect outcome, and no evidence of balance.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias

**b. Rationale:** Table 1 reports complete data for all 600 randomized women (300 per group: 240+60=300 in MgSO4 group; 227+30=257... actually reported as 227 (75.7%) + 30 (24.3%) totaling 257, which is a discrepancy)*. Based on the reported denominators used in Tables 2 and 3 (300 per group), it appears outcome data (delivery timing) were available for all or nearly all randomized participants. No dropouts, losses to follow-up, or exclusions after randomization are reported in the results. There is no indication of differential missingness between groups.

*Note: There is an apparent inconsistency in Table 1 percentages/numbers (227+30=257, not 300), but this appears to be a reporting/computational error in the original article rather than evidence of missing data, since Tables 2 and 3 confirm n=300 per group throughout.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:** 
- The outcome ("delivery after 48 hours") is an objective, observer-reported outcome not involving substantial judgement (a documented fact of delivery timing), which would ordinarily suggest low risk of measurement bias.
- However, delivery timing in the context of preterm labor management is not entirely free from clinician influence: decisions about whether/when to intervene obstetrically, whether to declare "failure of tocolysis," and whether to proceed to delivery can be influenced by clinical judgement, especially in a single-blind, non-placebo-controlled design where clinicians were aware of treatment assignment (IV MgSO4 vs. oral celecoxib).
- The study does not report whether outcome assessors (those determining/recording delivery timing) were blinded to intervention assignment. Given that the same clinical team managing labor (aware of assignment due to unblindable route of administration) would also be responsible for outcome-related decisions, there is potential for the assessment/decision-making regarding delivery to be influenced by knowledge of intervention received.
- No blinding of outcome assessment is described, and given the plausible link between clinical decision-making (informed by beliefs about drug efficacy/safety) and timing of delivery, we judge this as **Some concerns** rather than "Low," as there is a plausible but not clearly demonstrated pathway for influence.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:** No trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is referenced or available for this trial in the provided materials. The methods section pre-specifies the outcome of interest (arrest of contractions/delivery timing at 48 hours) and this outcome is reported in the results (Table 1) consistent with the stated methods, with a single analysis (chi-square test, single P-value) presented for this outcome — there is no evidence of multiple alternative analyses or subgroups being selectively reported for this particular outcome.

However, because there is no trial registry entry, protocol, or statistical analysis plan available to confirm that this was the sole pre-specified outcome/analysis approach and that reporting was not selectively chosen from among multiple possible analyses (e.g., different definitions or cut points, adjusted vs. unadjusted analyses), we cannot confirm the analysis was conducted according to a pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. This leads to a judgement of **Some concerns** rather than "Low risk of bias."

---

## Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **High risk of bias**

Given the "High risk of bias" judgement for the domain of deviations from intended intervention (Domain 2), the overall risk of bias for this outcome (frequency of delivery after 48 hours) is **High**, per RoB 2.0 guidance stating that a "High" judgement in any single domain results in an overall "High" risk of bias, regardless of judgements in other domains.
