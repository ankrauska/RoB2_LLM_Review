# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Surgical Time

**Study design:** Individually-randomized parallel-group trial
**Experimental intervention:** Percutaneous microwave ablation (Group A)
**Comparator intervention:** Laparoscopic resection (Group B)
**Outcome assessed:** Surgical time
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:**
- The article states: "Using the random number table method, 90 cases were divided into group A and group B (n=45)." This indicates a random component was used (1.1 = Probably yes).
- There is no description whatsoever of allocation concealment procedures. No mention of a central randomization system, sealed envelopes, or any mechanism preventing foreknowledge of assignment. This warrants a response of "No information" to signalling question 1.2, which, combined with lack of detail on concealment, precludes a "Low risk" judgement.
- Baseline characteristics (age, sex distribution, tumor size, nodularity) appear broadly similar between groups ("did not show significant differences in general information (P>0.05)"), and no evidence suggests problems with randomization on this basis (1.3 = No).
- Per the RoB2 algorithm: random sequence generation Y/PY + no information on concealment + no baseline imbalance problem → **Some concerns** (this matches the row: "Any response(1.1) / NI(1.2) / N/PN/NI(1.3) → Some concerns").

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- Surgical time is inherently a variable directly determined by the intervention procedure itself (ablation vs. laparoscopic surgery), and by design, participants and personnel (surgeons/proceduralists) could not be blinded to which intervention was being performed — this is an open-label, unblinded surgical comparison (2.1 = Yes, 2.2 = Yes).
- Because both participants and personnel were aware of the intervention, we must consider whether deviations arose because of trial context (2.3). There is no mention of any protocol deviations, non-protocol interventions, or crossover for the surgical time outcome specifically. However, no explicit discussion of fidelity to a standardized operative protocol exists; there's no information on whether any tailoring or non-protocol technique variations affected timing.
- More critically, given the outcome is "surgical time" — a variable intrinsically tied to which procedure was performed — the two interventions are fundamentally different procedures with different inherent time requirements (microwave ablation via percutaneous needle insertion vs. full laparoscopic resection with pneumoperitoneum, trocar insertion, and tissue resection). This is not a "deviation" issue in the classic sense, but the lack of blinding combined with no information on standardization of surgical techniques or learning curve/experience balance between surgical teams raises concern that performance differences (e.g., surgeon experience, effort, technique variation) could differentially affect timing beyond the intended intervention contrast.
- No statistical adjustment or intention-to-treat framework is relevant here since this is a procedural, single time-point measurement with no possibility of "crossover" — but the analysis itself does not appear to have excluded any participants (all n=45 per group with data presented), so signalling question 2.6 (appropriate analysis) = Yes.
- The primary driver of "High risk" here is that with an unblinded procedural comparison, and no reported standardization of operative protocols/team experience/technique consistency, there is substantive risk that deviations in technique or effort (arising from the trial context — e.g., surgeons possibly being less experienced/practiced or applying variable technique in one arm) could have influenced the recorded surgical time, and this cannot be ruled out. Given the mapping table default (unawareness cannot be claimed; deviations plausible; no information on balance or magnitude of effect on outcome), the assessment defaults to **High risk**.

*Note: If review authors consider that surgical time being intrinsically and appropriately determined by the assigned intervention (i.e., a direct consequence of intervention assignment rather than a "deviation") does not constitute bias due to deviations, an alternative judgement of "Low risk" could be argued. However, per strict application of the signalling questions (awareness of intervention = Yes for both participants/personnel with no mitigating information about balanced deviations), the conservative and tool-consistent default is High risk.*

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- All 90 randomized participants (45 in each group) appear to have contributed data to the surgical time analysis, as reported in Table 1, with no mention of exclusions, dropouts, or missing data for this outcome.
- The paper does not report any losses to follow-up or exclusions prior to measurement of surgical time (an intraoperative variable that would be recorded for essentially all patients undergoing the procedure).
- Since surgical time is captured at the time of the procedure itself, dropout after surgery would not typically affect its measurement. There is no indication of missingness for this specific outcome (3.1 = Yes).

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:**
- The method of measuring surgical time (presumably recorded by surgical/theatre staff or anesthesia records) is not inherently inappropriate for this objective, easily measurable variable (4.1 = No).
- However, measurement of surgical time did differ structurally between groups by the nature of the intervention (percutaneous ablation vs. laparoscopic resection), though this reflects the intervention itself rather than differential measurement methodology — this is not considered a measurement bias issue per se, so 4.2 = No (the measurement method — i.e., timing the procedure — was consistent in approach across groups even though procedures differed).
- The outcome assessors here would be the surgical/operative team recording start/end times of the procedure — an "observer-reported outcome not involving judgement" since surgical time is typically an objective record (start-to-finish time) rather than subject to interpretation.
- Critically, outcome assessors (surgical personnel recording the time) were NOT blinded, as they were the same personnel performing/aware of the procedure being conducted (4.3 = Yes, personnel aware).
- However, because surgical time is an objective, mechanically recorded variable (clock time) with little room for subjective judgement, knowledge of intervention assignment is unlikely to influence the recorded value (analogous to all-cause mortality) (4.4 = Probably No). This would suggest "Low risk," but because the paper provides no explicit description of how surgical time was defined/recorded (e.g., start and end points of "surgical time" are not defined — does it include anesthesia induction, positioning, etc.?), and no information on who specifically recorded these times or with what precision/standardization across groups, there remains some uncertainty.
- Given the ambiguity in operational definition and recording procedures, but the fundamentally objective nature of the outcome, a judgement of **Some concerns** rather than "Low risk" or "High risk" is most appropriate.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:**
- No trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan was identified/provided for this study (the task materials reference a trial registration doc_id, but no specific registry information, e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov number, is presented within the article itself, and no protocol details are described in the text).
- The methods section states: "Surgical time, intraoperative blood loss and incidence of complications were recorded for the two groups," indicating surgical time was a pre-specified outcome measured and reported in a straightforward manner (single measurement, single analysis, reported for both groups with means, SDs, t-value and P-value in Table 1).
- There is no evidence of selective reporting from multiple outcome measurements or multiple analytical approaches specifically for surgical time — it appears to be reported once, using a single metric (t-test comparison of means).
- However, because no protocol or trial registry information was available/accessible to confirm that this was the only measurement/analysis method planned in advance (i.e., we cannot fully verify the absence of selective reporting due to lack of access to a pre-specified analysis plan), a judgement of "Low risk" cannot be fully justified.
- Per the RoB2 decision algorithm: no information on adherence to a pre-specified plan (5.1 = No information) + no evidence of selection from multiple outcome measures (5.2 = No) + no evidence of selection from multiple analyses (5.3 = No) → **Some concerns**.
