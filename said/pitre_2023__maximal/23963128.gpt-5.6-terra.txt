**Outcome assessed:** effect of assigned level of supervision (direct, immediately available, or distant) on **patient care during the supervised simulation**, measured by the **team checklist score**. The assessment targets the **effect of assignment to supervision at baseline** (rather than the effect of adherence to the assigned supervision strategy).

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the report states that residents were randomized but does not describe either a genuinely random sequence-generation method or concealment of allocation. Baseline characteristics do not, however, show a clear imbalance suggesting a failed randomization process.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The article states only that: “**Residents were randomized to one of three levels of supervision**.” It does not state how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, blocked randomization) or how allocation was concealed from those enrolling residents.  
   - Thus, there is **no information** to establish that allocation was random and **no information** to establish that it was concealed until enrolment/assignment. Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment generally leads to *some concerns* unless there is a strong contextual basis for assuming adequate methods.
   - Table 1 shows broadly similar groups in age, sex, postgraduate level, ICU experience, specialty, and confidence. For example, postgraduate year 1–2 residents comprised 67%, 69%, and 61% of the direct, immediately available, and distant groups, respectively. There is no marked imbalance in group sizes either (18, 16, and 19 residents).
   - Although prior simulation experience appears somewhat higher in the distant-supervision group (median 3 sessions versus 1.5 in the other groups), this isolated difference in a small three-arm trial is not sufficient to indicate that randomization was compromised.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the supervision strategy. Participants and supervising fellows were necessarily aware of supervision assignment, but there is no evidence of non-protocol deviations caused by the trial context that would have differentially affected the team checklist outcome. The analysis appears to retain residents in their randomized supervision groups.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - Blinding of residents and fellows was not feasible because the intervention was defined by the supervisor’s physical proximity:
     - direct: supervisor in the simulation room from the beginning;
     - immediately available: supervisor initially reachable by phone and able to attend within 1 minute;
     - distant: supervisor available only by phone.
   - Therefore, residents and fellows were aware of assigned supervision. However, awareness alone does not produce bias for the assignment effect under RoB 2; bias requires deviations from intended intervention that arose because of the trial context, affected the outcome, and were imbalanced between groups.
   - The observed behaviors appear to be part of the intended supervision strategies rather than trial-context deviations. For example, in the immediately-available group, “**two residents did not call their supervisor, four residents obtained advice from their supervisor by phone only, and 10 residents were eventually assisted by their supervisor at the bedside**.” This reflects residents’ use of an immediately available supervisor, rather than evidence that the allocated strategy was replaced by an unintended intervention.
   - Likewise, the direct-supervision condition inherently allowed fellows to contribute to care, and the team checklist score was explicitly designed to capture care performed by “**any member of the team (resident, fellow, and nurse)**.” Fellow contribution is therefore part of the intended intervention and causal pathway, not an unintended cointervention.
   - The reported analyses compare the three randomized groups using one-way ANOVA, and the group denominators in Table 2 correspond to all 53 randomized residents (direct 18, immediately available 16, distant 19). There is no indication of an as-treated or per-protocol analysis excluding residents because of their actual use of supervision.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because team checklist-score data appear to have been available for all randomized residents.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - Fifty-three residents were recruited and randomized: 18 to direct supervision, 16 to immediately available supervision, and 19 to distant supervision.
   - These same denominators are reported for the supervised-scenario checklist outcome in Table 2: direct (n = 18), immediately available (n = 16), and distant (n = 19).
   - The paper reports a team checklist-score estimate for every group: direct 72% (SD 12%), immediately available 77% (SD 10%), and distant 61% (SD 11%).
   - No participant withdrawals, exclusions from the checklist analysis, missing outcome values, or imputation procedures are reported for this outcome. The available information therefore indicates outcome data for all, or essentially all, randomized residents.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns. The checklist-based method was applied across groups and is reasonably appropriate for assessing completion of predefined clinical tasks. However, outcome assessors were not reported to be blinded, and blinding would have been difficult because the supervision condition was visually and behaviorally evident in the simulation recordings. Although there is no indication that outcome assessment was actually biased, some judgement was involved in deciding whether checklist tasks had been completed.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The outcome was measured using a scenario-specific checklist: “**The number of clinical tasks completed during the supervised scenario was used to assign a team checklist score. A checklist task was considered done if performed by any member of the team**.”
   - The same conceptual measurement approach—a percentage of pre-specified checklist tasks completed by the team—was used in all intervention groups. There is no evidence that different outcome thresholds or fundamentally different ascertainment methods were used by supervision arm.
   - The study used video recordings and trained independent raters: “**The raters then independently reviewed and rated all the videotaped performance of the participants. Any discrepancy in the checklist scores was resolved by a third independent assessor**.”
   - However, the report does not state that raters were blinded to assigned supervision. In practice, blinding was probably not possible because direct supervision involved the fellow being physically present in the room, whereas distant supervision involved telephone access only.
   - Checklist task completion is more structured and less subjective than a global impression of performance. Nevertheless, determining whether a clinical task was adequately completed can involve some judgement, and assessors’ awareness of the supervision strategy could potentially influence classification of marginal actions.
   - There is no evidence that assessors had a particular interest in favoring one supervision strategy, nor evidence that they deliberately or likely differentially scored the groups. This supports *some concerns* rather than high risk of bias.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the publication describes the outcome and analysis but does not provide a pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The available material does not establish that the reported team checklist result was selected from multiple possible outcome measures, time points, scenarios, or analyses on the basis of its findings.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The paper prespecifies, within its Methods section, that patient care during supervised scenarios would be assessed using “**a team checklist score, the time to stabilization of abnormal vital signs, and the proportion of achievement of definitive management**.”
   - It also describes the planned analysis: “**we conducted a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the team checklist scores of the supervised scenario with the level of clinical supervision … as the independent variable**.”
   - The reported result corresponds to that described outcome and analysis: team checklist scores were reported for all three supervision groups, with an overall p value (p = 0.0013). The paper also reports the other stated patient-care outcomes, rather than reporting only the favorable checklist finding.
   - Nevertheless, a methods description in the published article is not equivalent to a prospectively finalized analysis plan. No protocol, dated statistical analysis plan, or sufficiently detailed registration information is available in the supplied material to verify that the specific checklist outcome, scenario handling, outcome metric, and ANOVA approach were finalized before unblinded data were available.
   - The study involved three pairs of scenarios and several outcomes, providing potential opportunities to select among measurements or analyses. There is no direct evidence that such selection occurred, but it cannot be excluded. Under RoB 2, this warrants *some concerns*, rather than low risk of bias.
