1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was explicitly computer generated, and allocation was concealed by use of an independent third party that prepared and retained the treatment-code information. The study was placebo controlled with indistinguishable tablets, further supporting that allocation was not disclosed at enrolment.

   The report states: “**Individuals were randomized by a computer-generated list**,” which “**was used for packaging of the tablets and then maintained by a third party that was not involved in the conduction of the study**.” It also states that “**both patients and investigators were blinded**.”

   Baseline characteristics were reported for all 60 randomized participants (30 per group). Although there were numerical differences in some individual comorbidities (e.g., congestive heart failure: 12 versus 8; cerebrovascular disease: 1 versus 6), group sizes were exactly balanced and there was no compelling imbalance in key prognostic factors that was clearly incompatible with chance. The authors report that “**there was no significant difference in any baseline parameter between the two groups**.” Thus, baseline differences do not suggest a failure of randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants, investigators, and apparently those delivering routine study care were blinded through use of an identical placebo. The active bicarbonate and placebo tablets had identical appearance, packaging, labelling, and taste: “**The appearance, packaging, and labeling of the tablets were identical. Both kinds of tablets had artificial mint flavor to give an identical taste. Both patients and investigators were blinded.**”

   There is no indication of important deviations caused by the trial context that would have differentially affected SGA. The dialysis regimen and clinical management were intended to be the same between groups apart from study treatment: “**Except for the study medication, the clinical management was the same as in other patients.**”

   Seven participants discontinued study medication because of adverse effects or symptoms (four bicarbonate and three placebo), but this was relatively balanced. Crucially, outcome follow-up continued despite treatment discontinuation: “**Although study medication was stopped in these patients, biochemical tests and nutritional assessment were continued for 52 wk according to the study protocol.**” The trial also states that “**Analyses were intention to treat irrespective of adherence to treatment regimen**.” This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to sodium bicarbonate versus placebo.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:** The report does not provide a participant flow diagram, numbers assessed for SGA at each time point, or an explicit account of missing SGA observations by group and visit. Therefore, it is not possible to confirm that SGA data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants.

   The paper reports events that could prevent later SGA measurement: during follow-up, “**2 patients of the treatment group died**” and “**5 patients of the placebo group died**”; one patient in each group transferred to long-term haemodialysis, and one treatment-group participant received a transplant. These events mean that SGA data at later visits were likely unavailable for at least some participants. The mortality imbalance (2 versus 5) also raises concern that the extent and reasons for missing SGA data could differ between groups.

   Although nutritional assessments reportedly continued after study medication discontinuation, this does not resolve missing data due to death, transplantation, transfer from peritoneal dialysis, or other missed assessments. No sensitivity analysis, imputation strategy, or analysis demonstrating robustness to missing SGA data was reported. Because nutritional status and SGA are plausibly related to illness severity, hospitalization, and death, missingness could depend on the true underlying SGA value. The absence of adequate reporting of SGA completeness and handling of missing repeated measurements therefore creates a high risk that missing data could have biased the longitudinal SGA result.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** SGA is an established and appropriate nutritional-status measure in peritoneal-dialysis patients, and it was the prespecified primary outcome. The trial used the same structured four-item, seven-point SGA method at the same scheduled visits in both groups.

   Importantly, outcome assessors were blinded to treatment allocation and biochemical findings: “**SGA was performed at 0, 12, 24, 36, and 52 wk by two trained observers who were blinded from the treatment group allocation and biochemical results of the patients.**” The report also describes assessor training and good agreement: “**the two observers were trained to achieve a Cohen’s kappa concordant coefficient for agreement of 0.84**.”

   Since SGA involves subjective clinical judgement, awareness of treatment could potentially influence assessment. However, the use of blinded trained observers, identical placebo tablets, and identical planned assessment schedules makes differential outcome measurement unlikely.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication clearly identifies overall SGA score as the primary outcome: “**The primary outcome measure was the SGA score.**” It reports SGA at the planned scheduled assessments (0, 12, 24, 36, and 52 weeks) and reports a repeated-measures analysis for the longitudinal SGA outcome. These features reduce concern that the SGA outcome itself was entirely selectively omitted.

   However, no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the material provided, and the supplied trial-registration information is not available for review here. Consequently, it cannot be verified whether the reported repeated-measures ANOVA model, adjustment for Charlson Comorbidity Index, choice of time points, handling of missing observations, and reported post-hoc comparisons were all prespecified before unblinded outcome data were available.

   There were multiple possible SGA-related results that could have been selected, including global SGA, four component scores, several follow-up time points, and potentially alternative analyses (final values, changes from baseline, adjusted versus unadjusted analyses). The paper reports global SGA and component outcomes, but without a dated protocol or statistical analysis plan it is not possible to exclude selection of the reported analysis or time-point comparisons on the basis of the observed results. Therefore, the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is **some concerns**, rather than low risk or high risk.
