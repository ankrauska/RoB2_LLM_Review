1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation procedure appears to have included a random element and to have kept the allocation concealed at the point of enrolment. The report states that the coordinator prepared “**100 sealed nontransparent envelopes**,” containing allocations to intervention or control, and that allocation was made by the participant or caregiver “**drawing one envelope**.” Although the report does not explicitly state that the envelopes were sequentially numbered, shuffled, or prepared by an independent person, use of sealed nontransparent envelopes drawn by participants provides reasonable assurance that the forthcoming allocation was not known before allocation.

   The unequal final allocation ratio (2:1 for the final 14 participants) was explicitly planned to allow more participants to receive the intervention, rather than appearing to result from manipulation of allocation. The authors acknowledge this change in allocation ratio.

   Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable. None of the reported between-group differences in patient characteristics, dementia severity, functional status, caregiver characteristics, or caregiver mental welfare was statistically compelling. For example, severe dementia was somewhat more frequent in the intervention group (36% versus 24%), but the overall dementia-stage comparison had *P* = .244. These differences are compatible with chance in a trial of 100 participants and do not indicate a failure of randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants, caregivers, and the dementia family care coordinator necessarily knew whether the intervention was received. The intervention was an overt, intensive case-management programme involving counselling, home visits, training courses, coordination of services, and telephone availability; blinding was not feasible.

   However, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding creates bias only when deviations from protocol arose because of the trial context, affected the outcome, and were imbalanced between groups. The reported differences in support and service coordination were the intended intervention, rather than unintended co-interventions or implementation failures. The article describes the intervention as systematically delivering case management and arranging available services according to patients’ needs. It does not report evidence that control participants received inappropriate intervention-programme support, that intervention participants were denied intended support, or that trial personnel introduced unplanned, outcome-relevant deviations because of knowledge of group assignment.

   The analysis also appears consistent with assignment to randomized group. All 100 randomized participants were followed, and institutionalization analyses compared the allocated intervention and control groups using Kaplan–Meier methods and a time-dependent Cox regression model. There is no indication that participants were analysed according to treatment actually received or excluded because of non-adherence.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   c. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   d. **Rationale:** There were no conventional losses to follow-up: “**There were no dropouts from the study during the 2-year follow-up**.” Institutionalization and death were obtained from the Social Insurance Institution register, and the participant flow diagram accounts for all randomized participants at one and two years. This substantially reduces concern about unobserved follow-up status.

   Nevertheless, the time-to-institutionalization analysis censored follow-up at death: “**In the survival analyses, times were censored at death if the patient died in community care before institutionalization.**” Death is a competing event for institutionalization and may be related to underlying health status and to the likelihood or timing of institutionalization. A total of 17 participants died in community care during follow-up (9/53 intervention and 8/47 control), which is not negligible.

   Mortality proportions were similar between groups at two years (17% in each group), which provides no clear indication of differential censoring by allocation. However, the report does not provide evidence that censoring at death was non-informative, nor does it report a competing-risk analysis or sensitivity analysis examining the impact of deaths on the institutionalization result. Thus, missingness/censoring could depend on the underlying outcome process, but it is not clearly demonstrated that this produced material bias.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Institutionalization was an appropriate and relatively objective outcome for the stated aim of prolonging community living. The main outcome was defined as “**the time to institutionalization … from the enrollment of patients in the study to their placement in long-term institutional care**.” Institutionalization and death information were obtained from the Social Insurance Institution register, so ascertainment was not dependent solely on participant or caregiver report.

   Importantly, the decision to institutionalize was made by external municipal social and healthcare experts, and the article states that these decision-makers “**were blind to the allocation and generally unaware that a patient was even participating in the study**.” This is strong protection against differential outcome assessment or provider decisions influenced by knowledge of allocation.

   The same institutionalization definition and ascertainment procedures appear to have been applied in both groups. Therefore, there is little reason to believe that measurement or ascertainment of institutionalization differed systematically by intervention group.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Time to institutionalization was clearly identified as the primary outcome in the article: “**The main outcome measure in the study was the time to institutionalization**.” The report provides several relevant results, including institutionalization counts at one and two years, Kaplan–Meier estimates at 6, 12, and 24 months, survival curves, and a time-dependent Cox regression analysis. The use of a time-dependent Cox model was explained as necessary because “**the assumption of proportional hazards was not met**.”

   However, there is no accessible pre-specified statistical analysis plan in the material provided, and the publication does not establish that the specific Cox model, the selected reporting time points, handling of death as censoring, or subgroup analyses by dementia severity were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The report includes descriptive subgroup survival curves and emphasizes apparent benefit in severe dementia, but it is unclear whether these subgroup analyses were pre-specified.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported institutionalization result was selected from multiple analyses or outcome definitions because it was favourable. Nonetheless, in the absence of a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan or usable registration information for this outcome, selective reporting cannot be fully excluded.
