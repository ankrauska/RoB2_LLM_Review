1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Allocation was described as randomized and was concealed from the operators until enrolment. The study used a split-mouth, cross-quadrant design, with the etch/primer system randomly allocated to the upper right quadrant and the corresponding allocation determining the opposite quadrants. The report states:

   > “Randomization for allocation of either of the two etch and primer systems to the upper right quadrant was undertaken using a block randomization method…”

   and:

   > “The operators were unaware of the assignment until the patient was entered into the trial.”

   Thus, there is reasonable evidence that allocation was concealed until the participant had entered the trial. Although the exact random sequence-generation procedure within blocks is not described in full detail, use of a stated block-randomization method is compatible with random allocation. The within-patient split-mouth design also inherently controls patient-level baseline characteristics, because each patient received both bonding systems in different quadrants. There is no indication of an important baseline imbalance or of manipulation of allocation.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Patients were unaware of which bonding system had been used:

   > “The patients were unaware which system had been used on each side of the mouth…”

   Operators could not be blinded because the two procedures differed:

   > “…as the two systems had different modes of application, it was not possible to blind the operators to the type of system being used.”

   However, lack of operator blinding alone does not establish bias for the effect of assignment. The report does not identify deviations from the protocol that arose because of the trial context, such as differential use of important cointerventions, differential implementation of the assigned bonding procedures, or deliberate departures from the allocated system.

   The bonding procedures were described in detail and applied according to manufacturer instructions. All brackets were followed under the same routine orthodontic treatment conditions. The analysis retained the participant who moved away after 7 months, using their observed follow-up time in the survival analysis:

   > “His data was still included in the analysis, as it was considered that it still provided useful information on bracket survival rates.”

   The analyses were consistent with estimating the effect of assignment: brackets were analysed according to their allocated etch/primer system, with regression and survival analyses adjusted for clustering within mouths. There is no evidence that participants or brackets were analysed according to a post-randomization treatment actually received rather than their allocated treatment.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data were available for nearly all participants and brackets over the planned 12-month period. The report states:

   > “All patients were observed for the entire 12-month period apart from one 13-year-old male who moved to another city after 7 months of observation, during which time no bracket failures occurred.”

   Therefore, only one of 39 participants had incomplete follow-up. His observed follow-up contributed to the survival analysis, rather than being excluded. The reason for incomplete follow-up—moving to another city—does not itself suggest that missingness depended on the true bracket-failure outcome. No differential loss to follow-up between etch/primer systems is possible at the participant level in the same way as in a conventional parallel-group design, because both systems were used within each participant.

   Given the very small amount of incomplete follow-up and the use of time-to-event methods that incorporated available follow-up, it is unlikely that missing data materially biased the comparison of failure rates.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Bracket failure is a relatively objective clinical outcome: a bracket was either still bonded or had failed. The investigators prospectively recorded failures during monitoring:

   > “If a bond failed, the tooth on which the failure occurred, the date of failure, and the amount of adhesive remaining on the tooth… were recorded.”

   The same outcome definition and monitoring procedure were applied to both etch/primer systems. There is no indication that the frequency or method of ascertainment differed by allocated bonding system.

   Operators were aware of the system used, but the outcome—physical bond failure of an orthodontic bracket—has limited scope for subjective interpretation. Knowledge of allocation is therefore unlikely to have influenced whether a failed bracket was identified or recorded. The adhesive remnant index involved more judgement, but this assessment concerns the failure-rate outcome, not the mode-of-failure outcome.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The article clearly reports the principal failure-rate comparison over the planned 12-month period, including numerical failure rates, a statistical comparison, and a survival-analysis result:

   > “During the 12-month observation period the bracket failure rate was 11.2 per cent for the Transbond™ Plus SEP and 3.9 per cent for the conventional… primer.”

   > “Transbond™ Plus SEP was found to have a significantly higher failure rate than the conventional… system (P = 0.001).”

   Nevertheless, the publication does not provide a dated prespecified statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the analyses for failure rate were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. While the trial describes a sample-size calculation and its planned 12-month observation period, it reports several possible analyses of bracket failure, including crude failure proportions, logistic regression, Kaplan–Meier curves, and Cox proportional-hazards models. The available report does not establish which analysis was prespecified as the primary analysis for the failure-rate outcome.

   In the absence of a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized protocol or statistical analysis plan confirming the prespecified outcome measurement and analysis, it cannot be excluded that the reported numerical result was selected from multiple possible analyses or time-to-event summaries. There is no direct evidence of selective reporting, but the lack of a prespecified analysis plan leads to **some concerns** under RoB 2.
