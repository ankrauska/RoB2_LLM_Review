# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Effect of Add-on Gabapentin on Opioid Withdrawal Symptoms
## Outcome: Severity of subjective withdrawal symptoms (Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale, SOWS)
## Effect of Interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Was the allocation sequence random?* The report states: "Subjects were assigned randomly to receive adjunctive treatment with either gabapentin or placebo under double-blind conditions, using a randomization automated system on a one-to-one basis." An "automated system" implies a random-number-generating process, supporting a judgement of "Probably yes." However, no further detail (e.g., simple vs. blocked randomization, software used) is provided.
- *Was the allocation sequence concealed?* "During the study, the randomization list was held securely, and released only after study completion," and gabapentin/placebo were "dispensed in identical appearing capsules." This suggests reasonable concealment, but the mechanism of concealment at the point of enrollment (e.g., central allocation, sequentially numbered containers) is not explicitly described — "Probably yes" rather than definitive "Yes."
- *Baseline imbalance:* Baseline SOWS scores differed noticeably between groups (Gabapentin 12.6 ± 7.3 vs. Placebo 14.9 ± 10.4), a ~18% relative difference in the primary outcome at baseline, in a small sample (n=20/group). No formal statistical test for baseline SOWS is reported, and the analysis subsequently statistically controls for other covariates (age, methadone dose) but not baseline SOWS score itself. While no p-value confirms a "significant" imbalance, the magnitude of the difference, combined with limited reporting detail on randomization procedures and vague allocation concealment methods, raises some concern that this could reflect chance in a small trial or a possible weakness in the randomization process.
- Overall: sequence random ("Probably yes"), concealment "Probably yes," but with an appreciable and unexplained baseline imbalance in the outcome of interest and incomplete reporting of methods → **Some concerns**.

---

### 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Were participants/personnel aware of the intervention?* The trial was double-blind, with gabapentin and placebo "dispensed in identical appearing capsules," and "patients who were randomized to the placebo group took the same number of capsules as those who were assigned to the treatment group." This supports blinding of participants and personnel (answer "No"/"Probably no" to awareness), which is the standard pathway toward a low-risk judgement in this part of the domain.
- *Deviations arising from trial context:* Despite blinding efforts, "one patient in the placebo group was excluded at day 14 and two others were excluded at day 17 because of non-compliance with the scheduled treatment programme and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter (OTC) drugs." The authors themselves speculate: "The non-compliance and self-medication in the placebo group may be due to the greater severity of withdrawal symptoms." This suggests that deviations (self-medication, non-adherence to the trial protocol) occurred asymmetrically between groups and may plausibly be linked to the trial context (unblinding via symptom severity, i.e., participants suspecting placebo assignment due to lack of symptom relief).
- *Did deviations affect the outcome, and were they balanced?* These deviations directly relate to the withdrawal-symptom outcome (patients discontinuing to self-medicate for symptoms) and were **not balanced** between groups (occurred only in the placebo arm).
- *Analysis*: It is unclear whether a full ITT analysis (including all originally randomized participants, regardless of adherence) was used for the SOWS outcome, or whether participants who deviated/self-medicated were simply removed from the analytic dataset (the n's decrease disproportionately in the placebo group over time in Table 1 and Table 3). This ambiguity, combined with unbalanced trial-context-related deviations that could affect the outcome, prevents a "Low risk" judgement, but the deviations were relatively few in number (3/20 placebo participants) and blinding was otherwise well maintained, so this does not reach "High risk."

### 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Was outcome data available for all or most participants?* No. By Day 18, the placebo group had only 17/20 participants with SOWS data (15% missing), while the gabapentin group retained all 20 (0% missing) — a differential dropout rate between arms.
- *Reasons for missingness:* The missing data were due to "non-compliance with the scheduled treatment programme and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter (OTC) drugs," which the authors attribute to "the greater severity of withdrawal symptoms" in the placebo group. This is a direct, author-acknowledged link between missingness and the true (unfavorable) value of the withdrawal-symptom outcome.
- *Could missingness depend on the true value of the outcome?* Yes — explicitly, per the authors' own explanation, participants with worse withdrawal symptoms were more likely to drop out and self-medicate, and this occurred disproportionately in the placebo arm.
- *Evidence the result is unbiased despite this?* No sensitivity analyses, imputation, or other statistical corrections for missing data are reported; the repeated-measures ANOVA (Table 2) simply appears to use available cases at each time point (varying n's in Table 1), a complete-case approach.
- Given (i) differential attrition between groups, (ii) reasons for missingness plausibly related to the true (worse) outcome values, and (iii) no correction/sensitivity analysis, this meets the criteria for **High risk of bias** due to missing outcome data — particularly concerning since the syndrome-severity outcome was likely systematically worse among those excluded from the placebo arm, which would tend to bias results *toward* an overestimate of gabapentin's benefit (i.e., the sickest placebo patients were removed from analysis).

---

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Appropriateness of method:* The SOWS is described as "a valid and reliable indicator of the severity of the opiate withdrawal syndrome over a wide range of common symptoms," an appropriate, validated participant-reported outcome measure for this purpose.
- *Did method differ between arms?* No; the same scale, administered at the same six time-points, was used identically in both groups.
- *Awareness of outcome assessors (participants, since this is self-report):* Because the trial was double-blind with identical-appearing capsules, participants (the outcome assessors for this participant-reported outcome) were very likely unaware of their allocation. No mention is made of unblinding due to distinguishable side effects, and gabapentin does not typically produce an easily identifiable signature effect that would reveal allocation to patients undergoing concurrent methadone-assisted detoxification with multiple symptomatic medications.
- *Could assessment be influenced by knowledge of intervention?* Since participants were plausibly blinded, and this is a subjective, participant-reported measure, risk of differential reporting bias is low, assuming blinding held (no evidence to the contrary is presented).
- Overall, method appropriate, consistent across arms, and outcome assessors (patients) likely blinded → **Low risk of bias**.

---

### 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- The methods section pre-specifies SOWS as the outcome measure, assessed "at six time-points during the study" (baseline, day 4, 7, 10, 18, and 21), and the results section reports data from all six time-points (Table 1, Figure 1) and all 16 individual withdrawal symptoms comprising the scale (Table 3), consistent with what was specified in the methods.
- There is no evidence of selective reporting of a subset of time points, subscales, or alternative outcome definitions; the full symptom checklist and total score are reported comprehensively, including non-significant results, and the authors explicitly report and discuss the lack of statistically significant differences rather than omitting unfavorable findings.
- Both an omnibus repeated-measures ANOVA (Table 2) and a Mann-Whitney U-test on total score are reported, both consistently showing non-significant results, without evidence that alternate analyses were tried and suppressed.
- Without access to a prospectively registered protocol/statistical analysis plan pre-dating unblinded data, some uncertainty remains, but the internal consistency and completeness of reporting (including null results) do not raise substantive concern for outcome- or analysis-selection bias for this specific outcome → **Low risk of bias**.
