1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The report states that eyes were “assigned randomly” to ranibizumab plus prompt versus deferred laser, but it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated or concealed:

   > “At study enrollment, 180 eyes were assigned to ranibizumab plus prompt focal/grid laser treatment and 181 to ranibizumab plus deferred laser treatment.”

   Therefore:

   - **Random sequence generation:** No information is provided on whether a computer-generated sequence, random-number table, permuted blocks, or another genuinely random method was used.
   - **Allocation concealment:** No information is provided about concealment of assignments until enrolment (e.g., central randomization or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes).
   - **Baseline balance:** The supplied Table 1 compares 5-year completers with non-completers *within* each treatment group, rather than providing a complete baseline comparison of the originally randomized prompt and deferred groups. Thus, it cannot adequately establish whether baseline imbalances between randomized groups suggested a problem with randomization.

   There is no direct evidence that the randomization process was flawed, but the absence of information about both sequence generation and allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to prompt versus deferred laser at baseline, regardless of subsequent receipt of laser or injections.

   Participants and treating personnel were probably aware of whether laser was delivered promptly or deferred, since the interventions were not plausibly masked. However, the relevant treatment strategies were explicitly protocol defined. In particular, deferred laser was intended to be withheld for at least 24 weeks and then could be initiated under specified criteria:

   > “Laser in the deferral group had to be delayed for at least 24 weeks after initiating anti-VEGF therapy. However, at or after 24 weeks, laser treatment could be given if there was persistent DME … that had not improved after at least 2 consecutive injections…”

   Treatment and retreatment decisions were governed by protocol-specified criteria:

   > “An assessment for retreatment with ranibizumab, retreatment with laser, or initiation of laser treatment for the deferred group, occurred at each visit. The treatment decision was based on protocol specified criteria…”

   The greater number of injections in the deferred-laser group and the fact that 44% of deferred-group eyes eventually received laser are expected features of the assigned management strategies, rather than deviations from them:

   > “By the 5-year visit, 44% of the eyes in the ranibizumab plus deferred laser treatment group had received at least one session of focal/grid laser treatment…”

   The primary longitudinal analysis compared groups as assigned and used all available follow-up data:

   > “A longitudinal analysis was performed to compare visual acuity … using all available visit data.”

   Although analyses excluded participants from one site whose eyes were subsequently judged not to meet the baseline OCT eligibility criterion, this appears to concern post-randomization confirmation of baseline eligibility rather than an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis. There is no evidence that participants were analysed according to treatment actually received or that exclusions due to treatment adherence were substantial enough to materially bias the assignment effect.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   c. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Five-year visual-acuity data were unavailable for approximately one-quarter of originally randomized participants, excluding deaths:

   > “Excluding deaths, the 5-year completion rate was 76% of the 163 original participants randomized to the ranibizumab + prompt laser group and 74% of the 150 original participants randomized to the ranibizumab + deferred laser group.”

   Thus, outcome data were not available for all or nearly all randomized participants. Missingness was largely related to the extension phase:

   > “Because the fourth and fifth years were extensions of the original study, some patients chose not to consent to the longer follow-up.”

   The proportion without a 5-year assessment was broadly similar between groups (24% versus 26%), which somewhat reduces concern about strongly differential attrition. However, there were identifiable differences between completers and non-completers, including age, previous panretinal photocoagulation, lens status, and diabetic-retinopathy severity:

   > “Some imbalances between completers versus non-completers were noted (age, prior PRP, lens status, diabetic retinopathy severity level)…”

   The authors adjusted for measured potential confounders, but acknowledged that this cannot rule out bias caused by unavailable outcome data:

   > “It is not possible to know whether incomplete data were related to unobserved factors that could have affected the results in either direction.”

   Because visual acuity and disease status could plausibly influence willingness or ability to continue follow-up, missingness could depend on the unobserved 5-year visual-acuity outcome. There is no reported sensitivity analysis demonstrating that the result was robust to plausible assumptions about missing data. Nevertheless, the similar attrition proportions between groups and use of longitudinal models with available data support a judgement of some concerns rather than high risk.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The outcome was best-corrected visual acuity, measured as an ETDRS letter score. This is an appropriate and objective standardized method for measuring visual acuity. The same visual-acuity measurement approach and follow-up schedule were used in both randomized groups.

   Importantly, the report states that visual-acuity examiners were masked:

   > “Some of the strengths of this study include … masking of the visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians.”

   Therefore, although participants and treating clinicians were likely aware of whether prompt laser was delivered, the individuals assessing visual acuity were masked to treatment allocation. This substantially limits the possibility that knowledge of allocation influenced measurement or recording of visual-acuity scores.

   There is no indication that ascertainment of visual acuity differed systematically between prompt- and deferred-laser groups. The method was appropriate, comparable across groups, and undertaken by masked assessors.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The report identifies best-corrected visual acuity at the 5-year visit as the main outcome:

   > “Main Outcome Measures—Best-corrected visual acuity at the 5-year visit.”

   It also reports the principal continuous visual-acuity result, categorical visual-acuity outcomes, and longitudinal visual-acuity estimates. The reported analyses are broadly consistent with the stated objective of comparing prompt versus deferred laser through five years.

   However, a pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before access to unblinded outcome data is not provided in the supplied materials. Although the article states that the protocol was available through the DRCR.net website, the available text does not establish that the 5-year visual-acuity analysis, its precise time point, the longitudinal-model specification, covariate adjustment strategy, truncation of visual-acuity changes, and categorical analyses were all pre-specified before unblinded 5-year data were available:

   > “The protocol is available on the DRCR.net website…”

   Multiple potentially eligible visual-acuity analyses were possible, including analysis of 5-year values, change from baseline, longitudinal data, adjusted versus unadjusted models, categorical gain/loss thresholds, and subgroup analyses. The publication presents several of these analyses, but no dated pre-specified analysis plan is available here to exclude selective choice among them.

   There is no clear evidence that the reported 5-year visual-acuity result was selected because of its direction, magnitude, or statistical significance. Consequently, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk of bias.
