**Outcome assessed:** Progression (change from baseline to day 90) in the Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale (FMMS), comparing fluoxetine 20 mg daily plus usual physiotherapy versus placebo plus usual physiotherapy. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was computer-generated, allocation was concealed, and baseline differences do not indicate a failure of randomization.

   * The publication states: “**Randomisation was balanced by centre, with an allocation sequence based on a block size of four, generated with a computer random-number generator by the pharmacist at the coordinating centre**.” This is an adequately random sequence-generation method.
   * It further states: “**Allocation was concealed by use of sequentially numbered opaque envelopes**.” Allocation was managed by the coordinating-centre pharmacist, rather than by recruiting clinicians, providing additional assurance against foreknowledge of assignments.
   * The groups were equal in size (59 randomized to each group). Some baseline differences existed—for example, mean age was 66.4 versus 62.9 years, prior stroke was reported in 17% versus 7%, and baseline FMMS was 17.1 versus 13.4 in the fluoxetine and placebo groups, respectively. However, in this modest-sized trial, these differences are not sufficiently extreme, numerous, or implausible to indicate subversion or a problem with randomization. The article describes the groups as “well balanced” and adjusted the primary analysis for centre, age, previous stroke, and baseline FMMS.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants, carers, site staff, and investigators were masked; adherence was similar between groups; and the primary analysis retained participants in their randomized groups except for those with unavailable outcome data, which is addressed under missing outcome data.

   * The trial was double blind and placebo controlled. The report states: “**Patients, carers, all site study staff, and investigators were masked to treatment assignment**.” Fluoxetine and placebo “**were identical to the active drug in appearance and packaging**.”
   * Both groups received physiotherapy and organized inpatient stroke care. Although rehabilitation programmes were not standardized between centres, this was part of the intended pragmatic care context and not evidence of a differential, trial-context-induced deviation. The analysis adjusted for centre.
   * Adherence was high and balanced: mean relative dose intensity was **94.8%** in the fluoxetine group and **94.0%** in the placebo group. Thus, there is no indication of important differential non-adherence.
   * The protocol allowed management of depression while preserving masking: if depression developed, clinicians could give open-label fluoxetine while continuing trial treatment. This was a protocol-specified procedure, rather than an unplanned deviation from intended intervention.
   * The article describes the primary analysis as the “**full-analysis set**,” including 57/59 randomized fluoxetine participants and 56/59 placebo participants. The five exclusions were due to death or withdrawal before day 90, not switching treatment groups or post-randomization exclusion of outcome-observed participants. For the assignment effect, this modified intention-to-treat/available-case approach is appropriate in this domain; the implications of missing outcome data are considered separately.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** FMMS data at day 90 were available for 113 of 118 randomized participants (95.8%), with similar and very small amounts of missing data in the two groups.

   * The flow diagram shows that 57/59 fluoxetine participants and 56/59 placebo participants contributed to the primary outcome analysis.
   * Only five participants did not provide the day-90 FMMS outcome: two deaths (one per group) and three withdrawals (one fluoxetine participant and two placebo participants). Therefore, missing outcome data were balanced: 2/59 (3.4%) in the fluoxetine group and 3/59 (5.1%) in the placebo group.
   * For this continuous outcome, outcome data from approximately 96% of randomized participants are sufficiently complete that the missing outcomes are unlikely to have made an important difference to the estimated treatment effect.
   * Some reasons for withdrawal—severe hypoxia, kidney tumour, and pulmonary embolism—could plausibly relate to health status. Nevertheless, the number of missing outcomes was very small and not meaningfully differential between groups. There is no indication that missingness was sufficiently extensive or imbalanced to materially bias the FMMS comparison.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** FMMS is an appropriate and established measure of post-stroke motor recovery; it was assessed similarly in both arms; and outcome assessors were masked.

   * The primary outcome was clearly defined as “**the mean change in FMMS score between inclusion (day 0) and day 90**.”
   * The report describes FMMS as “**widely used for assessment of motor recovery after stroke and has excellent intra-rater and inter-rater reliability and validity**.” Thus, the measurement method was appropriate for motor recovery.
   * Outcome measurements were scheduled identically in both groups, at baseline, day 30, and day 90: “**All motor assessments were made by a physiotherapist at day 0 (baseline) and then 30 days and 90 days after enrolment**.”
   * The report states that “**physicians assessing the outcome were masked to group assignment**,” and also that all site study staff and investigators were masked. Although the article refers to physiotherapists making the motor assessments, the reported broad masking of site staff/investigators provides adequate assurance that the assessors were masked.
   * FMMS scoring involves clinical judgement, so assessor knowledge could theoretically influence scores. However, masking, identical placebo capsules, and comparable assessment timing make differential outcome measurement unlikely.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The registered primary outcome and time point are consistent with the reported primary FMMS result, but there is no accessible, sufficiently detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. Consequently, selection among eligible analyses cannot be excluded.

   * The trial registration identifies “**Progression in the Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale**” at “**M3 (3 months)**” as the primary outcome. This matches the publication’s primary endpoint: change in FMMS from baseline to day 90.
   * The publication similarly states: “**The primary outcome was the mean change in FMMS score between inclusion (day 0) and day 90**.” Therefore, there is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected from a different outcome domain or from an alternative time point.
   * However, the registry entry was first posted in April 2008, whereas recruitment began in March 2005. The supplied registration does not provide a detailed, date-stamped statistical analysis plan that confirms the precise planned analysis—such as the choice of adjusted versus unadjusted analysis, exact covariates, and handling of missing FMMS data—was finalized before unblinded data were available.
   * The publication reports several analyses, including adjusted regression analyses controlling for centre and baseline factors that showed imbalance, as well as sensitivity analyses. The final reported primary estimate is adjusted for centre, age, previous stroke, and baseline FMMS. These analyses may be reasonable, but the absence of a prospectively finalized detailed analysis plan means that selection from multiple eligible analytical approaches cannot be ruled out.
   * There is no clear evidence of selective reporting, so a high-risk judgement is not warranted. However, under RoB 2, the lack of a verifiable prespecified analysis plan leads to **some concerns**.
