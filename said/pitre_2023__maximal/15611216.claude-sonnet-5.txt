# RoB 2.0 Assessment: BACE Intervention for Agitation in Dementia (Outcome: Agitation Score, Table 4)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect), as specified.

---

## 1. Bias due to Randomization: **Some Concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise from (i) lack of information about allocation concealment and (ii) a statistically significant and mechanistically important baseline imbalance in the key prognostic variable underlying the intervention (arousal imbalance).

**b. Rationale:**
- *Sequence generation:* The article states participants were "randomly assigned... to groups in time blocks of 1 week" and that "time blocks" were also randomized as treatment or control weeks. This confirms a random component (Signalling Q1.1 = Yes).
- *Allocation concealment:* No information is provided about how the randomization sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants. Because randomization occurred in **weekly blocks** (rather than individual-level concealed allocation), there is a plausible risk that staff/recruiters could anticipate whether a given week was a "treatment" or "control" week, potentially influencing recruitment or engagement during that week. This response is judged "No information" (Signalling Q1.2 = NI).
- *Baseline imbalance:* Table 1 shows a statistically significant difference between groups in "Arousal imbalance, min/12 hr" at pretest (Experimental M=107.4 vs Control M=84.6; t=-2.584, p=.012). This is not merely one of many nonsignificant comparisons — it is **the central prognostic variable** that determines eligibility for the active intervention (Phase 2/3) and is causally linked to the outcome (agitation). The authors themselves acknowledge: "Because random assignment did not equalize the groups on arousal imbalance, we controlled for this variable in the analysis." This meets the criterion for baseline imbalance suggestive of a randomization problem (Signalling Q1.3 = Yes), though the analysis did statistically adjust for it.
- Per the algorithm: NI on concealment + Yes on imbalance → **Some concerns** (not automatically "High," since sequence generation was adequately random and adjustment was performed, but the imbalance combined with unclear concealment prevents a "Low" judgement).

---

## 2. Deviations from Intended Intervention (Effect of Assignment to Intervention): **Some Concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Participants were blinded, but personnel delivering the intervention (nursing staff/GNP) could not be blinded given the nature of the intervention (altering the activity schedule). Additionally, the analysis excluded 10 participants who were not given the intervention, which departs from strict ITT principles.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Participant blinding:* "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions" (Signalling Q2.1 = No/blinded, though blinding of severely demented participants has limited practical relevance).
- *Personnel blinding:* Nursing staff and the GNP who implemented the new activity schedule were necessarily aware of group assignment — blinding personnel delivering a schedule-modification intervention is not feasible (Signalling Q2.2 = Yes).
- *Deviations arising from trial context:* There is no clear evidence that unblinded staff introduced non-protocol co-interventions that differentially affected the outcome. The authors controlled for the number of therapeutic activity sessions as a covariate (finding it non-significant), suggesting no major imbalance in co-intervention exposure was detected (Signalling Q2.3 ≈ No).
- *Analysis approach:* Critically, "10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention" and were excluded from the final analytic sample of 78. This is a departure from strict intention-to-treat analysis, since eligible randomized participants were excluded based on failure to receive intervention (a post-randomization event related to intervention delivery, e.g., staff being "short-staffed" or "busy"). This raises risk that the effect of assignment (rather than the intended per-protocol effect) is not perfectly estimated (Signalling Q2.6 ≈ No). However, the numbers involved (10/102 ≈ 10%) and stated reasons (staffing logistics, verified by independent coders) appear largely unrelated to the outcome trajectory, limiting the potential for substantial impact (Signalling Q2.7 ≈ Probably No).
- Combined, this yields a domain-level judgement of **Some concerns** rather than High risk, because unblinding of personnel was unavoidable, no strong evidence of harmful trial-context deviations was found, and the analytic exclusion, while a deviation from strict ITT, is unlikely by itself to have substantially biased the result.

## 3. Missing Outcome Data: **High Risk of Bias**

**a. Judgement:** A substantial proportion of randomized/enrolled participants were excluded from the final analysis for reasons that could plausibly relate to the outcome, and no sensitivity analyses were performed to assess robustness to these exclusions.

**b. Rationale:**
- Of 102 participants who began the study, the final analytic sample was 78 — a loss of **24/102 (~23.5%)** of enrolled participants: "Twelve died, 10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention, and 2 were excluded for erroneous or missing data" (Signalling Q3.1 = No; outcome data not available for all/nearly all).
- No sensitivity analyses were reported to demonstrate the result is robust to these missing data (Signalling Q3.2 = No).
- Missingness could plausibly depend on the true value of the outcome: participants who died (n=12) were likely more medically unstable, potentially with different (or unmeasurable) agitation trajectories; this is a substantively meaningful loss for a frail, severely demented long-term-care population (Signalling Q3.3 = Yes).
- Reasons for missingness differed in nature and are not clearly balanced between arms — the report does not provide a breakdown by group of the 12 deaths, 10 intervention-non-delivery dropouts, and 2 data-error exclusions. Death and failure-to-receive-intervention are recognized high-risk reasons for informative missingness in frail dementia populations (Signalling Q3.4 = Yes, likely depends on true value, given the vulnerability of this population and lack of group-stratified reporting).
- This pattern — sizeable proportion missing, plausible dependence on true outcome value, and no correction — supports a judgement of **High risk of bias** for this domain.

## 4. Measurement of the Outcome: **Low Risk of Bias**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome was measured using a validated, appropriate method, applied consistently across groups, by assessors blinded to intervention status.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Appropriateness of method:* Agitation was measured via direct observation using a visual analog scale operationalized with the Wisconsin Agitation Intensity Parameters (Table 3), grounded in the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory items, with a clear, reproducible scoring algorithm (Signalling Q4.1 = No, not inappropriate).
- *Consistency across arms:* The same observational protocol (3-min observations every 15 minutes, 8 a.m.–8 p.m.) was applied identically to both treatment and control participants (Signalling Q4.2 = No, method did not differ between arms).
- *Blinding of assessors:* "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions" (Signalling Q4.3 = No, assessors unaware).
- *Reliability:* Interrater reliability for agitation ranged from .95 to .984, following rigorous training with retraining triggered if reliability fell below .80, supporting the validity/consistency of measurement.
- Because outcome assessors were blinded, subsequent questions about influence of knowledge of intervention (Q4.4/4.5) are not applicable, and the domain is judged **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Selective Reporting: **Some Concerns**

**a. Judgement:** There is no explicit statement or accessible evidence that the agitation outcome and its associated ANCOVA analysis were specified in a pre-registered/pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available; however, there is no clear evidence of selective reporting from multiple candidate outcome measures or analyses for this specific result.

**b. Rationale:**
- The article does not reference a published trial protocol, registration number, or statistical analysis plan with a date-stamp confirming finalization prior to unblinded data availability (Signalling Q5.1 = No information).
- The primary agitation outcome and analytic approach (repeated-measures ANCOVA adjusting for pretest arousal imbalance and MMSE) appear to align with the paper's stated hypothesis and prior pilot work (Kovach & Schlidt, 2001; Kovach & Wells, 2002), suggesting the outcome and general analytic strategy were likely planned in advance rather than *post hoc* selected (Signalling Q5.2/5.3 ≈ Probably No for selection based on results).
- However, the paper reports **multiple secondary/exploratory analyses** (e.g., three additional 2×2 ANCOVA models with different effect modifiers; dose-response regression; stratified paired t-tests by arousal state) in addition to the primary result. While these do not appear to replace or substitute for the primary Table 4 result, the absence of a publicly available, date-stamped protocol/SAP makes it impossible to fully rule out selective emphasis or reporting choices (e.g., choice of covariates entered into the ANCOVA, such as MMSE and arousal imbalance, without a pre-specified covariate list).
- Given the lack of independently verifiable pre-specification (no protocol/registry information was available to confirm this), but no overt evidence of results-dependent selection for this specific outcome, the judgement is **Some concerns** rather than High risk or Low risk.
