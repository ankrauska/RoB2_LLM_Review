1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence appears genuinely random: “*one of our study investigators, who had no knowledge of the participants, randomly assigned them by using a computer-generated randomized list*.” Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable between daily and discretionary sunscreen groups, including sex, age, skin phenotype, previous sun exposure, sunburn history, smoking, prior sunscreen use, and clinical photoaging of the neck. The article also states that there were “*no differences in phenotype, sun exposure, or pretrial sunscreen use between the intervention and control groups at baseline*.”

   However, allocation concealment is not described. The report does not state that allocation was administered centrally, by an independent service/pharmacy, or through sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. The investigator generating/implementing allocation reportedly had “no knowledge of the participants,” which offers some reassurance, but it does not establish that recruiters could not foresee assignments before enrolment. Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns** when baseline imbalances do not themselves suggest a problem.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew whether they had been asked to apply sunscreen daily or use it at their discretion, because “*placebo sunscreen was considered unethical*.” Thus, participants were not blinded, and those delivering the intervention were probably also aware of allocation.

   Nevertheless, for the assignment-effect estimand, unblinding alone is not sufficient for bias. There is no evidence that deviations from the protocol arose because of the trial context, affected photoaging, and were unbalanced between groups. The study reports comparable sun exposure and other sun-protective behaviours during follow-up: “*Reported sun exposure was similar between the daily and discretionary sunscreen groups during the trial*”; similarly, use of shade and hats was comparable between groups.

   Adherence differed as expected between allocated groups—77% of daily-sunscreen participants reported use at least 3–4 days/week versus 33% of discretionary users—but this represents the effect of assignment to the daily-use strategy rather than a bias in estimating that effect. Analyses were conducted “*according to treatment as randomly allocated*,” using GEE models that included all available repeated measurements. This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment, subject to the separate issue of missing data.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not available at both time points for all, or nearly all, randomized eligible participants. Of the 886 eligible participants with at least one usable skin replica, only 604 contributed both a baseline and follow-up replica; 213 contributed a baseline replica only and 69 a 1996 replica only. Thus, a substantial proportion had a missing photoaging measurement at one of the two relevant time points.

   The authors provide useful reassurance. They report that “*having a missing microtopography grade in 1996 was not associated with baseline microtopography grade*,” and that treatment allocation was not associated with missing grades. They also performed complete-case and multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses, reporting that both “*replicated the results presented in Table 4*.”

   However, missingness could still plausibly depend on the true outcome, particularly because participants with only one assessment were more likely to have severe clinical photoaging of the neck and to report 2–5 sunburns. Although these factors were adjusted for and the sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary analysis, the reporting does not establish that missingness was unrelated to unobserved follow-up photoaging, nor does it provide a sensitivity analysis explicitly testing plausible missing-not-at-random scenarios. Therefore, the evidence reduces but does not eliminate concern.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Photoaging was measured using silicone skin-surface replicas from a standardized site—the back of the left hand—and assessed using the established Beagley and Gibson microtopography scale. The authors report that this measure had previously been validated as a measure of skin photoaging and that intra- and inter-grader repeatability was high: weighted kappa values were 0.81 and 0.86, respectively.

   The same measurement procedure was used for both sunscreen groups. Importantly, “*experienced assessors who were unaware of treatment allocation graded replicas*.” Therefore, outcome assessors were blinded, and differential assessment due to knowledge of assigned sunscreen strategy is unlikely. The report also notes that measurement error in microtopography grading was likely nondifferential because assessors were blinded.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The published report clearly identifies photoaging as the outcome of interest: “*The primary outcome was change in photoaging from 1992 to 1996 in those in the intervention groups compared with their respective controls.*” It also reports the principal sunscreen comparison and describes the analysis method, including an ordinal GEE model estimating relative change in microtopography grade over time. The reported overall sunscreen result is consistent across the main adjusted and unadjusted models and in complete-case and multiple-imputation analyses.

   However, there is no evidence of a detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. The cited trial registration, ACTRN12610000086066, was registered retrospectively, long after recruitment (1992), follow-up completion (1996), and therefore after outcome data would have been available. Retrospective registration cannot demonstrate that the reported photoaging measurement, time point, covariate adjustment, or analytic approach were prespecified before data analysis.

   The report does not provide clear evidence that the result was selected from multiple outcome measurements or multiple competing eligible analyses on the basis of its statistical significance or direction. Nonetheless, because a prospectively finalized analysis plan is unavailable—and multiple analytic choices were possible for an ordinal repeated-measures outcome—the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.
