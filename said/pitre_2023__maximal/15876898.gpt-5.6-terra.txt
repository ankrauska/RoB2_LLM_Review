**Outcome assessed:** body weight at 10 weeks, comparing adjunctive topiramate with placebo among women receiving olanzapine.  
**Effect of interest:** effect of assignment to topiramate versus placebo at baseline.

1. **Bias due to randomization: Some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The report describes the trial as “random, double-blind, placebo-controlled,” and states that “randomization was carried out confidentially by the clinic administration.” However, it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, random blocks) and therefore does not establish that the sequence contained a random component.

   Allocation concealment before enrolment is also insufficiently described. The statement that randomization was conducted “confidentially” by clinic administration suggests an attempt to protect allocation, but does not clarify whether recruiters could foresee assignments, whether allocation was centrally controlled, or whether sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes or another secure mechanism was used. The later statement that tablets were supplied in “numbered boxes” concerns blinding of treatment administration and does not, by itself, demonstrate concealment of the allocation sequence at enrolment.

   Baseline characteristics in Table 1 appear reasonably similar among the 43 analysed participants: mean initial weight was 86.6 kg in the topiramate group and 85.1 kg in the placebo group; age, height, olanzapine dose, prior weight gain, and treatment duration were also broadly comparable. However, these baseline data are reported after six placebo-group participants had dropped out and were excluded from analysis. Thus, they do not fully establish balance among all randomized participants.

   Overall, there is no clear evidence that randomization failed, but inadequate reporting of sequence generation and allocation concealment raises concerns.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: High risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   This was described as a double-blind placebo-controlled study: “Both subjects and clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment.” The report also states that olanzapine doses and concomitant medications “remained constant.” These features reduce concern that knowledge of assignment led to differential co-interventions or other trial-context deviations.

   However, the analysis did not estimate the effect of assignment to intervention in all randomized participants. The authors state:

   > “Six subjects who failed to appear for more than one evaluation (all from the placebo group) dropped out of the study, and their data were not further analyzed.”

   Thus, the reported weight result was based on 25 topiramate participants and 18 placebo participants, despite the intended allocation of 25 and 24, respectively. Excluding six randomized participants—all from the placebo group—is not an intention-to-treat or modified intention-to-treat analysis. It is a post-randomization complete-case analysis, and the imbalance in exclusions is substantial: 6/24 (25%) of the placebo group versus 0/25 of the topiramate group.

   Because all excluded participants were in the placebo group, and their weight outcomes may plausibly have differed from those retained, failure to analyse participants according to randomized assignment could have had a substantial impact on the estimated between-group difference. Under RoB 2 for the effect of assignment, this constitutes high risk of bias.

3. **Missing outcome data: High risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Weight data were not available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants. Of 49 planned/randomized participants (25 topiramate and 24 placebo), only 43 were analysed. The six missing participants represent approximately 12% of all randomized participants and 25% of the placebo group.

   The report identifies the immediate reason for missing data as failure to attend evaluations:

   > “Six subjects who failed to appear for more than one evaluation (all from the placebo group) dropped out of the study.”

   No weight outcomes for these participants were reported, no reasons for non-attendance beyond missed evaluations were provided, and no sensitivity analyses or imputation approaches were undertaken. Importantly, missingness occurred exclusively in the placebo group. This strong differential pattern makes it plausible that missingness was related to participants’ true weight trajectory, treatment response, dissatisfaction, health status, or other outcome-related factors.

   The statement that “their data were not further analyzed” confirms that the missing data were simply excluded. Since no evidence shows that the result was robust to plausible values for these missing outcomes, and missingness was both appreciable and highly unbalanced, the risk of bias due to missing weight data is high.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: Low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Body weight is an objective outcome, and repeated weight measurement is an appropriate method for assessing the outcome of interest. Participants were “seen, weighed” every two weeks during the 10-week trial. There is no indication that different scales, thresholds, schedules, or measurement procedures were used in the two groups.

   Participants and clinicians were reported to be blinded to treatment assignment:

   > “Both subjects and clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment.”

   Although the report does not explicitly identify the person who measured weight or state that the assessor was blinded, body weight is a relatively objective observer-recorded measure with little scope for assessor judgement. Knowledge of intervention assignment would therefore be unlikely to materially influence the recorded weight, particularly in a double-blind placebo-controlled setting.

   Accordingly, the method was appropriate, apparently comparable between groups, and unlikely to have been materially influenced by knowledge of allocation.

5. **Selective reporting: Some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The publication identifies weight checks as a primary outcome:

   > “Primary outcome measures were weight checks and self-reported changes on the scales of the SF-36 Health Survey, Bf-S Scale of Well-Being, and the Adjective Checklist EWL-60-S.”

   The paper reports a 10-week weight result and shows weight measurements over the study period. However, no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the material provided. In particular, the report does not specify in advance the precise primary weight-analysis approach—for example, whether the planned primary analysis was final weight, change from baseline, repeated-measures analysis, or another method; nor does it detail how missing data would be handled.

   Multiple measurements were collected every two weeks, creating potential opportunities to select a time point or analysis. The report presents the final 10-week comparison and a graphical trajectory, but does not provide sufficient prespecified analysis detail to rule out selective choice among possible analyses or time points. There is also no indication that analyses were prospectively specified before unblinded outcome data became available.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported 10-week weight result was selected because it was statistically favourable. Therefore, high risk is not justified; nevertheless, absence of a sufficiently detailed prespecified analysis plan leads to some concerns.
