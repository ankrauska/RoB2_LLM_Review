**Outcome assessed:** severe peer aggression at 12 months, comparing the therapist-assisted computer brief intervention (TBI) with the brochure control condition. The reported effect was a group-by-time odds ratio of **0.40 (95% CI 0.23–0.69; P < .01)**.

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns because the study was described as randomized and stratified by age and gender, and the available baseline data do not show a clear randomization failure. However, neither the method used to generate the allocation sequence nor allocation concealment was reported sufficiently to support a low-risk judgement.

**b. Rationale:**
- The report states that participants “were **randomly selected (stratified by gender and age: 14–15, 16–18 years), and assigned to 1 of the 3 study conditions**.”
- Stratification by age and gender is compatible with an appropriate randomization process, but the report does not state whether a computer-generated random sequence, random-number table, or another random mechanism was used.
- No information is provided about concealment of allocation before enrolment/assignment (e.g., central randomization, independently administered sequence, or sequential opaque sealed envelopes).
- The authors reported that there were “**no significant differences between conditions on age, race, gender**.” Baseline prevalence of severe peer aggression was also broadly similar between the TBI and control groups (82.7% versus 77.9%). These differences do not clearly suggest a failure of randomization.
- Thus, there is no evidence of an actual randomization problem, but insufficient detail on sequence generation and, especially, allocation concealment leads to **some concerns** rather than low risk.

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk for the effect of assignment to intervention. Participants and intervention personnel were necessarily aware of assignment, but there is no evidence that trial-context deviations from the intended interventions occurred differentially between groups or affected peer aggression. The reported analysis followed an intention-to-treat approach.

**b. Rationale:**
- Blinding of participants and intervention providers was not feasible: participants received either a therapist-assisted intervention, a computer intervention, or a brochure. Therefore, participants and those delivering the intervention were aware of allocated condition.
- However, for the assignment effect, awareness alone does not establish bias. RoB 2 requires evidence that deviations inconsistent with the protocol arose *because of the trial context*, affected the outcome, and were unbalanced between groups.
- The report does not identify important trial-context deviations, differential cointerventions, or protocol failures that would plausibly affect peer aggression.
- Intervention receipt was very high: “**.95% received their assigned intervention during the ED visit**.”
- The authors state: “**An intent-to-treat approach was used. All randomly assigned participants were included whether the intervention was received or not**.”
- The generalized estimating equation analysis was conducted according to randomized group. Although participants without observed follow-up outcome data could not contribute observed outcome data, that issue is addressed under missing outcome data rather than this domain.
- Therefore, despite lack of blinding, there is no indication of bias-producing deviations from intended intervention for the effect of assignment.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **high risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias because outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants, follow-up was less complete in the TBI group than in the control group, no adequate analysis or sensitivity analysis demonstrates robustness to missing data, and missingness in a self-reported behavioral outcome could plausibly depend on true peer aggression.

**b. Rationale:**
- Of 726 randomized adolescents, 607 completed 12-month follow-up: “**the 12-month follow-up rate was 83.6% (n = 607/726)**.” Thus, approximately 16% did not complete follow-up.
- For the peer-aggression outcome, the numbers in Table 1 indicate data for approximately:
  - TBI: 201/254 randomized participants (79.1%);
  - computer BI: 199/237 (84.0%);
  - control: 200/235 (85.1%).
- Therefore, missing peer-aggression data were more frequent in the TBI group (about 21%) than in the control group (about 15%). This differential loss is relevant because the principal effect estimate compares TBI with control.
- The article reports GEE analyses using “all data available,” but does not describe an approach that would establish that missing-data bias was adequately addressed, such as a justified imputation model, inverse-probability weighting, or sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-not-at-random assumptions.
- Reasons for loss to follow-up are not reported by randomized group. For a self-reported outcome such as severe peer aggression, continued aggression, disengagement, instability, or related risk behaviors could plausibly influence follow-up participation.
- The authors acknowledge this limitation: “**although attrition was low (14%) and an intent to treat analysis was conducted, it is always possible that those lost to follow-up may have biased results.**”
- Given the non-trivial and differential missingness, absence of evidence that results are robust to missing data, and plausible association between missingness and true aggression, this is judged **high risk of bias**.

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **high risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias because peer aggression was a self-reported, subjective behavioral outcome, and participants necessarily knew whether they received a therapist-delivered intervention, a computer intervention, or only a brochure. Knowledge of assignment was likely to influence reporting.

**b. Rationale:**
- Severe peer aggression was measured using self-report items from the conflict tactics scale: “**Items from the conflict tactic scale assessed past-year severe aggression toward peers**.”
- The outcome was converted to a binary self-reported measure: “**Severe past-year peer aggression (4 items) was computed as a binary variable (no/yes).**”
- The 12-month outcome was obtained through a “**self-administered computer survey**.” For a participant-reported outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor for RoB 2 purposes.
- Participants could not realistically be blinded to intervention allocation: the TBI involved a therapist-assisted motivational intervention, whereas controls received a trifold community-resource brochure.
- The trial registry records single masking of an “outcomes assessor,” but this does not overcome the concern for self-reported peer aggression, because the participant—not a research staff member—is the assessor of their own aggressive behavior.
- Reporting of aggression is susceptible to social desirability, perceived expectations, and demand characteristics. These influences are particularly plausible after a motivational intervention specifically designed to reduce violence and improve conflict-management behavior.
- Although computerized self-administration and confidentiality may improve the validity of sensitive self-reporting, they do not blind participants to the intervention they received. The authors themselves note that self-report validity is improved when “**staff are blind to condition assignment**,” but staff blinding does not remove participants’ awareness of assignment.
- Consequently, assessment of peer aggression was likely influenced by knowledge of intervention received, favouring the intervention, and the outcome-measurement domain is at **high risk of bias**.

### 5. Selective reporting: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns because the registry prespecified violence-related outcomes at 12 months, but did not provide a sufficiently detailed, date-stamped statistical analysis plan specifying the peer-aggression measurement, binary definition, analytical model, covariate strategy, or handling of missing data. There is no direct evidence that the reported peer-aggression result was selected based on its statistical significance.

**b. Rationale:**
- The registry identified alcohol misuse and violent behaviors as primary outcomes at 12 months: “**Primary outcomes will be evaluated at 3, 6 and 12-months and include alcohol use/misuse, violent behaviors** …”
- However, the registry does not specify the particular peer-aggression instrument, the “severe” peer-aggression binary definition, whether final values or changes over time would be analyzed, or the exact GEE model and covariate adjustment.
- The publication states that the analyses focused on dichotomous outcomes because effects at earlier follow-up were observed for dichotomized variables: “**At 3 and 6 months, the effects were noted in dichotomous treatment of the variables and not the continuous variables. The analyses focused on evaluating if effects noted at 6 months were sustained at 12 months; therefore, only dichotomous outcomes were examined.**”
- This statement indicates that alternative outcome parameterizations may have been available. It does not, however, establish that the reported binary peer-aggression analysis was selected because it was statistically significant.
- No full, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan was available to verify that the reported 12-month TBI-versus-control peer-aggression analysis was prespecified in its reported form.
- The study reports results for the major registered violence and alcohol outcome domains, so there is no clear direct evidence of selective reporting of the peer-aggression result. Nevertheless, insufficiently detailed prespecification warrants **some concerns**.
