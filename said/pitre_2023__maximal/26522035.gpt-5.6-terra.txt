**Outcome assessed:** patient acceptance of the appliance, comparing mini-implant anchored Forsus Fatigue Resistant Device (FMI) with conventional Forsus Fatigue Resistant Device (FFRD).  
**Effect of interest:** effect of assignment to intervention at baseline (intention-to-treat effect).

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
The allocation sequence was explicitly random, allocation was adequately concealed, and the reported baseline data do not suggest a failure of randomization.

* The authors reported computer-generated randomization: “**Random sequence generation was done with a computer-generated list of random numbers** obtained from an Excel spreadsheet,” using “**random permuted blocks with randomized order of block sizes**” of 6 and 4.
* Allocation concealment was appropriate: “**Allocation concealment was achieved with sequentially numbered and sealed opaque envelopes**,” which were “**concealed from the researchers and the assessors**.”
* Randomization was undertaken by “**a person who was not involved in the trial**.”
* Baseline characteristics—including age, skeletal measures, overjet, and mandibular crowding—were reasonably similar between the 16 participants in each group. Although a few numerical differences existed (e.g., MP/SN and MMP), there is no clear imbalance in a key prognostic factor that is implausible due to chance in this small trial.

Accordingly, the RoB 2 signalling questions would be answered: sequence random—**Yes**; allocation concealed—**Yes**; baseline imbalances suggesting a problem—**No**.

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

**b. Rationale:**  
Participants and clinicians necessarily knew whether mini-implants had been placed, so blinding during treatment was not feasible. However, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding alone does not establish bias. There is no reported evidence of deviations from the intended interventions that arose because of the trial context, were unbalanced between groups, and were likely to affect patient acceptance.

* The article states: “**Blinding of the clinicians and patients to the interventions in each group was impossible**.”
* Both groups received the FFRD as intended; the FMI group additionally received mandibular mini-implants. Follow-up and appliance activation procedures appear to have been applied similarly: “**Follow-up visits were every 3 to 4 weeks**” for assessment of appliance and, where relevant, mini-implant stability.
* The study reports no treatment discontinuations: “**No patients dropped out during the trial**.”
* The CONSORT diagram indicates that no participants were “lost to follow-up or discontinued intervention” and that all 16 participants per group were analysed.
* The planned analysis was described as intention-to-treat: “**The primary analysis was planned to be an intention-to-treat analysis to involve all patients who were to be randomly assigned; however, since there were no dropouts in the study, all the patients were included in the analysis.**”

There is no indication that trial personnel delivered non-protocol co-interventions differently because of treatment assignment, that patients obtained non-protocol interventions because of the trial, or that implementation failures created differential deviations likely to affect acceptance. The reported analysis retained all randomized participants in their assigned groups.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Outcome data were available for every randomized participant.

* The trial reports: “**The 32 surveys were retrieved from the patients; there were no dropouts.**”
* It further states: “**All questionnaires were given to and collected from the patients on the day of removal of the FFRD. All questions were answered correctly by the patients and their parents.**”
* The CONSORT flow diagram confirms that all 32 randomized participants—16 in each group—were analysed.
* The outcome tables contain data for 16 participants in each group for the questionnaire outcomes relevant to acceptance.

Thus, missingness could not plausibly have materially biased the result because there were no missing patient-acceptance outcome data.

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Patient acceptance was measured with a patient-completed questionnaire, making the participant the outcome assessor. Participants were necessarily aware of their assigned intervention, and knowledge of whether they had received additional mini-implants was likely to influence subjective ratings of acceptance, discomfort, appearance, satisfaction, and functional interference.

* The outcome was based on “**assessment questionnaires … regarding their experience with the FFRD**,” completed by patients on appliance removal.
* The questionnaire assessed inherently subjective domains, including ease of insertion, noticeability, speech, eating, sleep, external appearance, pain, swelling, oral hygiene, and satisfaction with results. These are participant-reported outcomes susceptible to expectations and preferences.
* The investigators acknowledge that treatment blinding was impossible: “**Blinding of the clinicians and patients to the interventions in each group was impossible.**”
* Although the article states that “**blinding was done for outcomes assessment**,” this cannot fully blind the outcome assessor for a participant-reported outcome: the patients themselves completed the questionnaire and knew whether mini-implants had been inserted. The principal investigator also “**thoroughly explained**” the questions to patients or parents, which could further permit awareness of treatment assignment during reporting.
* The instrument was modified from a previous questionnaire and translated into the participants’ native language. While this does not itself establish that it was invalid, no formal validation of the modified/translated questionnaire is reported.

The method was broadly appropriate for eliciting patient experiences and appears to have been used similarly in both arms. However, because the outcome was subjective, patients were unblinded, and knowledge of the visibly and physically distinct interventions was likely to affect reported acceptance, the measurement domain is at high risk of bias. The probable direction of any bias cannot be reliably predicted.

### 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The study was not registered, no protocol was published before trial commencement, and no pre-specified statistical analysis plan was available. Therefore, it is not possible to confirm that the reported patient-acceptance result and its analysis were selected according to plans finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

* The article explicitly states: “**Registration: This trial was not registered. Protocol: The protocol was not published before trial commencement.**”
* The article also reports that “**there were no outcome changes after commencement of the trial**,” but this retrospective statement cannot substitute for a dated, prospectively available protocol or analysis plan.
* The outcome domain was assessed using numerous questionnaire components (14 questions; several Likert-scale outcomes and yes/no outcomes). This provides opportunities to select among different patient-acceptance measures, subdomains, or analyses.
* The report does present results for the individual questionnaire questions and reports ridit analyses for questions 1–11, which reduces concern that only one favourable questionnaire item was selectively presented.
* Nevertheless, without a prospective protocol, registry entry, or dated statistical analysis plan, it cannot be verified that the choice of the primary patient-acceptance measure, timing, questionnaire scoring, reference group, and ridit-analysis approach were all prespecified before outcome data were examined.

There is no direct evidence that a particular favourable result was selected from multiple analyses or measurements; therefore, high risk of bias is not warranted. However, the absence of prospective registration and a pre-specified publicly available analysis plan leads to some concerns.
