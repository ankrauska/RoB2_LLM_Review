1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The sequence-generation method appears appropriate: “**A computer-based blocking method (four blocks) was used for the randomization and no stratification was made.**” This supports a random allocation sequence.

   However, allocation concealment is inadequately described. The report states that “**Separate physicians were assigned to randomly allocate patients into two groups**,” but does not state whether allocation was centrally controlled, concealed from recruiters before enrolment, or implemented with sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. Blinded medication packages reduce the likelihood of post-randomization unblinding, but do not by themselves demonstrate concealment of the allocation sequence before assignment.

   The reported baseline characteristics do not show important imbalance: age, sex, disease duration, relapse history, prior interferon treatment, EDSS and MRI lesion measures were broadly similar, with all reported baseline-comparison P values >0.05. The randomized group sizes (43 simvastatin and 42 placebo) were also consistent with the intended approximately 1:1 allocation. Nevertheless, baseline data appear to be reported for fewer than all randomized participants (e.g., 38 placebo and 42 simvastatin patients in Table 1), limiting a fully definitive assessment of balance among all randomized participants.

   Thus, random sequence generation was adequate and there is no clear evidence of a baseline imbalance problem, but insufficient information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Participants, treating personnel and outcome assessors were intended to be blinded. The publication states that participants were “**assigned (in random and double-blinded fashion)**,” that “**patients were unaware of their group assignment and all medications were given in similar packages**,” and that neurologists conducting follow-up were blinded to treatment assignment. The trial registry also describes quadruple masking (participant, care provider, investigator and outcome assessor). Therefore, there is no evidence of deviations caused by awareness of treatment allocation or by the trial context.

   However, the analysis used for the reported attack-number result does not appear to include all randomized participants. Although the authors state that analyses were “**performed according to intention-to-treat methods, and patients who withdraw from the study were followed for the whole study period**,” the primary Mann–Whitney analysis of total relapse number included only 40 simvastatin and 36 placebo participants, rather than the 43 and 42 randomized participants. Thus, nine randomized participants were excluded from this analysis.

   The exclusions were consequential and related to outcome/prognosis. The trial’s withdrawal criteria included “**having two relapses in the study period**,” and six patients were withdrawn because they had two relapses (two simvastatin, four placebo); another placebo participant had chronic exacerbation. The authors further state that only participants who had received study medication for at least 6 months were included in the final analysis: “**those of our dropout cases who did use simvastatin or placebo for 6 months (nine patients) were included in the final analysis.**” This indicates that some randomized patients withdrawing earlier were excluded post-randomization.

   Exclusion of eligible randomized participants, particularly when withdrawal may be related to recurrent attacks or worsening disease, is not a full intention-to-treat analysis and could substantially affect the relapse-count result. The imbalance in analysed sample size (40/43 versus 36/42) further reinforces this concern. Therefore, despite successful blinding, the analysis is inappropriate for estimating the effect of assignment and has potential for substantial impact: **high risk of bias**.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Attack-number data were not available for all randomized participants. The primary comparison included 40/43 (93%) simvastatin participants and 36/42 (86%) placebo participants—76/85 (89%) overall. Hence, outcome data were missing for nine randomized participants, including a larger proportion in the placebo group.

   There is no reported sensitivity analysis demonstrating that the result is robust to plausible outcomes among excluded/missing participants. The reported per-protocol and last-observation-carried-forward analyses do not adequately establish that missing attack data could not bias the effect estimate. LOCF is not generally sufficient to demonstrate protection against bias from missing outcome data, particularly for recurrent clinical attacks over a 1-year follow-up.

   Importantly, missingness plausibly depended on the true outcome value. Withdrawals included participants who experienced “**two relapses**,” a participant with “**chronic exacerbation**,” and participants who stopped participation for unspecified or personal reasons. The protocol itself listed “**>2 attacks in study**” as an exclusion criterion. Since the outcome is attack number, withdrawal following multiple attacks is directly related to the true value of the outcome. Furthermore, withdrawals/exclusions were more frequent in the placebo arm: the flow diagram reports premature withdrawal due to exacerbation in 6 placebo participants versus 3 simvastatin participants.

   Consequently, outcome data were incomplete, differential between groups, and missingness was likely related to disease activity/attack number. This constitutes **high risk of bias** due to missing outcome data.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The method of relapse ascertainment was clinically appropriate and prespecified. A relapse was defined as “**any new or worsening neurological deficit in the absence of high body temperature that lasted for more than 24 h after 1-month stability**.” Participants were evaluated every 3 months by a neurologist for “**possible complications, relapses, disease activity [and] neurological status**.” This is an appropriate clinical approach to measuring relapse/attack occurrence in relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis.

   Outcome assessment procedures appear comparable between intervention groups: both groups continued interferon beta-1a, were followed on the same 3-month schedule, and had the same relapse definition and management procedures.

   The report also states that the neurologists assessing eligibility and conducting follow-up examinations “**were blinded to the patients’ assignment to treatment group**.” The registry additionally records outcome-assessor masking. Because the assessors were blinded and relapse was assessed using a defined clinical criterion, knowledge of intervention assignment is unlikely to have influenced outcome assessment.

   The outcome measurement domain is therefore judged at **low risk of bias**. This judgement does not remove the separate concerns that relapse data were missing/excluded for some randomized patients.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The registry identifies “**attack number**” at “**1 year**” as the primary outcome, which is consistent with the publication’s statement that “**the main outcome evaluated in this study was the number of the relapses during the 1-year follow-up**.” Thus, the reported outcome domain and broad assessment time frame are concordant with the registration record.

   However, the registry was first submitted and posted in April 2008, whereas recruitment began in April 2005 and primary completion occurred in March 2008. Thus, the available registration was not prospectively posted before trial commencement and does not provide a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan. It does not specify the intended analysis population, statistical method, handling of withdrawals/missing outcome data, whether total attacks or attacks per interval would be the principal metric, or which adjustment strategy would be used.

   The publication reports several analyses for attacks: a stated intention-to-treat analysis, a per-protocol analysis, and an LOCF analysis of attacks per 3-month period. The main reported analysis was a Mann–Whitney U-test of total attacks, but the report provides no prespecified SAP that establishes this as the sole primary analysis or confirms that analysis choices were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   There is no direct evidence that the presented result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable to simvastatin. Nonetheless, the absence of a prospectively finalized detailed analysis plan, together with multiple plausible analysis populations and methods, means selective reporting cannot be confidently excluded. Therefore, **some concerns** are warranted.
