**Outcome assessed:** Agitation Score, Table 4 (pretest–posttest Group × Time comparison of BACE versus control).  
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to BACE versus control at baseline.

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
The report states that there was “random assignment” and that participants were “randomly assigned … in time blocks of 1 week,” with time blocks also randomized as treatment or control weeks. However, it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers) or whether allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants. In particular, there is no indication of central randomization, independently managed allocation, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes.

The weekly-block approach also makes the unit and implementation of randomization somewhat unclear: participants appear to have been allocated according to treatment/control weeks to avoid carryover rather than through clearly described individual allocation.

There was a baseline imbalance in time spent in arousal imbalance: experimental group mean 107.4 minutes/12 hr versus control mean 84.6 minutes/12 hr; *p* = .012. This was relevant to the intervention and potentially prognostic for agitation. The investigators adjusted for this variable, but adjustment does not establish that the randomization process was sound. Conversely, the groups were broadly similar for baseline agitation, age, cognition, functional stage, sex, and length of stay. The single statistically significant baseline difference could plausibly have occurred by chance, but—combined with absent information on sequence generation and allocation concealment—it raises concerns rather than demonstrating high risk.

Relevant reporting includes:

- “A pretest–posttest double-blinded experimental design with random assignment was used.”
- “We randomly assigned participants to study groups in time blocks of 1 week… We also randomized time blocks as treatment or control condition weeks.”
- “The experimental group participants spent significantly more time in arousal imbalance at pretest.”
- Table 1: arousal imbalance, *p* = .012.

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **high risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

**b. Rationale:**  
Although the article describes data collectors and participants as blinded, those responsible for implementing the BACE schedule could not realistically have been blinded, because the intervention required staff to alter participants’ activity schedules. More importantly, the reported analysis did not include all randomized participants in their assigned groups.

Ten participants were explicitly removed because they were not given the intervention:

- “Twelve died, **10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention**, and 2 were excluded for erroneous or missing data.”
- “A participant had to have been given at least one of the prescribed changes in activity schedule … **to be included in the analysis**.”
- The authors state that the intervention was not delivered because staff were “short-staffed, being busy, or having more emergent needs.”

Thus, participants assigned to BACE who received none of the intervention were excluded after randomization, rather than retained in an intention-to-treat analysis. This is an inappropriate analysis for the requested effect of assignment at baseline. The exclusion of 10 of 102 enrolled participants (approximately 10%), specifically because the intervention was not delivered, is sufficiently substantial to affect the Group × Time result. It could preferentially remove participants or circumstances associated with high care burden, staff shortages, or emergent clinical needs, all of which may also relate to agitation.

The reported repeated-measures ANCOVA appears to be based on the final sample of 78 rather than all randomized participants. Therefore, the analysis estimates an effect among a selected subset rather than the effect of assignment to BACE.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **some concerns**

**c. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**d. Rationale:**  
Outcome data were not available for all, or nearly all, participants initially enrolled. Of 102 participants, the final analytic sample was 78. For this domain, the 12 deaths and 2 exclusions for “erroneous or missing data” constitute missing outcome data; the 10 exclusions for receiving no intervention are principally addressed under deviations from intended intervention.

The report does not provide outcome-data completeness separately by randomized group, does not indicate when deaths occurred relative to posttest measurement, and does not report a sensitivity analysis for missing data. It also does not explain the nature of the “erroneous” data or whether missingness differed by group.

Deaths and missing posttest observations among people with moderate or severe dementia could plausibly be related to health status and possibly to agitation or other prognostic factors. However, the report does not provide enough evidence to conclude that missingness was likely dependent on the unobserved agitation score, nor does it establish a clear between-group differential in missingness. Therefore, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk.

Relevant reporting:

- “During the time period allotted for this study, 102 people participated in the study.”
- “Twelve died … and 2 were excluded for erroneous or missing data.”
- “The final sample was 78, with 36 receiving the experimental intervention and 42 in the control group.”

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**  
Agitation was measured by direct, structured observation at the same pretest and posttest time points in both groups. Research assistants observed participants for 3 minutes every 15 minutes from 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. and used predefined Wisconsin Agitation Intensity Parameters to score the visual analogue scale. The method was applied comparably in both trial arms.

Outcome assessors/data collectors were reported to be blinded to treatment allocation:

- “Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions.”

The report also describes extensive observer training and periodic reliability checks:

- “Training at sites continued until interrater reliability estimates were .85 or higher for all observational tools on five sequential measures.”
- “Following training sessions, interrater reliabilities … ranged from .95 to .984 for agitation.”

The investigators changed from the Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument to a visual analogue scale because they could not achieve adequate inter-rater reliability using the former measure. This change means that the final measure had less established validity than a standard instrument, but the article provides a clear operational definition, standardized scoring parameters, direct observation, and strong inter-rater reliability. There is no evidence that the final measurement method was inherently inappropriate or that it differed between groups. Blinded assessors further reduce the likelihood of differential measurement error.

### 5. Selective reporting: **some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**  
There is no accessible prespecified protocol, statistical analysis plan, or usable trial-registration information in the supplied materials that documents the intended agitation measure, time point, covariate set, handling of post-randomization exclusions, or primary analysis before unblinded outcome data became available. Consequently, it cannot be confirmed that the Table 4 ANCOVA result was specified in advance.

There are several potential analytic choices for this outcome. The report presents:

- unadjusted pretest and posttest group means;
- a repeated-measures ANCOVA adjusted for pretest arousal imbalance and MMSE;
- an additional model including number of therapeutic activity sessions;
- within-group paired *t* tests;
- analyses stratified by arousal-balance versus arousal-imbalance periods; and
- further subgroup interaction analyses based on cognition, sleep, and verbal ability.

In addition, the agitation outcome method was changed from the initially planned Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument to a visual analogue scale:

- “Our initial plan called for us to assess agitation by using the Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument… We were, however, unable to achieve sufficiently consistent interrater reliability estimates… We then trained RAs to assess agitation by using a visual analog scale from 0 to 100.”

That measurement change may have been legitimate and motivated by reliability problems, but its timing relative to outcome-data availability is not stated. The report does not provide clear evidence that Table 4 was selected from multiple analyses or outcome measurements because of a favourable result. Thus, high risk cannot be concluded. Nevertheless, absence of a prespecified analysis plan and the availability of multiple plausible measurements and analyses lead to some concerns.
