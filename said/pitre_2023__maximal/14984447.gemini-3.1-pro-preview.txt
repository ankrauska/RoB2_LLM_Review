Based on the RoB 2.0 guidance, here is the risk of bias assessment for the trial regarding the outcomes of SBGM frequency and HbA1c, considering the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline (intention-to-treat effect):

**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
**a. Judgement:** The method of randomization and allocation concealment are not clearly described, although baseline characteristics appear balanced.
**b. Rationale:** The trial report states that patients were "randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion to two groups," but it does not specify the method of random sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated) or whether the allocation sequence was adequately concealed (e.g., central randomization, sealed opaque envelopes) before assignment. However, baseline characteristics (Table 1) were well matched, suggesting no major baseline imbalances that would indicate a problem with the randomization process. Due to the lack of information on sequence generation and allocation concealment, this domain raises some concerns.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** Participants and personnel were aware of the intervention, but deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context are unlikely to have impacted the outcome, and an appropriate ITT analysis was conducted. 
**b. Rationale:** Given the nature of the intervention (providing free test strips vs. having patients purchase them), participants and personnel could not be blinded. The study notes that patients were "blind to the primary objective of the study," but they were aware of their strip provision status. There is no evidence that deviations from the intended interventions occurred due to the trial context. The authors state that "Efficacy data from the intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward, were analysed," which is an appropriate analysis strategy for estimating the effect of assignment to the intervention.

**3. missing outcome data: high risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** There was a large amount of missing outcome data, with a substantial imbalance in dropout rates between the two groups, and missingness is likely related to the true value of the outcome.
**b. Rationale:** The dropout rate was significantly unbalanced between the groups: "there were 25 patients remaining in the NC group and 16 patients in the C group, i.e. a dropout rate of 19% in the NC group and 48% in the C group." While the authors used a Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) approach, this does not adequately correct for bias when data are missing not at random. The authors themselves suggest that cost and inconvenience drove dropouts in the control group, stating that "patients in the C group who found the cost of the reagent strips to be problematic may well have dropped out of the study." Because those with poorer control or less means were more likely to drop out, missingness likely depended on the true value of the outcomes, leading to a high risk of bias.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
**a. Judgement:** The outcomes (HbA1c and glucometer downloads) are objective measures that are unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.
**b. Rationale:** The methods of measuring the outcomes (HbA1c via laboratory analysis and SBGM frequency via downloading the glucometer memory using computer software) were appropriate and identical across both groups. Although patients and personnel were unblinded, these are objective, observer-independent outcomes (automated test/device memory) where the assessment of the outcome is highly unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of the intervention received. 

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
**a. Judgement:** There is insufficient information to confirm that the trial was analyzed according to a pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available.
**b. Rationale:** The article does not reference a published protocol or a date-stamped statistical analysis plan that was finalized prior to the unblinding of outcome data. While the reported outcomes (HbA1c and SBGM frequency) are standard and expected for this type of intervention, the absence of a verified pre-specified analysis plan means we cannot completely rule out the selection of reported results from multiple eligible analyses (e.g., handling of the large amount of missing data). Therefore, this domain is judged to have some concerns.
