# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Attack Number (Total Relapses)
## Effect of Interest: Effect of Assignment to Intervention

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation:** The trial used "a computer-based blocking method (four blocks)...for the randomization." This is a random component (blocked randomization), so the answer to "was the allocation sequence random?" is **Yes**.
- **Allocation concealment:** The article states "Separate physicians were assigned to randomly allocate patients into two groups" and that patients "were unaware of their group assignment and all medications were given in similar packages." However, no details are given about whether the sequence was concealed from the physicians performing allocation, whether sequentially numbered/sealed containers were used, or whether the small block size (blocks of four) with unmasked allocators could allow prediction of the final assignments within blocks. There is **no information** provided on concealment mechanisms.
- **Baseline imbalance:** Table 1 shows no significant differences between groups on measured baseline characteristics (age, sex, disease duration, relapses since diagnosis, IFNb1a duration, baseline EDSS, baseline Gd+ lesions; all p>0.13). This is consistent with successful randomization and does not raise concern.

Given: sequence was random (Yes), no information on allocation concealment, and no baseline imbalance concerns → per the RoB2 algorithm (Y/PY sequence, NI concealment, N/PN imbalance) → **Some concerns**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Blinding of participants/personnel:** The protocol registration indicates "QUADRUPLE" masking including participants, care providers, investigators, and outcomes assessors. The article states "patients were unaware of their group assignment and all medications were given in similar packages, every 3 months," suggesting blinding of participants and personnel was intended and implemented (**No** to awareness).
- However, simvastatin has recognizable effects (e.g., cholesterol lowering could be tested; distinct side effect profile such as myalgia, elevated CPK/LFTs) that could potentially unblind clinicians or patients if lab values were disclosed. The article does not explicitly discuss whether lab results were withheld from clinicians/patients during the trial to preserve blinding, introducing some uncertainty about whether blinding was fully maintained throughout (**No information** on whether deviations arose from unblinding).
- No explicit evidence of non-protocol co-interventions or trial-context-driven deviations affecting the relapse outcome is reported.
- **Analysis:** The paper states "analyses were performed according to intention-to-treat methods, and patients who withdrew from the study were followed for the whole study period," consistent with an appropriate ITT analysis for the primary attack number outcome. This supports "Yes" to appropriate analysis (Part 2 = Low risk).

Because of the possible (but unconfirmed) risk that blinding could have been imperfectly maintained (e.g., via lab-based clues), and no explicit statement addressing this, the domain is best classified as "Some concerns" for Part 1, while Part 2 is Low. Combined per the algorithm → **Some concerns**.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- Outcome data were not available for all participants: of 85 randomized, 14 withdrew (5 with no reason in the first two months, 6 due to two relapses [2 simvastatin, 4 placebo], 1 chronic exacerbation [placebo], 2 for personal reasons [1 each group]). Per Figure 1, only 42/43 simvastatin and 38/42 placebo patients were included in the final analysis for MANOVA outcomes, although the article states ITT with follow up for the whole period; some patients (5, with no reason) may not have had 6-month data if withdrawal happened early ("those of our dropout cases who did use simvastatin or placebo for 6 months (nine patients) were included in the final analysis" implies **some dropouts before 6 months were excluded from final analysis**).
- **Could missingness depend on true outcome value?** Yes — this is highly plausible: patients who withdrew due to "two attacks" (i.e., experiencing the outcome event itself, relapse) were disproportionately from the placebo group (4 vs 2), and one further placebo patient withdrew due to "chronic exacerbation." This directly ties missingness to the true value of the attack-number outcome, and the reason for missingness (relapse-related withdrawal) differed numerically between arms (placebo: 4+1=5; simvastatin: 2).
- The authors attempted per-protocol and LOCF sensitivity analyses, both of which continued to show a significant difference favoring simvastatin, providing some reassurance; however, these sensitivity analyses do not fully resolve the concern because withdrawal for relapse means the true attack count for those participants is likely undercounted (they stopped being observed for further attacks after leaving), particularly disadvantaging the placebo group's recorded attack count.
- Given the plausible link between missingness and the true outcome (relapse-driven dropout, imbalanced between groups) but with some mitigating sensitivity analyses (per-protocol/LOCF concordant with primary result), this is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** Relapse was defined objectively as "any new or worsening neurological deficit in the absence of high body temperature that lasted for more than 24 h after 1-month stability," a standard and appropriate method for ascertaining MS relapses.
- **Comparability of measurement across groups:** All patients underwent identical scheduled neurological evaluations every 3 months, with the same relapse definition and treatment protocol (IV methylprednisolone) applied to both groups; no evidence that ascertainment methods differed between groups.
- **Blinding of outcome assessors:** The paper states "Four neurologists were asked to assess the patients' eligibility for the study and perform the follow-up examinations. They were blinded to the patients' assignment to treatment group," and medications were dispensed in "similar packages." This directly addresses awareness — assessors were blinded.
- **Potential for influence:** Attack/relapse determination in this study, while it does require some clinical judgement (an "observer-reported outcome involving some judgement"), was assessed by blinded neurologists using a pre-specified operational definition, minimizing the potential for knowledge of intervention to bias the assessment.

Given the outcome assessors were blinded and used a standardized definition, the risk of bias in measurement of the outcome is low.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registry (NCT00668343) prospectively specifies the primary outcome as "attack number" over a "1 year" timeframe, registered with study start in 2005 and posted in April 2008, prior to the 2010 publication.
- The published article reports the attack number/relapse outcome using a single, consistent definition and analysis approach (total relapses during the 1-year study, analyzed via intention-to-treat, Mann-Whitney U-test), which matches the outcome specified in the registry ("attack number," "1 year").
- While the paper additionally reports per-protocol and LOCF sensitivity analyses, these are supplementary and consistent (not seemingly selected because they showed a different or more favorable result — all analyses point the same direction and remain statistically significant), so there is no indication of selective reporting from multiple analyses based on results.
- There is no evidence of selective outcome-measurement reporting (e.g., multiple relapse definitions or unreported time points) — the single relapse definition and total-count metric was consistently used and matches trial registration.

Given consistency between the pre-specified registered outcome and the reported result, and no evidence of selection from multiple eligible analyses/measurements based on results, this domain is judged as low risk of bias.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

Per RoB 2.0 rules, since the domains "bias arising from the randomization process," "deviations from intended interventions," and "missing outcome data" are each judged as "Some concerns" (with none judged "High risk"), the overall risk of bias for the attack number outcome is **Some concerns**. The convergence of "Some concerns" across three domains (particularly regarding allocation concealment reporting and differential relapse-related dropout) suggests these should be interpreted cautiously, but none individually rises to a level that would drive an overall "High risk" judgement.
