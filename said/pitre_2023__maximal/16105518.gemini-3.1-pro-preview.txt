Based on the provided RoB 2.0 guidance and the trial publication, here is the risk of bias assessment for the outcome "change in the operation-specific checklist scores":

**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
a. Judgement: There is insufficient information provided in the manuscript to determine if the allocation sequence was random or if it was concealed. 
b. Rationale: The authors state only that participants "were randomly assigned to three experimental groups." No details regarding the method of random sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated numbers) or the method of allocation concealment (e.g., sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes) are provided. There is also no table of baseline characteristics to assess for potential imbalances, leading to "some concerns" for this domain.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Although participants and personnel were likely aware of the intervention assignment due to the nature of the educational intervention, there is no evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose because of the trial context. 
b. Rationale: The intervention was a 60-minute laboratory practice session for medical students on artificial bones. While blinding to the practice schedule (whole, blocked, or random) is not feasible, this lack of blinding is unlikely to lead to non-protocol interventions or deviations that would bias the intention-to-treat effect in this highly controlled, short-duration lab setting. 

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: It appears that outcome data were available for all or nearly all participants.
b. Rationale: The study enrolled 28 first- and second-year medical students. The entire experiment (acquisition phase and post-tests) took place in a single session (60-minute acquisition followed by immediate and 30-minute delayed post-tests). There is no mention of dropouts, withdrawals, or missing data in the results, implying complete case data for all randomized participants. 

**4. measurement of the outcome: some concerns**
a. Judgement: It is unclear if the outcome assessors (the orthopedic surgeons) were blinded to the participants' intervention groups, and the checklist evaluation involves subjective judgement. 
b. Rationale: The outcome was measured using a checklist by "Two experienced orthopedic surgeons." The article does not state whether these surgeons were blinded to the practice group of the students they were evaluating. Because operation-specific checklists require some degree of subjective clinical judgement (unlike objective automated metrics), knowledge of the assigned group could theoretically influence the assessment. 

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
a. Judgement: There is no reference to a pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial registration to confirm that the reported analyses were finalized before unblinded data were available.
b. Rationale: The researchers analyzed difference scores (post-test minus pre-test) for multiple metrics and across two different time points (immediate and delayed post-tests). Without a date-stamped, pre-specified protocol or trial registry entry, it is not possible to definitively verify that the outcome measurements and analysis methods (e.g., using difference scores rather than final values adjusted for baseline) were not selected based on the results.
