**Outcome assessed:** visual acuity at 3 months after LASIK (Figure 4; cumulative postoperative Snellen visual acuity/efficacy), comparing aspheric ablation (ASA) versus tissue-saving non-aspheric ablation (TSA). The effect of interest is the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The sequence-generation method was random: “*one eye was treated with the MEL80 standard aspheric profile (ASA) and the other eye was treated with the MEL80 non-aspheric profile (TSA), assigned at random using Microsoft Excel’s random number function*.” This is an acceptable random component for allocation-sequence generation.

   However, the report does not describe allocation concealment. It does not state who generated the Excel allocation, whether the allocation was held independently, or whether the surgeon/recruiting personnel could foresee the next eye’s assignment before enrolment or treatment. Therefore, allocation concealment cannot be judged adequate.

   Baseline refractive status appeared balanced: mean preoperative spherical equivalent was −2.43 ± 1.53 D in ASA eyes and −2.54 ± 1.47 D in TSA eyes (p=0.87). The paired-eye design also reduces concern about imbalance in patient-level prognostic factors. Nevertheless, absence of information about allocation concealment prevents a low-risk judgement under RoB 2.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Participants and treating personnel were probably aware of the assigned profile because the interventions were different laser-ablation profiles and no masking procedures were reported. However, for the effect of assignment to treatment, lack of blinding is problematic only if it leads to deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context, affected visual acuity, and were unbalanced between groups.

   The report describes that each eye received its assigned treatment profile and a common surgical approach was used: “*All LASIK procedures were performed using a Hansatome microkeratome … and the MEL80 excimer laser*”; the only randomized component was ASA versus TSA profile. There is no report of crossover, failure to deliver the assigned profile, differential cointerventions, retreatment before the 3-month outcome, or other non-protocol deviations arising because participants or personnel knew the allocation.

   The visual-acuity result in Figure 4 is reported for all 30 eyes at 3 months (“*The graph includes data for 30 eyes at 3 months postop*”), consistent with analysis according to the randomized eye-level assignment. Thus, there is no evidence that the analysis excluded eyes because of treatment received or analysed them according to a non-randomized treatment classification.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   The trial randomized 30 eyes from 15 patients, and the Figure 4 visual-acuity analysis is described as including “*data for 30 eyes at 3 months postop*.” Thus, visual-acuity outcome data appear available for all randomized eyes at the specified follow-up.

   No losses to follow-up, missing visual-acuity assessments, or differential exclusions from the 3-month visual-acuity analysis are reported. The amount of missing outcome data is therefore nil or sufficiently small that it could not plausibly have materially affected the visual-acuity comparison.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Visual acuity is a clinically appropriate and standard outcome for refractive surgery. The same stated visual-acuity assessments were used in both groups: “*Manifest refraction, uncorrected distance visual acuity and CDVA were measured before and 3 months after surgery*.” There is no indication that the testing method, timing, acuity chart, or threshold for visual-acuity measurement differed by randomized profile.

   However, the report does not state that the personnel measuring visual acuity were masked to whether an eye received ASA or TSA, and the participants themselves would probably have been aware that their two eyes had received different treatment profiles. Visual-acuity testing requires participant responses and can involve some examiner judgement or encouragement, so knowledge of treatment could potentially influence measurement at the margin.

   There is no direct evidence that outcome assessment was actually influenced by knowledge of treatment assignment, and visual acuity is less susceptible to expectation effects than a patient-reported quality-of-vision outcome. Nevertheless, because masking of outcome assessors is not reported and the potential for influence cannot be excluded, the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is some concerns rather than low risk.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The publication reports visual acuity at 3 months and presents the result in Figure 4, stating that “*100% of eyes were 20/25 or better*.” It also reports several related refractive-surgery outcomes, including uncorrected visual acuity, CDVA safety, spherical equivalent refraction, defocus equivalent, and stability.

   However, the available information does not provide a sufficiently detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan for visual acuity that was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. In particular, it is not possible to verify from the article whether the 3-month cumulative Snellen visual-acuity presentation, the selected acuity threshold (20/25), and the selected analytic presentation were pre-specified rather than chosen from potentially available alternatives, such as other visual-acuity metrics, thresholds, follow-up time points, or between-group analyses.

   There is no affirmative evidence that the reported visual-acuity result was selected on the basis of statistical significance or favourability; therefore, high risk is not warranted. Nonetheless, in the absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan and with multiple plausible visual-acuity measurements and analyses available, the result raises some concerns regarding selective reporting.
