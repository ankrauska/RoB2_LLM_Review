# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Change in mean sitting DBP from baseline after 4 weeks of therapy
## Effect of Interest: Assignment to intervention (ITT effect)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Random sequence generation:** The article states "Study patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio by using the mixed block randomization (4 and 6 blocks) method." This indicates a random component was used → **Yes**.
- **Allocation concealment:** The article provides no description of how the allocation sequence was concealed from investigators/enrollers. There is no mention of central randomization, sealed envelopes, or any other concealment mechanism. This is an open-label trial (masking: NONE per registry), and use of small, fixed block sizes (4 and 6) without documented concealment procedures raises concern that recruiters could potentially predict upcoming allocations, especially since blocks were small and fixed. → **No information/Probably No**.
- **Baseline imbalance:** Table I shows baseline DBP was well-balanced (104.7 vs 104.5 mm Hg, P=0.89), and most baseline characteristics were similar. However, age differed significantly: "the mean age in the candesartan-HCT group (51.0 [10.2] years) was about 3.2 years higher than that in the candesartan monotherapy group (47.8 [10.7] years; P<0.02) despite random allocation." While age is a known prognostic factor for BP, the magnitude of difference is modest and the DBP/SBP baseline values themselves were balanced, suggesting this is plausibly a chance imbalance rather than evidence of a flawed randomization process. However, the combination of no reported concealment method and a statistically significant baseline imbalance (P<0.02) in a key prognostic variable (age) raises some doubt.

**Overall:** Lack of information on allocation concealment, combined with a statistically significant baseline imbalance in age (a plausible confounder for hypertension trials), leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Participant/personnel awareness:** This was an **open-label** study (per registry: "maskingInfo": "masking": "NONE"; and the article's discussion explicitly states, "the open study design could be the potential source of bias regarding the safety profile"). Therefore, participants, carers, and people delivering the intervention were aware of assigned intervention → **Yes**.
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** There is no explicit evidence that the open-label design led to non-protocol interventions or systematic deviations that arose specifically because of trial context (e.g., no reported crossovers, no reported switching of treatment based on participant/provider preference). The forced titration at week 4 was a per-protocol design feature, not a deviation. There is no information suggesting the lack of blinding led to differential co-interventions or behavior changes between groups related to the *4-week* DBP outcome specifically. → **No information**.
- **Balanced deviations:** No information on this since no deviations were identified due to trial context.
- **Appropriate analysis:** The primary efficacy analysis was conducted on the **ITT population** ("Efficacy data were evaluated in the intent-to-treat (ITT) population... consisted of patients who had baseline BP values and ≥1 postrandomization BP values and who had taken ≥1 dose of study drug"). This is consistent with a modified ITT approach appropriate for assessing the effect of assignment to intervention → **Yes**.

**Overall:** Because participants/personnel were aware of intervention assignment (open-label) and there is no information confirming whether this awareness led to trial-context-driven deviations affecting the 4-week DBP outcome, but the analysis method itself (ITT) was appropriate, this leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" (per the algorithm: Y/PY to 2.1/2.2, NI to 2.3 → Some concerns).

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- For the 4-week DBP outcome (primary outcome), the ITT population included 111/117 (94.9%) in the combination group and 113/116 (97.4%) in the monotherapy group — outcome data were available for nearly all randomized participants at this early timepoint. Per Figure 2, most withdrawals/exclusions occurred later or were related to non-randomization (i.e., 20 patients not randomized due to screening failure, which is separate from missing outcome data post-randomization).
- The proportions of missing data are low and similar between groups (117→111 = 5.1% missing; 116→113 = 2.6% missing), which is unlikely to have created a substantial impact on the 4-week DBP result.
- There is no indication that missingness was related to the true DBP value (e.g., no differential dropout patterns reported specifically affecting the week 4 outcome).

**Overall:** Given the very high completion rate at 4 weeks and comparable proportions of missing data between groups, this domain is judged as low risk of bias for this specific outcome/timepoint.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** DBP was measured using a standardized, automated device (Omron) with a rigorous protocol (3 consecutive measurements, discarding extremes, averaging remaining three; consistent timing relative to dosing; same arm and same assessor for a given patient throughout study). This method is appropriate → **No** (not inappropriate).
- **Differential measurement between groups:** There is no indication that measurement methods differed between the two arms — the same BP measurement protocol was applied to both intervention groups → **No**.
- **Outcome assessor awareness:** DBP is an **observer-reported outcome that does not fundamentally require significant judgement** (it is a machine-measured, objective physiological variable), but the outcome assessor (the investigator taking BP measurements) was **not blinded**, since this was an open-label study. → **Yes**, outcome assessors were aware of intervention assignment.
- **Could assessment be influenced by knowledge of intervention?** Although BP measurement via automated device is relatively objective, subtle influences are possible (e.g., variability in technique, timing of cuff placement, patient positioning, or subconscious rounding) especially since the investigator was not blinded and was aware of group assignment. The RoB2 guidance for "observer-reported outcomes not involving judgement" (e.g., automated tests) suggests these are usually not likely to be influenced by knowledge of the intervention; however, unlike fully automated adjudication (e.g., lab test or mortality), BP is subject to some assessor-related variability (e.g., patient positioning/reassurance), and the unblinded nature of the study introduces a plausible avenue for bias, albeit modest. → **Probably yes**, could be influenced.
- **Likely influenced:** There is no direct evidence (e.g., no reported divergent measurement techniques or documented bias) that assessment was actually influenced in a way that materially biased the result, and the automated device with a structured protocol limits (but does not eliminate) subjectivity. → **Probably no**, not likely to have been actually influenced.

**Overall:** Because the outcome assessors were not blinded (open-label trial) and there is some plausible (though modest) potential for measurement to be influenced by knowledge of intervention, but no strong evidence that this occurred, this leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" rather than "Low" or "High."

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registry (NCT00621153) prospectively specifies the primary outcome as "Changes in Mean Sitting DBP From Baseline After 4 Weeks of Therapy" with a clear description ("Mean of the changed DBP from baseline after 4 weeks") and timeframe (4 weeks) that exactly matches the way it is reported in the published article and results posting.
- The registry results section explicitly reports numerical values for this outcome (combination: -17.0 mm Hg [0.89]; monotherapy: -14.1 mm Hg [0.88]), which are consistent with (very close to) values reported in the article's Table II (-17.8 [10.2] and -14.1 [10.1] for ITT population) — minor differences likely reflect LS means (ANCOVA-adjusted, as pre-specified) versus raw means, which is expected and appropriate given the pre-specified analysis plan.
- There is no evidence that this specific outcome (4-week DBP) was selected from multiple alternative measurements or analyses based on the results — it was the single, clearly pre-specified primary outcome, analyzed using the pre-specified ANCOVA model with baseline BP as covariate ("compared using the ANCOVA model with the treatment as a factor and BP at randomization as covariate"), matching the described analytic approach without evidence of selective reporting of alternative timepoints, scales, or models for this specific outcome.
- No evidence of selective outcome-measurement reporting (e.g., alternative DBP scales or definitions) or selective analysis reporting (e.g., only reporting favorable adjusted vs. unadjusted results) for this specific outcome.

**Overall:** Given the strong concordance between the pre-specified registry outcome definition/timeframe and the reported result, with a clearly pre-specified analysis plan (ANCOVA) that was followed, this domain is judged as low risk of bias.
