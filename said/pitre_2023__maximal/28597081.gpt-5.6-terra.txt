**Outcome assessed:** acoustic reaction time immediately after the 60-minute light exposure, comparing bright light and blue light with the control condition. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Low risk of bias**

### a. Judgement
The randomization process was adequately described: a genuinely random, computer-generated permuted-block sequence was used, with randomly varying block sizes, and allocation was implemented by an uninvolved person using serially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes. There is no indication of meaningful baseline imbalance or unequal group sizes suggesting failure of randomization.

### b. Rationale
The article reports:

> “Participants were allocated randomly to bright light (BRIGHT), monochromatic blue light (BLUE), or a control condition (CONTROL) using an online tool … which applies permuted block randomization with randomly varying block sizes of 3, 6, and 9.”

This supports a random allocation sequence. It further states:

> “The list was transmitted by a non-involved person using serially numbered sealed opaque envelopes.”

This provides reasonable assurance of allocation concealment, although some envelope safeguards (for example, explicit sequential opening only after irrevocable enrolment) were not reported. Nonetheless, use of opaque, sealed, serially numbered envelopes administered by a non-involved person is sufficient to judge concealment as probably adequate.

After the two retrospective exclusions, each arm contained 24 participants, and the reported descriptive information does not indicate an important imbalance suggestive of compromised randomization. Small differences in characteristics, if any, would be compatible with chance in a trial of this size.

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention: **Some concerns**

### a. Judgement
Participants were aware of their own assigned light exposure, although the test supervisor was blinded. There is no evidence that knowledge of allocation led to trial-context deviations, differential cointerventions, or differential implementation that would bias the effect of assignment. However, the primary group comparisons excluded two randomized participants retrospectively; therefore, the analysis was not a full intention-to-treat analysis of all 74 randomized participants. The small number excluded and their apparently pre-intervention, eligibility-measurement-related reason makes a substantial impact unlikely, but this raises some concerns.

### b. Rationale
Regarding awareness, the article states:

> “The test supervisor (RK) was blinded to the light condition each participant was exposed to. Participants only knew the light condition they were exposed to, but not the other two light conditions.”

The trial registration also identifies the study as having:

> “masking: NONE”

Thus, participants were aware of their exposure condition. This is particularly plausible for the bright-light group, given the intended intensity of approximately 4400 lux. Nevertheless, the article does not report that participants’ awareness resulted in differential behaviours, cointerventions, or departures from the protocol attributable to the trial context.

The substantial variation in delivered light intensity is acknowledged:

> “The median … intensities de facto reaching the participants’ eyes were considerably different with 1326 … lx in BRIGHT, 203 … lx in BLUE, and 115 … lx in CONTROL.”

However, for the requested effect of **assignment** to light condition, imperfect delivery or variable individual exposure does not itself indicate bias unless it arose because of the trial context and differed in a way likely to affect outcomes. The reported variation appears related to posture and direction of gaze rather than to differential trial-context cointerventions:

> “This was probably caused by different directions of gaze and sitting postures.”

The main concern is the exclusion of randomized participants:

> “Two subjects in BRIGHT and CONTROL had to be excluded due to invalid measurements of peak oxygen uptake in the cardiorespiratory fitness test.”

The reported analysis therefore included 72 rather than all 74 randomized participants:

> “The remaining 72 participants had a median age of 23 years.”

The investigators used ANCOVA comparing randomized groups, adjusted for pre-exposure reaction time:

> “Analyses of covariance were used to compare reaction time … after the light exposure between the three groups adjusted for reaction time … before the light exposure.”

This is otherwise an appropriate assignment-based analysis. However, exclusion of randomized participants means that it was not a complete intention-to-treat analysis. Since one participant was excluded from BRIGHT and one from CONTROL, and the reason was an apparently pre-randomization/eligibility-assessment problem, the potential influence on the acoustic-reaction-time comparison is unlikely to be substantial. Hence, **some concerns**, rather than high risk, are warranted.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

### c. Judgement
Outcome data were available for 72 of 74 randomized participants (approximately 97%). For this continuous outcome, the amount of missing or excluded outcome information is sufficiently small that it is unlikely to have materially altered the estimated intervention effects. There is no indication that missingness depended on the true acoustic reaction-time outcome.

### d. Rationale
The trial randomized 74 participants:

> “Seventy-four male athletes were allocated at random…”

Two were excluded:

> “Two subjects in BRIGHT and CONTROL had to be excluded due to invalid measurements of peak oxygen uptake in the cardiorespiratory fitness test.”

Thus, the acoustic-reaction-time analysis included 72/74 participants, or about 97% of those randomized. RoB 2 guidance indicates that, for a continuous outcome, outcome data for approximately 95% or more of randomized participants will often be sufficient to regard data as available for “nearly all” participants.

The stated reason for exclusion was not poor reaction time, adverse response to light, withdrawal due to the intervention, or another reason evidently related to the true post-exposure reaction-time value. It concerned invalid measurements from a fitness eligibility assessment. There is no evidence of differential attrition related to acoustic reaction time.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

### a. Judgement
Acoustic reaction time was measured using an appropriate, standardized computer-based task under the same room-light conditions before and after exposure in all groups. The outcome was based on recorded response times rather than subjective judgement, and knowledge of intervention assignment is unlikely to have materially influenced its measurement.

### b. Rationale
The outcome was measured with a standardized computer task:

> “Immediately before, as well as immediately after the light exposure, participants performed a computer-based simple acoustic reaction time task…”

The method was described clearly:

> “The acoustic signal in the reaction time task appeared randomly in intervals of 3–7 s over the duration of 5 min and subjects had to respond as fast as possible by pressing the space bar on a computer keyboard.”

The analysis rule was also prespecified and consistently applied:

> “Reaction times slower than 500 ms (0.5% of all trials) were defined as lapses and not considered… The median of all reaction times was calculated for each subject.”

All groups undertook the reaction-time task in comparable conditions:

> “The reaction time task and the maximum handgrip strength test were performed under room light conditions (~230 lx).”

Although participants knew their own light condition, acoustic reaction time is a performance-based, computer-recorded measure with limited scope for assessor judgement. Participants might theoretically alter effort in response to expectations, but there is no evidence that this occurred, and the brief simple reaction-time task provides a relatively objective measure compared with self-reported outcomes. There is also no indication that task administration, timing, equipment, thresholds, or outcome ascertainment differed between intervention groups.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

### a. Judgement
Acoustic reaction time was prospectively registered as a secondary outcome, and the publication reports the planned adjusted between-group comparison. However, the available registry record does not specify the measurement time point, task definition, planned analysis, covariate adjustment, or a dated statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data became available. Therefore, selection from multiple possible measurements or analyses cannot be fully excluded.

### b. Rationale
The trial was registered as NCT02203539 and listed reaction time as a secondary outcome:

> “Reaction time in milliseconds (ms)”

The article explicitly identifies reaction time as a prespecified secondary outcome:

> “Secondary aims were to assess the influence of bright and blue light on reaction time and handgrip strength which were pre-specified secondary outcomes.”

It also states:

> “All statistical methods used to compare the groups for reaction time and handgrip strength in the present report were pre-specified and registered.”

The reported principal analysis—ANCOVA of post-exposure reaction time adjusted for pre-exposure reaction time—is clinically and statistically reasonable:

> “Analyses of covariance were used to compare reaction time … after the light exposure between the three groups adjusted for reaction time … before the light exposure.”

However, the supplied registration record identifies reaction time only broadly and does not document the following in sufficient detail:

* the exact acoustic reaction-time task;
* the post-exposure time point selected for the primary secondary-outcome analysis;
* use of median reaction time;
* handling of reactions slower than 500 ms;
* the ANCOVA model and baseline adjustment;
* the additional regression analyses using actual light intensity; or
* a prespecified statistical analysis plan dated before access to unblinded results.

The publication reports multiple analyses, including adjusted group comparisons and regression analyses by light intensity. There is no direct evidence that the reported analysis was selected because of its result, but absence of an accessible detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan means that such selection cannot be ruled out. Accordingly, this domain is judged as **some concerns**, not high risk of bias.
