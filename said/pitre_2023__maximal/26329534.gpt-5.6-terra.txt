1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report repeatedly describes participants as randomized to delivery-method groups (postal mail, email, and hybrid) and states that the sample was “stratified by delivery method, questionnaire length, item placement, and gender of respondent.” However, it does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, or random permuted blocks), nor does it describe allocation concealment before assignment.

   There is therefore no basis to judge that allocation was concealed or that the sequence was definitely random, beyond the authors’ use of the term “randomized.” In RoB 2, a statement that a study was randomized without methodological detail is insufficient to establish low risk of bias.

   Baseline characteristics are presented only for survey respondents rather than for all randomized pharmacists. Consequently, the reported differences between delivery-method groups—for example, the proportion Black respondents was 3.2% in postal mail, 26.4% in email, and 19.2% in hybrid—cannot be interpreted as evidence of a flawed randomization process, since response itself is the outcome and selective response can create post-randomization differences. Thus, there is no usable baseline table for assessing imbalance among all randomized participants.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew the assigned delivery approach: recipients could see whether they received a mailed questionnaire, an email invitation, or a mailed postcard directing them to an online survey. Personnel implementing the intervention were also likely aware of delivery method.

   However, for the effect of assignment, awareness alone does not create bias. RoB 2 requires evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, affected the outcome, and were unbalanced between groups. The different reminder procedures were specified components of the delivery strategies, rather than deviations: postal participants received a repeat questionnaire and reminder postcard; email participants received reminder emails; and hybrid participants received repeat postcards.

   The report provides no evidence that trial personnel selectively altered delivery procedures, delivered non-protocol cointerventions, or otherwise caused deviations from assigned survey method that would plausibly affect the probability of response. The reported analysis is also consistent with assignment to delivery strategy: response rates were calculated for the assigned delivery-method groups. Although undeliverable questionnaires were excluded from denominators, these appear to have been treated as ineligible/non-deliverable cases (“incorrect address, deceased person, etc.”), rather than eligible participants selectively excluded because of response-related post-randomization information.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:** For response rate, the outcome was whether an eligible pharmacist returned a completed questionnaire. This outcome was effectively known for all delivered questionnaires: a questionnaire was either returned complete, returned incomplete, or not returned.

   The article reports a clear accounting of the sample:

   > “Out of the original 7200 intended respondents … 233 of them did not receive the questionnaires, which yielded a net of 6967 delivered questionnaires. Of these, 710 were returned with no missing data.”

   Figure 1 similarly reports the numbers allocated, receiving surveys, returning surveys, and completing surveys for each delivery method. Non-return is not missing outcome data for this outcome; it is the outcome category indicating no completed response. Likewise, questionnaires returned with incomplete answers were classified as not meeting the predefined “completed survey” outcome definition, rather than being unobserved response outcomes.

   The exclusion of undeliverable questionnaires is explicitly described and relatively limited (139 in the postal group and 94 in the hybrid group; none in the email group because delivery was guaranteed). These cases could not receive the assigned survey as intended, and the report provides reasons such as invalid address or death. There is no indication that missing outcome information, rather than known non-response/non-deliverability, affected the result.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome—return of a completed questionnaire with no missing items—was defined clearly and measured using objective administrative records. The article states:

   > “The response rate was defined as the percentage of eligible respondents that returned completed questionnaires, with no missing data for any questions.”

   The outcome ascertainment was appropriate for the research question and was applied using the same substantive criterion in every group: a completed questionnaire with no missing data. Although the mode of questionnaire delivery differed by randomized group, that difference was the intervention under evaluation, not differential outcome measurement bias.

   Outcome assessors did not need to make subjective clinical or interpretive judgments about whether a response occurred. Whether a completed mailed survey was received or a complete online questionnaire was submitted is unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of the assigned delivery method. Therefore, differential measurement error is unlikely to have materially biased the comparison of response rates.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Response rate was an explicitly stated study objective and was defined in the Methods section before results were presented:

   > “The three indicators analyzed in this article were the response rate, the average response time, and the economic cost per respondent.”

   The article reports response-rate results by each of the planned classifications: delivery method, questionnaire length, demographic-item placement, and recorded gender. This is reassuring, and there is no direct evidence that the reported response-rate result was selected from multiple reported response definitions or analyses because it was statistically favorable.

   Nevertheless, low risk of bias under RoB 2 requires confidence that the data were analysed according to a sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The available materials do not provide a dated statistical analysis plan specifying the precise response-rate denominator, handling of undeliverable questionnaires, analysis population, comparisons, and planned statistical tests. The report states that chi-square tests were used, but this alone does not demonstrate prespecification.

   In addition, response rate could plausibly have been operationalized in more than one eligible way—for example, completed surveys only versus all returned surveys, or original randomized sample versus delivered/eligible sample. The investigators chose “completed questionnaires, with no missing data” and adjusted denominators for undeliverable questionnaires. These choices are reasonable and described, but the available information does not establish that they were finalized before outcome data were known. Therefore, there are some concerns rather than low risk of bias.
