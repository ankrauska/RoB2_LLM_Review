**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The allocation sequence was adequately generated and concealed, and there is no evidence of problematic baseline imbalances.
b. Rationale: The trial report states, "eligible patients were assigned to escitalopram or placebo treatment according to a computer-generated randomisation list. The details of the randomisation series were unknown to any of the investigators and were contained in a set of sealed opaque envelopes." Baseline characteristics (Table 1) were comparable between the groups.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Participants and personnel were blinded to the assigned intervention, minimizing the risk of deviations arising from the trial context. An appropriate intention-to-treat analysis was used to estimate the effect of assignment.
b. Rationale: The authors explicitly mention that "Study medications were tablets for oral administration, of identical appearance, taste or smell" and "All study personnel and participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire duration of the study." Additionally, "All efficacy analyses in the double-blind treatment period were conducted using the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, which consisted of all randomised patients who took at least one dose of trial medication and had at least one post-randomisation efficacy assessment."

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
c. Judgement: Outcome data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants through survival analysis, and the rates and reasons for withdrawal (aside from relapse) were low and balanced between groups.
d. Rationale: For time-to-event outcomes, censored patients are accounted for in the log-rank and Cox analyses. The authors note, "the overall withdrawal rate, excluding relapses, was comparable for both treatments: 7.9% for patients treated with escitalopram (13 patients), and 8.9% for patients treated with placebo (14 patients)." Thus, it is unlikely that missingness depends on the true value of the outcome in a way that biases the result.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The methods used to measure the outcome were appropriate and comparable between groups, and outcome assessors were blinded to the intervention assignment.
b. Rationale: Relapse was defined as "an increase in the Y-BOCS total score of ≥5 points, or lack of efficacy as judged by the investigator." Both patients and evaluating investigators were blinded: "All study personnel and participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire duration of the study."

**5. selective reporting: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The reported result aligns with the pre-specified primary analysis plan and does not appear to have been selected from multiple analyses or outcome measurements based on the results.
b. Rationale: The authors state, "The prospectively defined primary analysis of efficacy was the time to relapse from the start date of double-blind treatment." The time to relapse outcome and its definition (increase in Y-BOCS ≥5 points or lack of efficacy) were fully reported as intended, indicating low risk of selective reporting bias.
