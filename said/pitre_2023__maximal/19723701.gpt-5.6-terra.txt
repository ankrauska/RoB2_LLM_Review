1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The trial was described as randomized and used stratification by clinical centre and ischemic status, with “an algorithm that ensured near balance in each stratum.” Baseline characteristics were closely similar between groups, with no imbalance suggesting a material failure of randomization. For example, mean age was 64 versus 65 years, male sex 75.6% versus 74.7%, mean ejection fraction 0.24 in both groups, and QRS duration ≥150 ms in 65.1% versus 64.2% (ICD-only versus CRT–ICD).

   However, the report does not state that the allocation algorithm contained a random component, nor does it report how allocation was concealed from personnel enrolling participants. The description of an algorithm achieving near-balance could be consistent with minimization, but the presence of a random element is not stated. Similarly, there is no description of central randomization, a secure web/telephone system, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes.

   Thus:
   - Sequence generation: **no information / probably random in the context of this large multicentre trial**, but not adequately described.
   - Allocation concealment: **no information**.
   - Baseline imbalance: **no**, because observed differences appear compatible with chance.

   Under RoB 2, lack of information about allocation concealment, despite reassuring baseline balance, leads to **some concerns** rather than low risk.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to CRT–ICD versus ICD-only at baseline. The trial used an intention-to-treat analysis: “Data analysis was performed according to the intention-to-treat principle,” and the registry likewise states that “Data from all patients enrolled were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis.” This is appropriate for the effect of assignment.

   Participants and treating physicians were not blinded. The article states: “The treating physicians were aware of study-group assignments,” and the registry specifies “masking: NONE.” There were also departures from the assigned intervention:
   - In the CRT–ICD group, 11/1089 did not receive a device, and 82/1089 (7.5%) received an ICD-only device because of technical difficulty positioning the CRT lead.
   - In the ICD-only group, 19/731 did not receive a device, and 91/731 (12.4%) received CRT–ICD during follow-up. Of these, 30 received CRT–ICD “at the physician’s discretion before reaching an end point,” while 61 crossed over after a heart-failure event.

   For the assignment effect, ordinary non-adherence, technical failure, or treatment changes after clinical deterioration do not necessarily constitute bias-producing deviations. The crossovers after a heart-failure event cannot have affected occurrence of that first event. However, the 30 pre-endpoint physician-discretion crossovers, in an unblinded trial, could plausibly reflect trial-context-related management differences. The report does not provide enough information to determine whether these deviations were caused by awareness of allocation, whether they affected the composite outcome, or whether they were balanced in a way that would eliminate concern.

   Therefore, despite an appropriate ITT analysis, unblinded care and incomplete information about potentially relevant pre-endpoint deviations lead to **some concerns**.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   c. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   d. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data were not clearly available for all or nearly all randomized participants. The article reports that 44/1089 (4.0%) participants in the CRT–ICD group and 55/731 (7.5%) in the ICD-only group “declined to continue participating in the study, were withdrawn by a physician, or were lost to follow-up.” This represents 99/1820 participants (5.4%) overall, with appreciably more such participants in the ICD-only group.

   The primary outcome was a time-to-first-event composite including nonfatal heart-failure events. Participants who discontinue follow-up or are lost may have unobserved heart-failure events or deaths after censoring. The report does not provide:
   - outcome-event ascertainment after withdrawal or loss to follow-up;
   - reasons for missing primary-outcome data separately by randomized group;
   - numbers censored, censoring times, or evidence that censoring was non-informative;
   - sensitivity analyses showing that plausible missing outcomes would not alter the result.

   The imbalance in non-completion/loss (7.5% ICD-only versus 4.0% CRT–ICD) is concerning because missingness could plausibly be related to health status, including deterioration, treatment dissatisfaction, or heart-failure events. The composite result was driven principally by heart-failure events, for which continued clinical ascertainment is particularly important. Although the registry states that all enrolled participants were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis, this does not itself demonstrate complete outcome ascertainment or address potentially informative censoring.

   Accordingly, missingness could, and is plausibly likely to, depend on the true outcome value; the differential amount of discontinuation/loss supports a **high risk of bias**.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   All-cause death is an objective component of the composite outcome, but the primary result was largely driven by nonfatal heart-failure events: 151 CRT–ICD versus 167 ICD-only first heart-failure events, whereas deaths as first events were relatively few. The heart-failure-event component required clinical judgement and management decisions. Specifically, a heart-failure event required signs and symptoms consistent with congestive heart failure plus intravenous decongestive therapy or an augmented in-hospital decongestive regimen.

   The article explicitly states:  
   > “The diagnosis of heart failure, which was made by physicians who were aware of study-group assignments…”  

   and later acknowledges:  
   > “Members of the heart-failure adjudication committee were unaware of study-group assignments, but the investigators who decided on therapy or hospital admission for patients with heart failure were aware of such assignments. It is possible that the investigators’ knowledge of study-group assignment contributed in some way to the lower frequency of heart failure in the CRT–ICD group.”

   Blinded independent adjudication and prespecified criteria are important safeguards. However, blinded adjudication cannot fully eliminate bias if unblinded treating clinicians influence whether symptoms are labelled as heart failure, whether patients are admitted, or whether intensified decongestive therapy is given—the very actions required for an event to meet the endpoint definition. Thus, ascertainment of the heart-failure component could have been influenced by knowledge of allocated treatment.

   Because the composite outcome’s apparent benefit was driven by this susceptible heart-failure component, and because the authors themselves acknowledge the potential influence of unblinded clinical decisions, this domain is judged **high risk of bias**.

5. **Selective reporting: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The trial was prospectively registered (NCT00180271; first posted September 16, 2005) and the registered primary outcome is consistent with the published primary endpoint:

   - Registry: “Mortality From Any Cause or First Heart Failure (HF) Event.”
   - Publication: “The primary end point was death from any cause or nonfatal heart-failure events (whichever came first).”

   The registered outcome definition and follow-up framework are consistent with those reported in the article. The trial was designed as an event-driven group-sequential trial, and the article reports the prespecified monitoring structure, including the Wang–Tsiatis group-sequential design, planned interim analyses, and prespecified efficacy boundary. The report states that the trial was stopped after the ninth planned analysis because the prespecified efficacy boundary was crossed.

   The principal result reported corresponds to the registered and prespecified primary endpoint and primary ITT comparison: HR 0.66 (95% CI 0.52–0.84). The registry similarly reports the ITT time-to-first-event comparison and HR of 0.66 (95% CI 0.52–0.84). There is no evidence that the reported primary composite result was selected from multiple eligible outcome definitions, time points, or analyses on the basis of the findings.

   Although a full dated statistical analysis plan is not available in the supplied material, the protocol, registry, and publication are consistent regarding the primary outcome and analysis. Therefore, selective reporting for this specific result is judged at **low risk of bias**.
