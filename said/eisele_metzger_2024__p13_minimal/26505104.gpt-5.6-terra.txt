1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - Participants are stated to have been “randomly allocated” to the four instructional conditions (SC, BC, CB, FL). However, the report gives no account of the random-sequence generation method (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table) and no information on allocation concealment.  
   - This is particularly important because recruitment was by convenience sampling over several years and across two institutions. Without concealment, investigators enrolling participants could potentially foresee or influence assignment.  
   - No baseline diagnostic pretest was undertaken. The authors acknowledge that they “cannot conclusively rule out an imbalance between the conditions or professional groups.” Although the groups appear broadly similar in professional composition, baseline knowledge relevant to the neurology/rheumatology materials was not measured.  
   - There is no direct evidence that allocation was manipulated or that assignment was non-random. Nevertheless, “randomized” alone is insufficient reassurance in the absence of sequence-generation and concealment details.  

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**  
   - The interventions were delivered individually through a custom computer program. This standardised delivery is likely to have limited differences in implementation other than the intended differences in instructional content and ordering.  
   - Participants were instructed to read the allocated explanations and had the same maximum learning time (30 minutes). Learning time and time per diagnostic vignette reportedly did not differ statistically between groups.  
   - Blinding of participants to the nature of their instructional material was not realistically possible: the materials visibly differed in whether and how basic science information and causal links were presented. However, lack of participant blinding is inherent to this educational intervention and does not itself demonstrate a deviation from the assigned condition.  
   - There is no indication that participants crossed over between conditions, received co-interventions, or were exposed to material from another study arm during the learning session. The one-week reminder supplied only the names of disorders and was apparently applied to all groups.  
   - Thus, there is no persuasive evidence that deviations from the intended instructional conditions, beyond those inherent in the intervention, biased delayed diagnostic performance.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
   - Delayed diagnostic performance required return one week later. Five enrolled participants did not complete the delayed session (three CB and two BC) and were excluded from analysis. Attrition was therefore confined to two intervention groups and was proportionally greater in CB.  
   - More seriously, two additional participants—one each in BC and CB—were excluded after the delayed diagnostic assessment because they spent less than five minutes on the test and obtained extremely low scores (0% and 8%). These exclusions were based on post-randomisation behaviour and outcome-related information. Very poor delayed performance is precisely the outcome of interest; removing such observations can favour the affected groups.  
   - The exclusions were not handled using an intention-to-treat analysis, and no sensitivity analysis was reported to show whether inclusion of these low scores, or plausible values for those missing delayed data, would alter the group-by-time interaction.  
   - The flow and arithmetic are also insufficiently clear. The initial group sizes total 101 (SC 26, BC 25, CB 25, FL 25), whereas the stated final analysis sample is 94. The reported five non-completers plus two post hoc exclusions would suggest seven exclusions, but the group totals and narrative do not reconcile cleanly—especially the reduction from SC 26 to SC 25 despite no SC non-completer being described.  
   - Because missingness and exclusions were differential across groups, include outcome-based exclusions, and are not transparently accounted for, the risk of bias in delayed diagnostic performance is high.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low risk**  
   - **Outcome assessed: delayed diagnostic performance.** This was measured one week after instruction using an alternate version of a 12-item clinical-vignette test. For each vignette, participants selected one diagnosis from four options. The outcome is therefore a relatively objective, pre-specified score (number/proportion correct), rather than a subjective participant-reported judgement such as perceived learning, enjoyment, or confidence.  
   - Scoring was likely automated by the computer program or, at minimum, determined against fixed correct answers. The report gives no indication that assessors knew group assignments or exercised discretionary judgement in scoring.  
   - Different diagnostic-test versions were counterbalanced between immediate and delayed assessment, and the authors state that item analysis indicated similar test difficulty.  
   - Participants could not realistically be blinded to the type of instructional material received. However, knowledge of the allocated instructional format is unlikely to alter the objectively scored answer key directly. Any effect on effort or learning is more appropriately considered under deviations from intended interventions, not outcome measurement.  
   - The outcome materials have been used in prior studies and were reviewed by an independent clinical expert, although this does not eliminate concerns about external validity. Such concerns are not, by themselves, evidence of differential measurement bias between randomised groups.  

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The authors identify diagnostic-test performance as the “primary analysis,” and report the group-by-time interaction relevant to retention/delayed diagnostic performance. This is reassuring.  
   - However, no prospectively registered protocol, statistical analysis plan, or time-stamped trial registration is provided. It is therefore impossible to verify that the primary endpoint, analytic model, covariates, contrasts, and subgroup/comparison choices were specified before outcome data were examined.  
   - The ANCOVA covariates were described as having been selected “based on the correlations” between performance and timing variables. This data-informed covariate selection permits analytic flexibility.  
   - The report presents multiple outcomes (memory performance, immediate diagnostic performance, delayed diagnostic performance), several group comparisons, and post hoc least-significant-difference testing. Least-significant-difference testing is relatively permissive and, without a pre-specified contrast plan or adjustment for multiplicity, increases the opportunity for selectively emphasising nominally significant comparisons.  
   - The reported significant interaction is compatible with the study hypothesis, but the absence of a protocol or pre-specified analysis plan leaves some concern that the chosen analysis and highlighted comparisons were selected after examining the data.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The overall judgement is **high risk of bias**, principally because delayed diagnostic outcome data were handled in a manner likely to bias the result: attrition was differential by group, two exceptionally poor delayed-test performers were excluded after outcome assessment, no intention-to-treat or sensitivity analysis was reported, and participant-flow reporting is internally difficult to reconcile.  
   - There are also some concerns about inadequate reporting of randomisation and the absence of a pre-specified protocol or analysis plan. These issues further reduce confidence in the reported advantage in delayed diagnostic performance for science-based causal explanations.
