1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk** (The investigators state that allocation was generated using a computer-generated randomization list and that allocation concealment was maintained through telephone inquiry. These are appropriate methods and make foreknowledge or manipulation of allocation unlikely. The groups appeared reasonably similar in reported baseline characteristics, although the abdominal-hysterectomy group had somewhat greater median body weight and estimated uterine weight. More importantly, surgeons were more often attending physicians in the LAVH group than in the AH group [71% versus 40%], but this is an imbalance in treatment delivery rather than evidence of a flawed randomization sequence or concealment process.)

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns** (There were no reported conversions from LAVH to AH, and the article does not report crossovers or exclusions after randomization. Thus, participants appear to have received their allocated operation. However, surgical expertise differed materially between groups: LAVH was performed more frequently by attending surgeons, whereas AH was more frequently performed by residents under attending supervision; the difference was statistically significant. The authors themselves acknowledge that the LAVH surgeons were significantly more experienced. Operator experience can affect postoperative recovery and discharge readiness, and therefore hospital stay, independently of the surgical approach. This imbalance may represent a systematic departure from a comparable implementation of the allocated interventions. In addition, discharge was explicitly left to each patient rather than governed by prespecified, uniform discharge criteria. Although this chiefly affects outcome measurement, it also permits post-randomization expectations and care practices to influence the endpoint.)

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk** (Hospital stay is reported for all randomized participants: 28 allocated to LAVH and 20 allocated to AH. There were no reported deaths, withdrawals, conversions, or missing hospital-stay observations, and the denominators in the hospital-stay table correspond to the randomized group sizes. The lower response rate to the later postoperative questionnaire is irrelevant to the endpoint assessed here, length of hospital stay.)

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—length of hospital stay: high-risk** (Length of stay, recorded as days in hospital, may superficially appear objective. However, its ascertainment in this trial was highly vulnerable to knowledge of intervention and to subjective decision-making. Neither patients nor clinicians could be blinded to LAVH versus open abdominal hysterectomy. Crucially, the protocol states: “Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital.” There were no stated standardized discharge criteria, no blinded assessor, and no evidence that discharge decisions were made independently of patient expectations or clinician preferences. The authors acknowledge elsewhere that patients could not be blinded and that expectations might influence recovery-related outcomes. LAVH was a newer, minimally invasive procedure likely associated with favourable expectations, while AH involved a visibly larger incision and potentially different clinician advice. Moreover, reimbursement was reportedly based on hospital days in the German system, which may also have influenced discharge practices. Thus, the recorded number of hospital days was likely influenced by awareness of allocated treatment in a manner plausibly related to the observed effect.)

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns** (The manuscript explicitly identifies length of hospital stay as the primary outcome and gives a sample-size calculation based on reducing median stay from 9 to 7 days. The result for hospital stay is reported for both groups, with numerical estimates (median 6.5 versus 10 days) and an assertion of statistical significance. This is reassuring. However, no trial registration, publicly available protocol, dated statistical analysis plan, or prespecified detailed analytic approach is provided. Therefore, it is not possible to verify whether the chosen summary measure, analysis method, timing, or presentation of hospital stay was specified before outcome data were examined. The report gives medians and interquartile ranges but does not provide an effect estimate with confidence interval. These limitations leave some concern about selective analytic reporting, although there is no direct evidence that the primary outcome itself was omitted or substituted.)

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias** (The outcome-specific risk of bias in measurement of length of hospital stay is high: discharge timing was determined by unblinded patients without standardized discharge criteria, in a comparison where expectations and provider behaviour could plausibly differ by surgical approach. There are also some concerns regarding unequal surgeon experience and absence of a verifiable prespecified analysis plan. Consequently, confidence that the observed shorter hospital stay after LAVH reflects the causal effect of surgical approach, rather than differences in expectations, discharge decisions, and treatment delivery, is substantially reduced.)
