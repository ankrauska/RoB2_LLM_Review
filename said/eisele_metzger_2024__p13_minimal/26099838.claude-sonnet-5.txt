# Risk of Bias Assessment: "Three-dimensional printing models improve understanding of spinal fracture—A randomized controlled study in China"

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns.** The manuscript states students were "randomized into three groups using a computer program," but no further detail is provided regarding the random sequence generation method (e.g., random number generator, block randomization) or, critically, **allocation concealment**. There is no description of who generated the allocation sequence, who enrolled participants, or whether assignment was concealed until after enrollment. Given the study design, blinding of participants to their allocated intervention was inherently impossible (students obviously knew whether they were viewing CT films, 3D screen images, or holding physical 3D-printed models), which is unavoidable but is compounded by the lack of detail on concealment prior to allocation. Baseline characteristics (age, sex, school year) appear balanced across groups, which is reassuring, but the absence of methodological detail on concealment precludes a "low risk" rating.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns.** No blinding of participants or personnel was possible or attempted (inherent to the intervention), and this is not explicitly acknowledged as a limitation regarding performance bias. Additionally, "a teacher provided a detailed interpretation of the fracture in each group" — there is no description of standardization of this teaching input across the three arms (e.g., identical script, teacher blinded to hypothesis, same teacher(s) across groups, equal time spent). Variability in teacher enthusiasm or emphasis (particularly if teachers were not blinded to the study hypothesis favoring 3Dp) could differentially influence outcomes, especially the subjective evaluation questions. No mention of a pre-specified analysis excluding protocol deviations, though notably no participants were reported as deviating from assigned group.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Low risk.** The manuscript explicitly states "a total of 120 medical students... participated, and no one dropped out." All randomized participants (40/40/40) appear to be included in the final analysis for both the knowledge test and evaluation questions, with complete data reported in Tables 2 and 3.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome
Assessed separately for each outcome type:

**(a) Knowledge test — sum score of correct answers on 10 anatomical questions and time to completion:**
**Low risk.** This is an objective, answer-key-based outcome (multiple choice/binary correct answers), scored against a pre-defined correct answer key (Table 1) and timed by an invigilator. While the invigilator/grader was not explicitly stated to be blinded to group assignment, the objective nature of the outcome (matching answers to a fixed key) makes assessor blinding largely irrelevant to differential bias.

**(b) Evaluative questions (#11–14: pleasure, assistance, effect, confidence):**
**High risk.** These are participant-reported subjective outcomes. Since participants could not be blinded to their allocated teaching modality (they knew whether they used CT images, a 3D screen model, or handled a physical printed model), their responses to "Did you enjoy this?" "Was this helpful?" "How confident are you?" are highly susceptible to expectation and novelty effects. Students in the 3Dp arm, aware they received a novel, tactile, "high-tech" tool, would plausibly rate their experience more favorably irrespective of true learning benefit. This represents a substantial and unaddressed source of bias for this outcome domain.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Some concerns.** No trial registration number, published protocol, or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is referenced anywhere in the manuscript. It is therefore impossible to verify whether the 10 anatomical questions, 4 evaluation questions, and the specific statistical comparisons (multiple post-hoc pairwise tests, sex-by-group interactions) were pre-specified or were selected/emphasized post hoc based on which comparisons reached significance. The manuscript reports numerous secondary/exploratory subgroup analyses (sex differences within each modality) without correction for multiple comparisons or a stated a priori hypothesis about sex interactions, raising concern about selective emphasis of significant findings (e.g., the isolated finding that males outperformed females only in the 3D group is highlighted extensively in the abstract/discussion despite being a subgroup finding with borderline p-value, p = 0.0508).

## 6. Overall rating: **High risk of bias**

**Justification:** While the primary knowledge-test outcome and missing data domains are low risk, the combination of (i) some concerns regarding randomization/allocation concealment reporting, (ii) some concerns regarding standardization of teaching delivery across arms, (iii) high risk of bias for the subjective, unblinded evaluative outcomes (pleasure, assistance, effect, confidence) — which form a key part of the paper's conclusions ("equally appreciated... by both sexes," "3Dp model... more predominant" ratings) — and (iv) some concerns about selective outcome reporting in the absence of a registered protocol, cumulatively and substantially lower confidence in the reported results. The high-risk rating for the subjective outcome measurement alone, combined with multiple "some concerns" domains, is sufficient to warrant an overall "high risk" judgment.
