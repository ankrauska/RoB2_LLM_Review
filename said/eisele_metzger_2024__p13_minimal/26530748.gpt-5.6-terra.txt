1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns** (The manuscript states that 427 patients were “randomly allocated” to EBRT or vaginal brachytherapy, but gives no information on the sequence-generation method—such as computer-generated randomization or random-number tables—or on allocation concealment. The cited primary trial is described as open-label, so blinding of patients and treating personnel was not feasible. Lack of blinding itself is not necessarily a randomization-process defect, but the absence of reported allocation concealment leaves a material possibility that group assignment could have been anticipated at enrolment. Baseline HRQL scores were compared and the report does not identify important baseline imbalances, but this does not establish that allocation was adequately concealed.)

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk** (The compared interventions, postoperative EBRT and vaginal brachytherapy, were clearly defined and are inherently difficult to blind. No evidence is presented of systematic treatment crossovers, differential discontinuation of radiotherapy, or important unbalanced co-interventions caused by knowledge of treatment allocation. Although open-label treatment could influence patients’ experiences and reporting of quality of life, that concern principally affects measurement of this subjective patient-reported outcome and is addressed in domain 4. There is insufficient evidence that deviations from the assigned radiotherapy strategy, rather than the assigned intervention itself, biased the global-health result.)

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk** (The long-term HRQL analysis was restricted to patients who had provided baseline and at least one follow-up questionnaire: 348/427 randomized participants (81%). For the 7- and 10-year assessments, questionnaires were sent only to patients who were previous responders, alive, disease-free according to the trial database, and had a current address. Thus, the analysis population was selected after randomization on survival, disease status, and prior response. At 7 years, 205/265 eligible patients returned a questionnaire (77%); at 10 years, 80/119 returned one (67%). In addition, only 76.2% of the evaluable long-term questionnaires had all QLQ-C30 items completed, although the exact completeness for the two global-health items is not reported. Responders at 7 years were younger and had fewer comorbidities than the full randomized cohort. These features make it plausible that missingness and exclusion were related to global health/quality of life, and possibly differed by treatment arm. No convincing analysis is reported to show that missing global-health data were unrelated to their unobserved values, nor is a sensitivity analysis presented that would support robustness to informative missingness.)

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — EORTC QLQ-C30 Global health: high risk** (Global health was measured using the two patient-reported EORTC QLQ-C30 global quality-of-life items, scored on a 0–100 scale. This is a validated instrument and the same questionnaire was used in both groups, which supports appropriate and consistent outcome measurement. However, the trial was open-label: participants knew whether they had received pelvic EBRT or vaginal brachytherapy. Global health/quality of life is intrinsically subjective and is directly susceptible to expectations about treatment toxicity, prior symptoms, and beliefs about the relative desirability of the treatments. Since patients themselves assessed the outcome, neither outcome assessors nor participants were blinded. The article does not describe measures that could plausibly prevent knowledge of intervention assignment from influencing responses. Consequently, measurement of this subjective patient-reported endpoint has a high risk of bias.)

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns** (The analysis includes numerous QLQ-C30 domains, symptom subscales, single items, several time points, and both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. The manuscript describes the statistical model and notes a stricter significance threshold of .01 for multiple testing, which is helpful. However, no trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial registration specifying the long-term 7- and 10-year global-health endpoint, the exact analysis population, time point, and model is provided in the material available. Global health is reported as nonsignificant, but it is unclear whether the reported longitudinal contrast and presentation were fully prespecified, particularly given the extensive outcome and time-point multiplicity. There is therefore no basis to conclude confidently that the reported global-health result was selected from multiple eligible analyses without knowledge of the results.)

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias** (The result for EORTC QLQ-C30 Global health is at high risk of bias because of substantial, potentially informative long-term missing outcome data and because this subjective patient-reported outcome was assessed in an open-label trial. Concerns about inadequately reported randomization/concealment and selective reporting further reduce confidence.)
