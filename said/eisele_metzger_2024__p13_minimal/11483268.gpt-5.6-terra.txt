1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The study is described as “randomised,” with 64 participants assigned to Vaqta and 63 to Avaxim, and the baseline demographic characteristics appear broadly comparable.  
   - However, the report gives no information on how the random sequence was generated (for example, computer-generated sequence, random-number table, blocking, or stratification). Thus, it cannot be verified that allocation was truly random.  
   - Crucially, allocation concealment is not described. In an explicitly open-label study, lack of blinding need not itself mean that allocation was unconcealed, but there is no evidence that the person recruiting participants could not foresee or manipulate the next assignment.  
   - Therefore, the randomization process cannot be judged low risk.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
   - Participants were allocated to receive a single booster dose of either Vaqta or Avaxim, administered intramuscularly at the scheduled 24–28-week interval after priming with Avaxim.  
   - The study was open-label. Nevertheless, for the endpoint of anti-HAV antibody GMT one month after vaccination, participants’ and staff’s awareness of the administered vaccine is unlikely to cause meaningful departures from the assigned biological intervention or materially alter antibody production.  
   - No systematic co-interventions, differential follow-up schedules, differential blood-sampling procedures, or treatment crossovers are reported.  
   - One participant reportedly received an earlier hepatitis-A booster and another travelled to India between visits; these were protocol deviations handled by exclusion from the per-protocol analysis. These isolated events do not indicate a systematic imbalance in deviations caused by knowledge of allocation.  
   - Although the report says that both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses were undertaken, only per-protocol results are presented. That omission is more properly considered under selection of the reported result rather than evidence that the intended interventions were not delivered.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - Of 127 randomised and vaccinated participants, 121 were included in the per-protocol immunogenicity analysis: 59/64 in the Vaqta group and 62/63 in the Avaxim group. Thus, six participants were excluded, including four without both pre- and post-vaccination serological results.  
   - Missing serological outcome data therefore affected at least 4/127 participants (approximately 3%). This is not a large absolute proportion, but the distribution of the four missing post/pre serology results by randomised group is not reported.  
   - In addition, the per-protocol exclusions were imbalanced overall (five exclusions in the Vaqta group versus one in the Avaxim group). Not all exclusions were for missing data, but this imbalance makes it difficult to exclude differential effects on the analysed GMTs.  
   - The authors state that intention-to-treat and per-protocol results were “close enough,” but do not report the intention-to-treat results, nor describe methods for handling missing antibody values.  
   - Since the small amount of missing data is unlikely by itself to overturn a large observed antibody response, this is not necessarily high risk; nevertheless, insufficient information prevents a low-risk judgement.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — anti-HAV antibody geometric mean titre (GMT) 1 month post-vaccination: low-risk**  
   - This endpoint is based on a laboratory measurement of anti-HAV antibody concentration, rather than a participant-reported or clinically subjective outcome.  
   - Samples were obtained at a defined follow-up visit, 21–35 days after booster vaccination, and analysed using a specified quantitative modified HAVAB assay, calibrated against a WHO standard serum immune globulin. The lower measurement limit is reported as 6 mIU/mL.  
   - Although the trial was open-label and it is not stated whether laboratory personnel were blinded to vaccine allocation, knowledge of allocation is unlikely to influence a standardised quantitative serological assay materially.  
   - The outcome definition and assay method are sufficiently clear, and there is no indication that measurement methods differed by intervention group.  
   - Accordingly, measurement bias for this objective laboratory endpoint is unlikely.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The manuscript identifies anti-HAV GMT one month post-vaccination as the primary evaluation criterion and reports this outcome, including group-specific GMTs and confidence intervals. This is reassuring.  
   - However, no protocol, statistical analysis plan, trial registration record, or prospectively specified analysis document is available in the material provided. It is therefore impossible to verify that the reported time window, analysis population, non-inferiority margin, transformations, and statistical methods were all specified before outcome data were examined.  
   - The study states that both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses were performed, but reports only the per-protocol analysis because the authors judged the results “close enough.” The unreported intention-to-treat results are particularly relevant in a non-inferiority comparison, where reliance on per-protocol analyses can favour a conclusion of non-inferiority.  
   - The one-sided non-inferiority approach and four-fold margin are described, but the absence of the full planned analysis results and of a protocol leaves some concern about selective choice of the reported analysis.

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   - The outcome measurement itself is at low risk of bias, and there is no persuasive evidence of systematic deviations from the assigned booster interventions.  
   - However, the randomization method and allocation concealment are undocumented; missing serology data and exclusions are incompletely accounted for; and only per-protocol rather than intention-to-treat immunogenicity results are reported despite both analyses reportedly having been conducted.  
   - These issues collectively justify an overall judgement of **some concerns** for the anti-HAV antibody GMT one month post-vaccination.
