# Risk of Bias Assessment: Bond Failure Rate Trial (Transbond™ Plus SEP vs Conventional Etch/Primer)

## Endpoint: Failure Rate

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: The randomization method itself appears robust—block randomization (Roberts and Torgerson, 1998) was used to allocate etching systems to quadrants, with the stated property that any 10 consecutive subjects would contain 5 with SEP on the upper right and 5 on the upper left. Allocation concealment was reasonably addressed, as "operators were unaware of the assignment until the patient was entered into the trial." However, I have concerns: (1) no detail is given on the sequence generation for the split-mouth cross-quadrant design beyond the upper right quadrant—how were the remaining three quadrants allocated? This is unclear and raises questions about whether the full mouth randomization scheme was equally rigorous. (2) The operators, by necessity, could not be blinded to the intervention during placement due to differing application techniques—this is inherent to the intervention and not strictly a randomization flaw, but it does raise possibility of subtle differential technique/attention leaking bias into the conduct of the trial from the point of allocation. (3) Baseline balance between groups is not explicitly reported (e.g., number of teeth by type between the two systems), though the split-mouth design mitigates this somewhat. On balance, the process is described with reasonable rigor but incomplete detail introduces some concern.

---

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: This was an unblinded trial for operators (unavoidable due to differing application steps), which is appropriate to flag as a deviation risk since operator awareness of allocation during application could introduce differential care, pressure, or technique despite intending to follow manufacturer protocols. The manuscript states the protocol for each system was followed "according to manufacturer's instructions," suggesting adherence to intended intervention delivery. There is no mention of any protocol deviations, co-interventions, or non-adherence being tracked or analyzed (e.g., no per-protocol vs intention-to-treat distinction is discussed, though given the immediate/objective nature of bracket bonding, this is less critical here). The analysis appears to include all randomized brackets as bonded (appropriate ITT-like handling). However, absence of explicit reporting on protocol fidelity/deviations, combined with unblinded operators performing a technique-sensitive procedure, introduces some concern that deviations (even unintentional, e.g., differential moisture control or extra care with the "new" material) could have influenced outcome.

---

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: LOW RISK**

Justification: The manuscript explicitly states that "all patients were observed for the entire 12-month period" except one patient who moved away after 7 months, during which no failures occurred; his data were retained via appropriate survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier), which correctly handles censored data. This is a very low rate of missingness (1/39 patients, partial follow-up) and was handled with an appropriate statistical method (censoring within survival analysis) rather than exclusion. There is no indication of missing bracket-level outcome data—every bonded bracket's failure/non-failure status over the observation period appears accounted for. This domain is well handled.

---

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — Failure Rate: LOW RISK**

Justification: Bracket failure (debonding) is an objective, clinically verifiable outcome (a bracket is either physically attached to the tooth or not)—this is not a patient-reported/subjective outcome like pain or satisfaction, so the concern about patient blinding influencing subjective symptom reporting does not apply here. Patients were blinded to which system was used on which side ("patients were unaware which system had been used on each side of the mouth"), reducing the possibility that a patient could bias reporting even if this were relevant. However, assessment of failure (and the secondary measure of ARI score/mode of failure) was performed by the operators, who were **not blinded** to which system was allocated to which quadrant once treatment began. Since ARI scoring involves a degree of clinician judgment (a categorical/subjective rating of adhesive remnant amount), there is a modest risk of assessment bias here, though bracket failure itself (present vs absent) is a hard, objective outcome with limited room for assessor-driven bias. Overall, given the objective nature of the primary failure endpoint and patient blinding, risk is judged low for this specific outcome (failure rate), though I flag that the ARI/mode-of-failure secondary outcome carries higher risk due to unblinded assessment.

---

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: No trial registration or published protocol is referenced or available for verification, which is a significant limitation for assessing this domain thoroughly—I cannot confirm whether the failure rate outcome, the statistical models used (logistic regression AND Cox proportional hazards, presented in parallel), and the subgroup analyses (gender, age, operator, tooth location, number of manipulations) were all pre-specified or if some were exploratory/post hoc. The paper reports multiple statistical approaches to the same or related questions (logistic regression showing no significant gender effect but Cox regression showing a significant gender effect), and selectively highlights the significant Cox result in the abstract/conclusions while the non-significant logistic regression finding is comparatively downplayed. This pattern of running multiple models and multiple subgroup analyses without a pre-specified analysis plan raises the risk that the reported results were selected, at least in part, based on their statistical significance. The primary outcome (failure rate) itself is reported comprehensively and appears aligned with the stated study aim, which reduces some concern for the primary comparison, but the multiplicity of secondary/subgroup analyses without a visible pre-registered protocol raises overall concern.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **SOME CONCERNS**

**Justification:** No single domain was rated "high risk," but multiple domains (randomization process detail, deviations from intended intervention due to unblinded operators, and selection of the reported result due to absence of a registered protocol and multiplicity of statistical models/subgroups) were rated "some concerns." While the primary comparison of failure rate between the two etching systems is likely reasonably robust given the objective nature of the outcome, patient blinding, and low missing data, the cumulative concerns across randomization detail, intervention fidelity, and analytic transparency are sufficient to preclude a "low risk" overall rating, but do not individually or cumulatively rise to the level of "high risk." I would want to see the original trial protocol (if one exists) and further detail on quadrant-level allocation methodology before revising this judgment.
