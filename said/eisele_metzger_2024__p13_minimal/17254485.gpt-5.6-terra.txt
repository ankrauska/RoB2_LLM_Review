**Endpoint assessed: rate of emergency hemostasis**

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The authors state that participants were allocated using a “random number table” produced through Microsoft Excel. This is, in principle, a potentially random sequence-generation method.  
   - However, the report gives no information on **allocation concealment**: it is not stated who generated the sequence, who enrolled participants, whether assignments were held in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes or a secure central system, or whether clinicians could foresee the next assignment. In an emergency-treatment setting, absence of concealment can readily permit selective assignment.  
   - Baseline data are broadly similar for reported variables, including active esophageal bleeding (23/90 versus 20/82). However, balance in observed characteristics cannot establish that allocation was adequately concealed. There is also an apparent numerical imbalance in grades of esophageal varices (e.g., F3: 42 versus 51), although the authors report a non-significant overall comparison.  
   - Therefore, sequence generation is reported but protection against foreknowledge of allocation is not. This leaves **some concerns**.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**  
   - The intended contrast was clear: immediate combined Histoacryl embolization plus EVL in the therapy group versus Histoacryl embolization with planned EVL two months later in the control group.  
   - The intervention could not realistically be blinded to endoscopists or patients, because the timing and performance of EVL were plainly apparent. However, lack of blinding alone does not necessarily indicate bias from deviations, particularly for a procedural emergency endpoint.  
   - Both groups reportedly received somatostatin before and after the procedure, reducing concern about unequal co-intervention of this kind.  
   - Ten control-group patients with ongoing active esophageal variceal bleeding underwent rescue EVL because bleeding was not controlled. This is a clinically appropriate response to treatment failure. Importantly, these patients appear to have remained classified as failures for the emergency-hemostasis outcome (72/82 were counted as controlled), rather than being reclassified as successes after rescue treatment.  
   - There is no evidence that participants crossed over before outcome assessment, that the assigned procedures were not delivered as planned, or that deviations were unequally caused by knowledge of allocation in a manner likely to bias the reported emergency-hemostasis comparison.  
   - Accordingly, risk is judged **low risk**, while recognising that rescue treatment reflects the inherent clinical consequences of the delayed-EVL strategy rather than a protocol violation.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk**  
   - The emergency-hemostasis result is reported for all randomized participants: 90/90 patients in the therapy group and 72/82 in the control group were included in the stated rates.  
   - No withdrawals, losses to follow-up, exclusions after randomization, deaths before outcome ascertainment, or missing emergency-hemostasis assessments are described.  
   - Because emergency hemostasis appears to have been assessed during the immediate treatment episode, substantial loss of outcome data is unlikely.  
   - Although the reporting is terse and does not explicitly say that every randomized participant had complete outcome assessment, the denominators correspond to all randomized patients.  
   - Therefore, the risk of bias from missing data for this endpoint is **low risk**.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: some concerns**  
   - **Outcome: rate of emergency hemostasis.**  
   - The outcome is clinically important and partly objective: ongoing variceal hemorrhage or cessation of visible bleeding can be assessed endoscopically, and persistent clinically important bleeding would ordinarily be identifiable.  
   - Nevertheless, the manuscript does not provide an adequate operational definition of “emergency hemostasis.” It does not specify the required duration without bleeding, whether hemodynamic stability or hemoglobin change was required, whether endoscopic cessation alone was sufficient, or the precise time point at which hemostasis was adjudicated. The explicit definition supplied in the paper applies only to *early rebleeding*, not emergency hemostasis.  
   - The treating endoscopists and outcome assessors were necessarily aware of treatment assignment. Knowledge of assignment could influence subjective components of the determination that hemorrhage was “controlled,” the decision to proceed to rescue EVL, and documentation of success—especially where bleeding may have been intermittent or where gastric and esophageal bleeding sources coexist.  
   - Since the endpoint is more objective than participant-reported pain or satisfaction, lack of blinding does not by itself make bias inevitable. However, the combination of unblinded clinical assessment and an insufficiently specified endpoint leaves **some concerns**.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - No trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospectively specified primary outcome is provided. The study was conducted from 2000 to 2006, and the article does not identify which outcome was primary.  
   - Emergency hemostasis is described in the Results and is consistent with the clinical rationale presented in the Introduction. Thus, there is no direct evidence that the endpoint was introduced only after results were known.  
   - However, the outcome measurement and analysis were not pre-specified in sufficient detail. In particular, the manuscript does not define emergency hemostasis, its assessment time point, or analytic handling of rescue EVL in advance.  
   - The lack of protocol/registration makes it impossible to exclude selective choice among possible definitions of immediate hemostasis, assessment times, analyses, or reported outcomes.  
   - Therefore, this domain is judged **some concerns**.

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   - The overall risk of bias for the reported effect on the **rate of emergency hemostasis** is **some concerns**.  
   - The principal reasons are absent information about allocation concealment, unblinded assessment using an inadequately defined emergency-hemostasis outcome, and no protocol or registration to verify pre-specification of the reported analysis.  
   - There is no evident missing-data problem, and rescue EVL in failures appears to have been handled in a manner compatible with the assigned-treatment comparison.
