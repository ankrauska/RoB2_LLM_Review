**Endpoint assessed: peer aggression at 12 months** (severe past-year peer aggression, a binary self-reported outcome).

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - Participants were said to be “randomly selected” and then randomly assigned to therapist-assisted brief intervention (TBI), computer brief intervention (CBI), or control, with stratification by sex and age group. Stratification is compatible with a sound randomization process.  
   - However, the manuscript does not state how the random sequence was generated (for example, a computer-generated sequence, random-number table, or permuted blocks). “Randomly assigned” is an assertion, not a description of the method.  
   - Crucially, allocation concealment is not described. It is unclear whether staff enrolling participants could foresee or influence the next allocation before assignment. This is especially important in an ED-based behavioural trial where recruitment and allocation occurred during the same visit.  
   - Baseline prevalence of severe peer aggression differed somewhat across groups (TBI 82.7%, CBI 75.5%, control 77.9%), although the reported adjusted model does not suggest a gross baseline imbalance and the difference could readily be due to chance.  
   - Thus, there is no affirmative evidence that allocation was compromised, but essential details of sequence generation and concealment are absent.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
   - The interventions were delivered immediately during the ED visit: a therapist-assisted computer-facilitated intervention (median 37 minutes), a computer-only intervention (median 29 minutes), or a control brochure. These are necessarily unblinded interventions; participant awareness is inherent in the intervention rather than, by itself, a problematic deviation.  
   - More than 95% of participants reportedly received their assigned intervention. This indicates good adherence to assigned treatment at the point of delivery.  
   - The analysis was described as intention-to-treat: all randomized participants were included irrespective of whether the assigned intervention was received.  
   - No evidence is presented of important contamination, systematic switching between arms, or unequal additional treatment caused by trial participation. Nor is there evidence that the analysis excluded non-adherent participants.  
   - The behavioural effect of receiving counselling—including altered motivation or behaviour—is the intended effect being evaluated, not a bias-producing deviation. On the information available, this domain is at low risk.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - Of 726 randomized participants, 607 (83.6%) completed the 12-month follow-up: approximately 16.4% had no directly observed 12-month outcome data. This is not trivial for a self-reported behavioural outcome.  
   - The authors state that generalized estimating equations used all available longitudinal data and that analyses followed an intention-to-treat approach. However, this does not eliminate bias if missingness at 12 months depended on unobserved peer aggression or differed by randomized group. GEE analysis is valid under assumptions about the missing-data mechanism; those assumptions are not demonstrated here.  
   - The report does not provide, for this endpoint, attrition by randomized group, reasons for non-response by group, or comparisons of baseline aggression and other prognostic characteristics between those followed and those lost.  
   - The authors themselves acknowledge that loss to follow-up might have biased results.  
   - There is no clear evidence that missingness was related to outcome or intervention, but neither is there sufficient evidence that it was not. Hence, some concerns rather than high risk.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — severe peer aggression at 12 months: high-risk**  
   - Peer aggression was measured by participants’ self-report of past-year severe aggression using conflict-tactics items, dichotomized as any versus none. Examples include hitting or punching, serious physical fighting, and weapon use.  
   - The outcome is subjective and highly susceptible to under-reporting because it concerns socially disapproved, potentially incriminating behaviour. Although confidential computer self-administration is a sensible method likely to improve disclosure, it does not ensure unbiased reporting.  
   - Participants could not be blinded to whether they had received a therapist-delivered motivational intervention, a computer intervention, or merely a resource brochure. The trial registry says outcome assessors were masked, but the outcome was self-administered by the participants themselves; assessor masking cannot blind participants to their own treatment assignment.  
   - The TBI explicitly aimed to reduce violence, improve motivation, anger management, conflict resolution, and avoidance of violent situations. Participants who received it may therefore have had stronger expectations to improve and stronger incentives to report less aggression, independently of any true change in aggression.  
   - Because knowledge of intervention assignment could plausibly influence reporting of this subjective, self-reported primary outcome, and because the direction of such bias would favour the intervention, this domain is high risk.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high-risk**  
   - The trial was prospectively registered (NCT00251212), and the registry broadly identifies violent behaviours and alcohol misuse at 12 months as primary outcomes. Thus, peer aggression is within the registered outcome domain.  
   - Nevertheless, the registry does not pre-specify the particular “severe peer aggression” binary definition, the specific four-item construction, the dichotomization rule, the group-by-time GEE model, covariate handling, or the precise comparison that would be emphasized.  
   - The study assessed multiple violence outcomes (peer aggression, peer victimization, violence consequences), multiple alcohol outcomes, three groups, and several follow-up time points. The paper highlights the significant TBI-versus-control results for aggression and victimization, whereas CBI was null and violence consequences were null.  
   - The authors explicitly report that they focused on dichotomous outcomes because effects at earlier follow-up were found for dichotomized rather than continuous variables and because they wished to assess whether earlier effects persisted. This creates a substantial opportunity for selecting the outcome scale/definition and analytic presentation on the basis of earlier observed results.  
   - No dated, detailed statistical analysis plan is provided that would demonstrate that this exact binary severe-aggression result and analysis were specified in advance. In a setting with many related outcomes and analyses, selective reporting is a serious concern.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The result for peer aggression at 12 months is at **high risk of bias**, principally because the outcome was an unblinded participant-reported measure of a subjective and socially sensitive behaviour directly targeted by the intervention, making differential reporting highly plausible. There is also high risk of selective-result reporting because the registered outcome specification was broad and the published analysis selected dichotomous outcomes after earlier findings, amid multiple outcomes, comparisons, and time points. Missing-data and randomization-reporting limitations further reduce confidence in the estimated effect.
