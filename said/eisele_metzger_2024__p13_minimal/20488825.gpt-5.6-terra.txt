**Outcome assessed: attack number (total relapses during 1-year follow-up).**

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - A computer-based blocked randomization method (“four blocks”) was reported, which is in principle an acceptable random sequence-generation method.  
   - The trial was described as double-blind; the registry specifies masking of participants, care providers, investigators, and outcome assessors. Tablets were reportedly supplied in similar packages. These features reduce the likelihood that knowledge of allocation distorted enrolment or treatment assignment.  
   - However, allocation concealment is not described adequately. “Separate physicians” allocated participants, but the report does not say whether they used a secure central randomization system, sequentially numbered opaque sealed containers, or another method preventing prediction or manipulation of assignments. Small fixed blocks, particularly without stratification, can become predictable if block size is known.  
   - Baseline variables appear broadly balanced, although the placebo group had a somewhat higher mean baseline EDSS (1.6 versus 1.2). This is compatible with chance, but does not remedy the lack of a clear allocation-concealment description.  
   - There are also inconsistent participant-accounting data: the text says 85 were randomized (42 placebo, 43 simvastatin), whereas the flowchart indicates that only 38 placebo and 42 simvastatin participants received allocated treatment. These discrepancies make the conduct of randomization and post-randomization exclusions less transparent.  

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: high risk**  
   - The intended comparison was simvastatin 40 mg/day plus interferon beta-1a versus placebo plus interferon beta-1a for 12 months. Blinding and use of apparently similar medication packaging would, if maintained, limit deviations due to awareness of allocation.  
   - However, the protocol and manuscript specified withdrawal after **two relapses during the study**, and six participants were withdrawn for this reason (four placebo and two simvastatin). Relapse is the primary outcome. Thus, an outcome-related event caused treatment discontinuation and removal from the trial regimen. This is a serious design and analysis problem for a relapse-count outcome.  
   - Five participants withdrew within the first two months because they “refused to cooperate,” and other participants discontinued for personal reasons or chronic exacerbation.  
   - Despite claiming intention-to-treat analysis and follow-up of withdrawals for the whole study period, the report then states that only dropout cases who had used study medication for at least six months (nine participants) were included in the final analysis, because simvastatin was assumed to require six months to act. This is incompatible with a genuine intention-to-treat analysis. It excludes early discontinuers after randomization on a post-randomization criterion.  
   - The primary analysis appears to include 40 simvastatin and 36 placebo participants, rather than all 43 and 42 randomized participants. Per-protocol and LOCF analyses were also undertaken. These analyses do not appropriately estimate the effect of assignment to intervention when discontinuation, particularly discontinuation triggered by relapses, has occurred.  
   - Since discontinuation and exclusion were related at least in part to disease activity and relapse, and since handling of these deviations was inappropriate and inconsistent, the result for attack number is at high risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions.  

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
   - Eighty-five participants were randomized, but the reported primary attack-number analysis included only 76 participants (simvastatin n=40; placebo n=36), implying outcome data were unavailable or excluded for nine randomized participants. The figure instead reports 42 and 38 analysed, creating an unresolved discrepancy.  
   - Fourteen participants withdrew overall. Five withdrew in the first two months without a stated clinical reason; six withdrew after two relapses; one placebo participant had chronic exacerbation; and two withdrew for personal reasons.  
   - Missing or excluded primary-outcome data are therefore not trivial: approximately 11% (9/85) appear absent from the principal attack-number analysis, and the reporting does not clearly establish attack counts through 12 months for every randomized participant.  
   - Missingness is plausibly related to the outcome. In particular, participants with two relapses were required to leave the study, and chronic exacerbation is likely related to relapse activity. Furthermore, exclusions differed numerically between groups: the analysed sample was reduced by 3/43 in the simvastatin arm and by 6/42 in the placebo arm.  
   - The authors’ statement that withdrawals were followed for the full period is not reconciled with their subsequent exclusion of those who had received treatment for less than six months. No complete participant-level accounting, no clearly defined imputation strategy for the primary endpoint, and no credible sensitivity analysis addressing informative missingness are provided.  
   - Because the missing/excluded data may depend on relapse occurrence and could plausibly alter the estimated group difference, this domain is high risk.  

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (attack number): low risk**  
   - Relapse was operationally defined as a new or worsening neurological deficit, in the absence of fever, lasting more than 24 hours after one month of stability. This is a reasonably prespecified clinical definition.  
   - Follow-up neurological assessments occurred every three months, and four neurologists responsible for eligibility assessment and follow-up examinations were reported to be blinded to treatment allocation. The trial registry also identifies outcome assessors as masked.  
   - Participants were reportedly unaware of allocation, which should reduce differential symptom reporting and differential presentation for assessment. This matters because relapse recognition can involve subjective symptoms and clinical judgement.  
   - There is no indication that the methods for detecting or adjudicating relapses differed by treatment group.  
   - Although the report does not describe central relapse adjudication or procedures for assessing attacks occurring between scheduled visits, the stated participant and assessor blinding, together with an explicit relapse definition, make substantial differential outcome-measurement bias unlikely.  

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The registry identifies **attack number at one year** as the sole primary outcome, and the manuscript reports total relapses over the one-year follow-up. Thus, the reported primary outcome corresponds to the registered primary endpoint.  
   - Nevertheless, the registry contains no prespecified statistical analysis plan, no definition of the intended analysis population, no specification of the statistical model for attack counts, and no planned approach for withdrawals or missing outcome data.  
   - The manuscript reports multiple analyses for the attack outcome: a Mann–Whitney comparison of total attacks in a reduced sample; a per-protocol analysis; and an LOCF-based analysis of attacks per three-month period. The reported primary result is statistically significant, but the basis for choosing this particular analysis as primary is not adequately established.  
   - The text describes an intention-to-treat approach but applies a six-month treatment-exposure condition to dropout inclusion. Such post hoc or inadequately prespecified analytic flexibility is especially problematic when the primary analysis excludes randomized participants and when withdrawals are related to relapse.  
   - There are internal inconsistencies in reported analysed denominators and in the presentation of adjusted estimates, further limiting confidence that the reported estimate was selected from a prespecified analysis.  
   - Therefore, while there is no direct evidence that the endpoint itself was switched, there are some concerns about selective selection of the analysis and reported result.  

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall judgement is **high risk of bias**. The principal reasons are high risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions and due to missing outcome data. Participants were withdrawn after recurrent relapses—events constituting the primary outcome—and early withdrawals were excluded from the purported intention-to-treat analysis using a post-randomization six-month exposure rule. The unequal and outcome-related exclusions, together with inconsistent accounting of analysed participants, could materially exaggerate or otherwise distort the apparent reduction in attack number with simvastatin.
