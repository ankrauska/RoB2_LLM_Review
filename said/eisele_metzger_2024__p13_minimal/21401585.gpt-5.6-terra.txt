**Endpoint assessed:** Quality of movement of the impaired upper limb, measured by the Melbourne Assessment of Unilateral Upper Limb Function (MUUL). The relevant principal contrast is CIMT versus bimanual training (BIM), particularly at 26 weeks (reported estimated mean difference approximately 4.4 percentage points favouring CIMT).

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
- Children were matched before randomization on important prognostic characteristics: age, sex, side of hemiplegia, and baseline MUUL function.  
- Allocation within matched pairs was generated using a **computer-generated list of random numbers**, which is an appropriate random sequence-generation method.  
- Allocation concealment was explicitly described: assignments were held in **concealed envelopes opened by non-study personnel**. This should have prevented investigators recruiting, matching, or assessing children from predicting the forthcoming allocation.  
- Baseline MUUL scores and other reported baseline characteristics were reasonably similar between groups (MUUL means: 67.1 in CIMT and 70.8 in BIM), and the regression model included matching variables as covariates. The modest numerical difference in baseline MUUL does not, by itself, indicate failure of randomization.  
- There is a minor reporting inconsistency: the abstract refers to 63 children, whereas the Results section reports 64 randomized, with 32 allocated to each group. This is plausibly explained by one BIM participant dropping out early, leaving 63 in the baseline table. It does not undermine the described randomization procedure.  

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
- Blinding of children and camp therapists was not feasible: CIMT required a glove/constraint on the unaffected limb, whereas BIM explicitly practised two-handed activities. Thus, participants and personnel necessarily knew the assigned intervention.  
- However, the differences in treatment content were the intended experimental contrast, rather than inadvertent deviations. Both groups received a planned 60-hour, 10-day intensive day-camp programme, delivered in comparable settings, by a core therapy team, with similar therapist-to-child ratios.  
- Intervention adherence was high. CIMT compliance was reported as 100%; BIM compliance was 97%. Most children received either 60 or 54 hours, with reduced exposure largely attributable to illness or public holidays. One BIM child received 42 hours because of illness. These deviations appear limited and do not suggest differential departure from the assigned treatment likely to materially bias the MUUL comparison.  
- The analysis was stated to be according to the intention-to-treat principle.  
- Concurrent therapy was reportedly unchanged for nearly all children. One CIMT child received upper-limb botulinum toxin A between the 3- and 26-week assessments. This is a potential post-randomization co-intervention favouring CIMT at 26 weeks, but it affected only one participant and there is no indication that this participant drove the result.  
- Camps were delivered consecutively, with BIM camps occurring before CIMT camps. This creates some potential for time/order effects or progressive staff learning. Nevertheless, there is no evidence that such effects caused important departures from the intended interventions, and the intervention contrast itself was delivered as planned.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
- Of 64 randomized children, 62 received their allocated intervention. One BIM participant dropped out on day 2 because of pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties; the manuscript implies another participant may not have received the allocated intervention, but reporting is not fully clear.  
- The provided trial-flow description indicates some non-attendance at follow-up, but the exact numbers with observed MUUL data at each time point, separately by group, and the reasons for every missing MUUL assessment are not reported clearly in the manuscript text provided.  
- The authors state that analyses were intention-to-treat, but they do not explain how missing outcome measurements were handled. In particular, it is not clear whether generalized estimating equations were fitted using all available observations under an assumed missing-at-random mechanism, whether any imputation was used, or whether children with missing follow-up data were excluded from particular analyses.  
- Attrition appears small, retention is described as high, and there is no affirmative evidence that missing MUUL data were related to the true, unobserved MUUL outcomes differently between CIMT and BIM. Therefore, a high-risk judgement is not warranted.  
- Nonetheless, the lack of transparent outcome-specific denominators and missing-data handling prevents confidence that missingness could not have biased the 26-week MUUL result.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (MUUL): low-risk**  
- MUUL is an assessment of observed upper-limb movement quality. Although the intervention is visible to children and treating therapists, the main vulnerability would be bias in outcome scoring.  
- The clinicians conducting the assessment sessions were aware of treatment allocation. This could in principle influence participant encouragement, test administration, or other aspects of the assessment.  
- Importantly, the primary MUUL assessments were videotaped and scored in random order by trained occupational therapists **masked to group allocation**. Blinded scoring materially reduces the principal risk of differential outcome assessment.  
- The manuscript also reports strong intra-rater reliability for the independent MUUL scorer (intraclass correlation coefficient 0.93; 95% CI 0.84–0.97). Reliability does not itself prove absence of bias, but it supports the consistency of scoring.  
- There is no evidence that videos revealed the allocated intervention—for example, the CIMT glove was an intervention-time device and MUUL testing was not described as being conducted while constrained. Nor is there evidence that assessors were unblinded.  
- Consequently, despite unblinded assessors administering the test, the blinded, randomized-order video scoring means that bias in measurement of the MUUL outcome is unlikely.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: low-risk**  
- The report identifies MUUL as a primary outcome, defines it clearly, and reports the relevant between-group results at both prespecified follow-up occasions (3 and 26 weeks), rather than selectively reporting only the favourable 26-week result.  
- The study cites a published trial protocol (the INCITE protocol in *BMC Neurology*, 2010). The manuscript’s stated hypotheses, primary outcomes, assessment schedule, and analysis broadly correspond to the reported design: MUUL and AHA measured at baseline, 3 weeks, and 26 weeks.  
- The statistical approach is described: a generalized estimating-equation model using repeated baseline, 3-week, and 26-week measurements, including treatment-by-time interactions and adjustment for matching characteristics. The reported MUUL treatment effect corresponds to this approach.  
- Both non-significant and significant MUUL contrasts are presented: no statistically significant between-group difference at 3 weeks and a difference favouring CIMT at 26 weeks. This reduces concern about selective time-point reporting.  
- There is a small numerical inconsistency between Table II (treatment-by-time coefficient 4.5, 95% CI 2.2–6.7) and Table III/text (4.4, 95% CI 2.2–6.7). This appears compatible with rounding or a minor reporting error, not selective reporting.  
- No evidence is provided of undisclosed alternative MUUL analyses or selective choice among multiple outcome definitions.  

**6. Overall rating: some concerns**  
The randomization and allocation concealment procedures were adequate; intervention delivery and adherence were generally strong; and MUUL scoring was appropriately blinded through masked video ratings. The principal limitation is incomplete reporting of outcome-specific follow-up completeness and of the method used to handle missing MUUL data. This creates **some concerns**, but not high risk, because apparent attrition was limited and there is no evidence that missingness was differential or outcome-related.
