1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The investigators state that allocation used a computer-generated random sequence, with minimisation/stratification for age, sex, and family history of coronary heart disease, using the Pocock–Simon method. This is an acceptable method for generating an unpredictable allocation sequence.  
   - Baseline characteristics were broadly balanced between groups, which is compatible with successful randomization.  
   - However, the report does not describe adequate *allocation concealment*. One investigator (HJ) generated both the random allocation sequence and the study-arm assignment. There is no statement that allocation was implemented through a secure central system, opaque sequentially numbered envelopes, or another process preventing foreknowledge of the forthcoming allocation.  
   - Randomization occurred after the first study visit, which is reasonable, but the report does not establish that personnel enrolling or scheduling participants could not influence assignment.  
   - Therefore, the sequence generation itself is satisfactory, but insufficient information about concealment leaves **some concerns**.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**  
   - This was explicitly an unmasked trial. Blinding of participants, genetic counsellors, and physicians was not feasible because the intervention was disclosure of genetic-risk information rather than conventional-risk information alone.  
   - Lack of blinding is intrinsic to the intervention and does not itself demonstrate a problematic deviation. The relevant causal effect is plausibly the effect of being allocated to, and receiving, genetic-risk disclosure in usual clinical interaction.  
   - Both groups received structured disclosure, a genetic-counsellor consultation, a physician consultation, and use of a decision aid. Thus, the principal difference between arms was the inclusion of the genetic risk score in risk communication.  
   - All 207 randomized participants reportedly completed the second visit containing the intervention encounters. There is no evidence that participants crossed over, did not receive their assigned disclosure, or that substantial additional interventions differed systematically between groups.  
   - Analyses appear to compare the randomized groups, and there is no indication of an inappropriate per-protocol exclusion after randomization for the shared decision-making outcome.  
   - Accordingly, there is **low risk** of bias from deviations from the intended interventions.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk**  
   - The shared decision-making survey was completed by 206 of 207 randomized participants: outcome data were available for approximately 99.5% of the randomized population.  
   - The single missing survey appears to have occurred in the CRS+GRS group (tables report 103 respondents in each group, despite 104 participants allocated to CRS+GRS).  
   - The amount of missing outcome data is trivial and is very unlikely to alter the estimated between-group difference materially. There is no indication that the missingness was related to the unobserved shared decision-making score.  
   - The authors’ handling of item non-response is not ideal: unanswered individual SDM-Q items were assigned a score of zero, while participants with no completed questions were excluded. This could bias scores if item non-response were differential or outcome-related. However, no material item-level missingness is reported, and the effective outcome-data completeness is exceptionally high.  
   - Therefore, for the shared decision-making survey score, the risk due to missing outcome data is **low risk**.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: high risk**

   **a. Participant-reported SDM-Q shared decision-making score: high risk**  
   - The principal reported SDM score was derived from the 11-item SDM-Q, a patient-reported assessment of the participant’s perception of shared decision-making during the consultation. This is a subjective outcome.  
   - Participants necessarily knew whether genetic-risk information had been disclosed. The trial registry confirms no masking.  
   - Knowledge of receiving genetic information could influence participants’ satisfaction, expectations, perceived novelty or value of the encounter, and willingness to rate the consultation favourably, independently of the actual extent of shared decision-making. This is particularly relevant because the intervention involved a conspicuous, potentially salient genomic-risk disclosure.  
   - The outcome assessment was apparently undertaken after the consultation and may therefore have been vulnerable to response or social-desirability bias, especially in a setting involving a genetic counsellor and physician.  
   - Although the SDM-Q is described as validated, use of a validated instrument does not remove bias arising from unblinded participants reporting a subjective outcome.  
   - Additionally, the investigators dichotomised each original four-point Likert response into agreement versus disagreement and assigned a score of zero to missing items. This nonstandard simplification may reduce measurement precision and can exacerbate ceiling effects. The observed scores were indeed near the maximum possible score (means 10.40 and 10.61 out of 11).  
   - Thus, measurement of the participant-reported SDM-Q score is at **high risk of bias**.

   **b. OPTION5 video-based shared decision-making score: high risk**  
   - The manuscript also reports OPTION5 scores from a random subset of 80 recorded encounters. OPTION5 is an observer-rated measure and is less directly susceptible than participant self-report to patient expectation effects.  
   - However, recordings were analysed by one author, and the report does not state that this assessor was blinded to treatment allocation. In practice, the intervention—discussion of a genetic risk score—would likely be identifiable from the video.  
   - The scorer therefore could have known, or readily inferred, whether an encounter was in the CRS or CRS+GRS arm. OPTION5 includes judgement-based assessment of clinician behaviours and is not a wholly objective measurement.  
   - No duplicate independent ratings, inter-rater reliability, blinded adjudication, or prespecified method for handling assessor disagreement is reported.  
   - Although the 80 encounters were described as a random sample, the absence of blinded rating for a judgement-based outcome makes detection bias plausible.  
   - Accordingly, measurement of the OPTION5 score is also at **high risk of bias**.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The trial was prospectively registered (NCT01936675), and the registration identifies “shared decision making (as assessed by survey)” as a secondary outcome. This is reassuring insofar as shared decision-making was not evidently introduced only after inspection of the results.  
   - However, the registry information supplied does not specify the SDM-Q instrument, the scoring transformation, timing of assessment, handling of missing questionnaire items, the particular statistical analysis, or whether the OPTION5 analysis was prespecified.  
   - The manuscript reports several potential representations of shared decision-making: individual SDM-Q items, the total SDM-Q score, the proportion reaching a perfect score of 11, and OPTION5 findings in a subsample. The availability of multiple outcome definitions and analyses creates scope for selective choice of results.  
   - In particular, converting the SDM-Q’s four-category Likert responses into binary scores and imputing zero for missing individual answers are analytic decisions not clearly prespecified in the registration information.  
   - The authors report results relatively comprehensively and do not appear to suppress an obviously unfavourable reported analysis. Nevertheless, without the full dated protocol and statistical analysis plan specifying these measures and analyses, selective-reporting bias cannot be excluded.  
   - Therefore, this domain has **some concerns**.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The overall rating is **high risk of bias** because the principal endpoint—participant-reported shared decision-making—is subjective and was measured in participants who were necessarily aware of the intervention received. This creates a serious risk that reported scores reflect expectations or perceptions related to receiving genetic information rather than genuine differences in shared decision-making.  
   - The supporting OPTION5 assessment has the same broad concern because the single video assessor was not reported to be blinded and could likely infer allocation from the content of the encounter.  
   - Additional concerns about allocation concealment and incomplete prespecification of outcome scoring and analyses further reduce confidence in the reported null comparison.
