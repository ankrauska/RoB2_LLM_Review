**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process**: low-risk
The randomization process utilized a computer-generated sequence with a 1:1 allocation ratio, and allocation was concealed using sealed envelopes. Although patients could not be blinded to the assigned MR scanner due to the nature of the interventions, there was no indication of baseline imbalances or subverted allocation that would indicate problems with the randomization process itself. The baseline characteristics of the groups were similar.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions**: low-risk
There were some deviations from the intended interventions as a subset of patients who experienced claustrophobia were cross-referred to the alternative scanner. However, the study analyzed these cross-referred patients in the primary analysis, and none of the patients was scanned on both scanners. Subgroup analyses excluding cross-referred patients confirmed that these deviations did not impact the primary findings. Furthermore, given the pragmatic diagnostic nature of this study, such deviations do not introduce significant bias into the assessment of image quality. Blinding of patients and personnel was impossible due to the differences in scanner designs, but it is unlikely that this affected the objective image quality acquisition parameters. 

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data**: low-risk
Of the 106 patients with a clinical indication for spinal MR imaging, 93 underwent MR imaging and were included in the analysis. One patient aborted imaging on the first scanner after one sequence and was excluded from the primary analysis for image quality. The amount of missing outcome data is small and unlikely to introduce significant bias, especially since reasons for missing data (claustrophobia leading to non-acquisition of images) were documented and balanced/expected within the context of the larger parent trial. 

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome**: low-risk
The image quality outcomes were evaluated both qualitatively and quantitatively. For qualitative parameters, two examiners graded the images in consensus. Crucially, the examiners were blinded to the imaging technique (scanner type) and patient identity at the time of analysis. While complete blinding to the scanner type might be challenging for experienced radiologists if they can recognize scanner-specific image artifacts, the use of objective quantitative measures (e.g., SNR, CNR, contour sharpness computed via software) provides robust data that mitigates subjectivity. The measurement methods were standardized and applied consistently across both groups.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result**: low-risk
The reported endpoints are in alignment with the methods section and standard radiological practices for assessing image quality. Multiple parameters (qualitative scoring, SNR, CNR, contour sharpness) were consistently reported for all acquired sequences across the studied anatomical regions (cervicothoracic and thoracolumbar spine). There is no evidence that certain results were selectively omitted or highlighted based on their statistical significance. 

**6. Overall rating**: low risk
Based on the assessment, all domains were judged as low risk. The randomization was adequate, deviations and missing data were handled appropriately and had little impact on the image quality assessment, the outcome assessors were blinded (with objective quantitative metrics supporting subjective ones), and the reported results match the prespecified methodologies. Confidence in the reported outcome is high.
