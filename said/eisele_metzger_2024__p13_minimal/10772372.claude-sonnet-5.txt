# Risk of Bias Assessment: Pharmacy Counseling on Compliance and Lipid-Lowering Therapy Trial

**Endpoint assessed: Compliance**

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: Some concerns**
Justification: The authors state patients were "randomized to telephone contact or no telephone contact using a computer-generated list of random numbers," which supports a genuinely random sequence generation. However, there is no mention of allocation concealment mechanisms (e.g., sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes; central randomization) to prevent foreknowledge of assignment by the enrolling pharmacist/investigator. Given the small sample (n=30) and lack of concealment detail, selection bias at enrollment cannot be excluded. Baseline characteristics are reported as "similar," which is reassuring but does not substitute for a description of concealment procedures.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: Some concerns**
Justification: This is an inherently unblinded behavioral intervention (weekly telephone calls vs. no calls), and neither patients nor the intervening pharmacist could be blinded to group assignment. The authors themselves acknowledge a probable Hawthorne effect in the control group ("those patients were acutely aware that their behavior was being assessed"), indicating that the awareness of being monitored likely altered behavior in ways not attributable to the intervention itself. There is no ITT statement, and no discussion of protocol adherence, co-interventions, or contamination between groups. This introduces a systematic risk of bias in interpreting the true causal effect of the "telephone contact" intervention itself versus the general effect of being observed.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: Low risk**
Justification: The manuscript explicitly states "none of whom was lost to follow-up" over the full 2-year period, and complete compliance data appear to have been collected for all 30 patients at all time points (6, 12 weeks, 1 year, 2 years). No missing data are reported for the compliance endpoint.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (Compliance): Some concerns**
Justification: Compliance was assessed using pill/packet counts (short-term) and pharmacy refill records (long-term). These are relatively objective measures compared with patient self-report, and pill counts were "not performed within the patient's view," reducing some risk of reactive over-reporting at the moment of assessment. However:
- Patients were not blinded to their group assignment, and behavior-based outcomes (filling prescriptions, returning pills) are susceptible to influence by the intervention itself (i.e., patients receiving calls know they will be asked about compliance, which may drive the very behavior being measured — a Hawthorne-type outcome-ascertainment bias rather than a true treatment effect).
- Refill records do not confirm actual ingestion of medication, only pharmacy transactions, which is an imperfect proxy for true compliance and could be differentially distorted by group awareness.
- No blinded outcome assessor is described for tabulating pill counts or interpreting refill data.
Given these issues, and the fact that compliance is a quasi-subjective/behavioral outcome heavily influenced by patient awareness of being monitored, there is meaningful risk of bias in measurement, though mitigated somewhat by the use of pharmacy records rather than pure self-report.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: Some concerns**
Justification: No trial registration, published protocol, or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is referenced anywhere in the manuscript, making it impossible to verify whether the reported compliance outcomes (6-week, 12-week, 1-year, 2-year) and subgroup breakdowns (by drug, by insurance status, by sex) were pre-specified or emerged post hoc from multiple exploratory analyses. The manuscript reports numerous secondary/subgroup analyses (insurance coverage stratification, sex-based comparisons, drug-specific compliance patterns) that appear exploratory and are not corrected for multiplicity, raising concern that the significant long-term compliance result may partly reflect selective emphasis on a positive finding after an initially "surprising" null result at 12 weeks. The explicit narrative structure — negative primary short-term finding reframed as building toward a positive long-term result — raises further concern about selective reporting/interpretation.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** While there is no missing outcome data (low risk), there are "some concerns" in four of the five domains: randomization (lack of concealment details), deviations from intended intervention (unblindable behavioral intervention with acknowledged Hawthorne effect), outcome measurement (behavior-based compliance outcome vulnerable to influence by awareness of monitoring), and selection of the reported result (no protocol/registration, multiple exploratory subgroup analyses, narrative shift from null to positive framing). The convergence of concerns across multiple domains — particularly the fundamental unblindability of the intervention combined with a behavioral/self-influenced outcome and the absence of a pre-specified analysis plan — substantially lowers confidence in the reported compliance findings. This combination elevates the overall risk of bias rating from "some concerns" to **high risk**.
