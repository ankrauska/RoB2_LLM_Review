# Risk of Bias Assessment: Clinical Pregnancy Rate

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns.** Randomization was performed using computer-generated random numbers, which is an adequate method for sequence generation. However, allocation concealment is questionable: the manuscript states women were "allocated randomly... with sealed envelopes by specialist nurse" — this suggests envelope-based allocation, which is prone to subversion (e.g., envelopes could be opened prematurely or not adequately sealed/opaque, and there is no explicit description of envelope opacity, sequential numbering, or safeguards against tampering). Furthermore, this trial used three distinct drug formulations (Gonal-F, Metrodin-HP, Pergonal) with different vial types/packaging, and there is no mention of blinding of participants, treating physicians, or outcome assessors. Given that gonadotrophin dosing was individually titrated based on clinical response, unblinded clinicians could have influenced monitoring or stimulation decisions differentially by group. Baseline characteristics (Table I) were balanced, which is reassuring, but the concealment mechanism itself is not described with sufficient rigor to rule out risk.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns.** This was an open-label trial (no blinding of participants or care providers is mentioned anywhere in the manuscript). Given that dose adjustments were individualized based on ultrasound/E2 monitoring, and different formulations were used, there is a risk that clinicians' knowledge of group assignment could have influenced decisions on dose titration, timing of hCG trigger, or cycle cancellation criteria, potentially in a manner correlated with expected outcomes (rFSH being newer and marketed as superior). No mention is made of standardized, blinded protocols for these decisions. There is no clear ITT analysis described — the flow diagram indicates "Analysed" equals "Allocated" for each arm, suggesting no cycle cancellations were excluded from analysis, which is reassuring, but the absence of blinding for dose-adjustment decisions remains a concern for deviation bias.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Low risk.** Based on the CONSORT flow diagram, 81 patients were randomized and analysed in the rFSH group, and 80/80 in the other two groups, with no missing outcome data reported for the primary endpoint of clinical pregnancy. All randomized patients appear to have been followed up and included in the analysis (n=241 randomized, n=241 analysed based on Table II denominators). No dropouts or losses to follow-up are described that would affect the pregnancy rate outcome.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — Clinical Pregnancy Rate
**Some concerns.** Clinical pregnancy was defined objectively (transvaginal ultrasound confirmation 6 weeks after IUI, following serum βhCG measurement 14 days post-hCG), which is a relatively objective outcome measure not directly dependent on subjective patient report. However, the outcome assessors (those performing the confirmatory ultrasound) are not stated to be blinded to group allocation. Since the trial was open-label (no blinding described for participants, clinicians, or outcome assessors), there remains a possibility — albeit lower than for subjective patient-reported outcomes — that knowledge of allocation could subtly influence the diagnostic process (e.g., timing or intensity of follow-up scanning) in favor of the "newer, presumably superior" rFSH group. Given the objective nature of the outcome (ultrasound-confirmed pregnancy), the risk is not high, but the absence of assessor blinding prevents a "low risk" rating.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Some concerns.** No trial registration number or published protocol is cited or available for comparison, making it impossible to verify whether the reported outcome (clinical pregnancy rate) and analyses were pre-specified without modification. The primary outcome (clinical pregnancy rate) is clearly stated in the Methods and consistently reported in Results, which is reassuring. However, several secondary outcomes (duration of stimulation, total gonadotrophin dose, follicle number, multiple pregnancy, miscarriage, OHSS) are also reported, and there is no way to confirm whether additional outcomes or analyses were conducted but not reported, or whether the statistical test choice was pre-specified. The study is likely underpowered (as acknowledged by the authors themselves: "the low power value and the P value of clinical pregnancy rate close to significance are all the limitations of our study" and P=0.04–0.046, close to the significance threshold), raising a possibility that the analysis or reporting could have been influenced by the observed data (e.g., choice of statistical test — Yates-corrected chi-square vs. Fisher's exact — is not clearly matched to each specific comparison). Without a registered protocol, this cannot be fully excluded.

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** While randomization sequence generation was adequate and missing outcome data were minimal, the trial suffers from multiple compounding concerns: unclear/inadequate allocation concealment (envelope-based, no blinding of participants or care providers), absence of blinding of outcome assessors for an outcome that—despite being relatively objective (ultrasound-confirmed pregnancy)—could still be subject to assessment bias in an open-label trial, and no accessible trial registration or protocol to verify absence of selective outcome reporting. The combination of "some concerns" across the randomization process, deviations from intended intervention, outcome measurement, and selective reporting domains — particularly in a trial where the primary result (P=0.04–0.046) is marginally significant and could be sensitive to any of these biases — substantially lowers confidence in the reported effect estimate for clinical pregnancy rate. This constellation of concerns meets the threshold for an overall "high risk" of bias judgment rather than merely "some concerns."
