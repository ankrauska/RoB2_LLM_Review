# Risk of Bias Assessment: Peer Aggression at 12 Months
## SafERteens Trial (Cunningham et al., Pediatrics 2012)

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process:** Low risk (Randomization was computerized, stratified by gender and age group (14-15, 16-18), and occurred during the ED visit after baseline assessment was completed — a method that plausibly prevents foreknowledge of allocation and supports adequate concealment. Baseline comparability across the three arms was checked and confirmed for age, race, and gender; the one imbalance detected — dropping out of school — was adjusted for in sensitivity analyses without changing results. No evidence of subversion of the randomization schedule or baseline imbalance affecting the aggression outcome specifically.)

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions:** Low risk (This was an open-label behavioral trial in which participants and interventionists were necessarily aware of assignment (the control group received a brochure, while active arms received a BI), which is essentially unavoidable given the nature of the intervention. Critically, >95% of participants received their assigned intervention, an intention-to-treat analysis was used for all randomized participants, and there is no indication of relevant co-interventions or contamination between arms that would differentially affect the peer-aggression outcome.)

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data:** Some concerns (The overall 12-month follow-up rate was 83.6% (607/726), meaning ~16% of randomized participants have missing outcome data for peer aggression. The manuscript does not report attrition broken down by treatment arm, nor reasons for loss to follow-up by arm, making it impossible to confirm that missingness was unrelated to the outcome or balanced across groups. Although GEE was used and is robust to data missing at random, the assumption of MAR is not empirically supported by the reporting, and no sensitivity analyses (e.g., multiple imputation, pattern-mixture) were presented to test the robustness of the peer aggression finding to differential/informative dropout.)

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (peer aggression):** High risk (Peer aggression was measured exclusively via self-report using items from the Conflict Tactics Scale, a subjective participant-reported outcome. Participants were not blinded to their treatment assignment — those in the TBI condition had a clear, memorable, motivationally-charged interaction with a therapist, versus a brief brochure in the control condition. This creates a strong potential for differential reporting/social desirability bias, wherein TBI participants may underreport aggressive behavior at follow-up due to awareness of the intervention's goals and recall of the motivational conversation, rather than a true behavioral change. Although the registration record lists the "outcomes assessor" as masked, this is not meaningful here because the outcome is entirely self-administered by the (unblinded) participant on a computer survey — there is no independent, blinded assessor verifying the aggression outcome. The authors' own discussion acknowledges reliance on self-report as a limitation, citing measures taken to improve validity [e.g., computer self-administration, confidentiality] — but these measures address general disclosure honesty, not the differential expectancy/social-desirability bias introduced by knowledge of assignment to a therapist-delivered intervention. This is a serious concern for a subjective self-reported behavioral outcome and is judged high risk.)

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result:** Low risk (The trial was prospectively registered (NCT00251212) with violent behaviors and alcohol misuse listed as primary outcomes at 12 months, consistent with what is reported here. Peer aggression, peer victimization, and violence consequences were pre-specified analogous constructs consistent with prior 3- and 6-month publications from the same trial (Cunningham 2009; Walton 2010), indicating a consistent a priori set of outcome measures across time points rather than post hoc selection. The analysis plan (dichotomous outcomes, GEE, group-by-time interaction) is stated to follow directly from the 6-month analysis approach. No evidence of selective outcome switching, and null results for alcohol variables are reported transparently alongside positive violence findings, reducing concern about selective reporting for this specific outcome.)

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** While randomization and intervention fidelity domains are low risk, and outcome selection also appears low risk, there is a serious concern regarding measurement of this specific outcome (peer aggression) — an unblinded, subjective, self-reported behavioral outcome in a trial where blinding of participants was not possible, creating substantial potential for reporting bias in favor of the more intensive/salient therapist-delivered intervention. This is compounded by incompletely characterized missing outcome data (16% overall attrition, not reported by arm). Per the algorithm, a single domain judged "high risk" (measurement) is sufficient to rate the overall result as high risk of bias, and this is reinforced by the additional "some concerns" in the missing-data domain.
