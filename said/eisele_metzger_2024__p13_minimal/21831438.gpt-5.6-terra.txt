**Outcome assessed:** Change in mean sitting diastolic blood pressure (DBP) from baseline to 4 weeks of therapy, comparing candesartan 16 mg/hydrochlorothiazide (HCT) 12.5 mg with candesartan 16 mg monotherapy.

**Effect reported:** In the ITT analysis, mean DBP change at week 4 was −17.8 mm Hg (SD 10.2; n=111) in the candesartan/HCT group and −14.1 mm Hg (SD 10.1; n=113) in the candesartan-only group; reported between-group P=0.0028. The registry reports adjusted least-squares means of −17.0 and −14.1 mm Hg, respectively.

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The study is described as randomized, with 1:1 allocation using “mixed block randomization (4 and 6 blocks).” Use of varying block sizes is compatible with a genuinely random sequence and is preferable to a single fixed, small block size.  
   - However, the report does not state who generated the allocation sequence, who held it, how allocation was implemented, or whether allocation was concealed from recruiting investigators before enrolment. There is no mention of central randomization, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or an equivalent concealment mechanism.  
   - This was explicitly an open-label trial. Open-label status is not itself evidence of a defective randomization sequence, but, without allocation concealment, investigators could potentially have foreseen or influenced assignments—particularly in a multicentre industry-sponsored trial.  
   - Baseline DBP and SBP were closely balanced, but age differed statistically between groups (mean 51.0 versus 47.8 years; P=0.02). This isolated imbalance does not demonstrate manipulation or failure of randomization, but it provides no reassurance sufficient to compensate for the absent concealment details.  
   - Therefore, the random sequence appears plausibly random, but concealment of allocation is inadequately reported.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**  
   - The intervention comparison at the relevant 4-week endpoint appears to have been implemented as intended: candesartan 16 mg/HCT 12.5 mg versus candesartan 16 mg monotherapy. The later forced titration to candesartan 32 mg occurred only after the week-4 efficacy assessment and therefore cannot affect the specified primary endpoint.  
   - The trial was open label, so participants and treating staff knew treatment assignment. Knowledge of assignment could in principle influence adherence or concomitant care. However, the primary outcome is an instrument-derived blood-pressure measurement, and no differential cointerventions, rescue antihypertensive treatments, treatment switching before week 4, or systematic protocol deviations relevant to the primary outcome are reported.  
   - Compliance was assessed by interview and pill counts, with high median reported compliance in both groups (98.6% combination and 97.8% monotherapy). These measures are imperfect, and open-label reporting may exaggerate adherence, but there is no evidence of important differential non-adherence.  
   - The efficacy analysis was described as ITT, although its operational definition required baseline data, at least one post-randomization BP measurement, and receipt of at least one dose. This is not a strict all-randomized ITT analysis, but the resulting exclusions are considered principally under missing outcome data.  
   - On the available evidence, there is no indication that deviations from assigned treatment materially biased the 4-week DBP comparison.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - Of 233 randomized patients (117 combination; 116 monotherapy), the reported week-4 ITT analysis includes 224 patients (111 and 113, respectively). Thus, the primary-outcome analysis excludes 9 randomized participants: 6/117 (5.1%) in the combination group and 3/116 (2.6%) in the monotherapy group.  
   - The exclusion proportion is not large, but it is unequal between groups. No participant-level reasons are given specifically for absence of the week-4 DBP outcome, and no arm-specific description identifies whether these 9 patients withdrew, experienced adverse effects, were lost to follow-up, never received treatment, or had other missing-data circumstances.  
   - The registry describes more discontinuations over the whole eight-week study—20/117 in the combination group and 15/116 in monotherapy—including more losses to follow-up in the combination group (5 versus 1). These later figures cannot simply be assumed to apply before week 4, but they make the absence of a week-4 missing-data accounting unsatisfactory.  
   - There is no report of imputation, sensitivity analyses, or analyses including all randomized participants with appropriately justified handling of missing week-4 data. The analysis instead excludes participants without a post-randomization measurement.  
   - Because the amount of missing data is modest and the treatment effect is reasonably larger than the likely influence of a few observations, a high-risk judgement is not compelled. Nevertheless, the reasons for missingness and whether missingness was related to both outcome and treatment are insufficiently documented.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low risk**  
   - **Outcome: change in mean sitting DBP from baseline to week 4.**  
   - This is a physiological, instrument-based outcome, not a participant-reported subjective outcome. The trial used a fully automated Omron blood-pressure device. This substantially limits the opportunity for assessors’ expectations to influence the recorded numerical DBP directly.  
   - Measurement procedures were reasonably standardised: readings were taken at approximately the same time of day before dosing; participants rested for at least five minutes in a quiet room; three sitting measurements at one-minute intervals were obtained; and the average was used. If DBP values differed by more than 10 mm Hg, additional readings were obtained and the highest and lowest of five values discarded.  
   - The arm with the highest mean DBP at baseline was selected for subsequent measurements, and the investigators attempted to use the same assessor for a given participant throughout the study. These provisions support within-participant consistency.  
   - The trial was unblinded. Staff taking measurements knew treatment assignments and could theoretically influence conditions such as rest time, cuff positioning, or whether the protocol was followed rigorously. However, automated measurement, repeated readings, and a prespecified measurement procedure make material differential measurement bias unlikely for this objective endpoint.  
   - Hence, despite lack of blinding, measurement bias for the primary DBP outcome is judged low risk.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The primary outcome and its time point were prospectively registered: “Changes in Mean Sitting DBP From Baseline After 4 Weeks of Therapy.” The manuscript identifies the same outcome as its primary objective. Thus, the published endpoint is concordant with the registered primary endpoint.  
   - The stated analysis—an ANCOVA with treatment as a factor and baseline BP as a covariate—is appropriate for the registered outcome. The registry presents the primary outcome as least-squares means, consistent with an adjusted ANCOVA-type analysis.  
   - However, the manuscript principally presents unadjusted mean changes with SDs (−17.8 versus −14.1 mm Hg), while the registry gives an adjusted least-squares mean of −17.0 mm Hg for the combination group and −14.1 mm Hg for monotherapy. The manuscript does not clearly present the adjusted treatment contrast, its confidence interval, or the exact model-derived estimates corresponding to the reported P value.  
   - No full protocol or prespecified statistical analysis plan is available to verify, for example, the precise covariance model, handling of missing data, whether any centre effects were planned, or whether alternative analyses were undertaken and selectively omitted.  
   - There are material inconsistencies in the trial records: the article reports 233 randomized and 224 in the ITT set, whereas the registry’s design module gives an actual enrolment of 214 and its results section refers to 223 randomized. In addition, the supplied description of the study flow figure erroneously suggests that the monotherapy group switched to candesartan 32 mg/HCT at week 4, contrary to the textual protocol. These discrepancies do not establish selective reporting of the primary result, but they reduce confidence in the reporting record.  
   - The endpoint was clearly registered and reported, so high risk is not justified; nevertheless, incomplete transparency about the prespecified analysis and inconsistent numerical records warrant some concerns.

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   - The primary DBP outcome was objectively measured with a reasonably standardised automated procedure, and the registered primary endpoint was reported at the intended 4-week time point.  
   - Nonetheless, allocation concealment is not described, the week-4 analysis excludes randomized participants without an adequate account of why their outcome data were missing, and inconsistencies between the publication and registry impair confidence in the exact analysis and reporting record.  
   - These are important limitations, but the available evidence does not demonstrate a high risk of bias for the 4-week DBP result.
