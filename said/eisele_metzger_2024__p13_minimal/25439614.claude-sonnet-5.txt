# Risk of Bias Assessment: Visual Acuity Outcome
## DRCR.net 5-Year Ranibizumab Prompt vs. Deferred Laser Trial

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: Some concerns**

*Justification:* The manuscript states participants were "randomly assigned" but provides no description of the randomization method (e.g., computer-generated sequence), allocation concealment mechanism, or stratification factors in this report (though prior publications from this trial series reportedly used adequate methods). Baseline characteristics between prompt and deferred groups are not shown in this paper for the original randomized cohort (only for completers vs. non-completers within each arm), making it impossible to verify baseline balance from this document alone. Given this is a well-known multicenter NIH-funded network trial with an established track record of rigorous randomization in earlier publications, I lean toward "some concerns" rather than "high risk," but I cannot confirm true randomization/allocation concealment from the text provided here alone — this must be verified against the original 2010 protocol paper (reference 1).

---

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: Some concerns**

*Justification:* This is an open-label trial by design — laser was either given promptly (mandatory at baseline) or deferred (≥24 weeks); this cannot be masked from treating physicians or patients due to the nature of the intervention. Treatment decisions for retreatment with ranibizumab/laser were based on protocol-specified criteria, which somewhat mitigates deviations. However, note that 9 (7%) of prompt-laser completers received other rescue treatments (bevacizumab, triamcinolone, vitrectomy), with 2 of these explicitly noted as "not" per protocol — an acknowledged protocol deviation. Additionally, laser could be given in the deferred group based on OCT/investigator judgment thresholds, introducing potential for non-protocol-driven crossover decisions given unblinded status. These deviations are unlikely to be substantial enough to bias the intention-to-treat estimate strongly, but the open-label nature combined with documented off-protocol treatments in the prompt arm raises some concern.

---

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: Some concerns**

*Justification:* Excluding deaths, only 76% of the original prompt-laser arm and 74% of the original deferred-laser arm completed the 5-year visit — a substantial ~24-26% loss to follow-up from the original randomized cohort. Although completion rates among those who consented to the extension were high (97% and 92%), this is a biased sub-selection since consent to the extension itself was non-random and occurred after 3 years of differential treatment experience. Table 1 explicitly documents that baseline characteristics differed between completers and non-completers (age, prior PRP, lens status, DR severity) — confirming that missingness was not at random and is related to true prognostic factors. While the authors attempted to address this via adjusted longitudinal models incorporating all available visit data and confounders, this is a statistical correction rather than a design safeguard, and residual bias from unmeasured factors related to attrition cannot be excluded. This represents meaningful risk given the magnitude of loss (~25%) and documented imbalance.

---

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (Visual Acuity): Low risk**

*Justification:* Visual acuity is a participant/examiner-assessed outcome but was measured objectively via certified, **masked visual acuity examiners** using a standardized E-ETDRS protocol — this is explicitly stated as a strength ("masking of the visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians"). Because the outcome assessors (not the patients or treating physicians) were blinded to treatment allocation, and the outcome (letter score on a standardized chart) is not merely patient self-report of a subjective symptom (e.g., pain, satisfaction) but an examiner-administered objective measurement, the risk of differential measurement bias is low despite the open-label treatment assignment. The same certified/masked examiner protocol was used across both arms and over time, and methodology of acuity assessment did not differ between groups.

---

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: Low risk**

*Justification:* The primary outcome (visual acuity at 5 years) was pre-specified as the "Main Outcome Measure" and is consistent with the outcome specified in earlier publications from this same trial (1-year, 3-year reports) and the registered protocol (available on the DRCR.net website, as cited). Multiple pre-specified secondary and exploratory VA-related metrics (≥10-letter change, ≥15-letter change, subgroup analyses by baseline VA) are reported comprehensively, including both favorable and unfavorable results for each group (e.g., higher loss ≥10 letters trends are also reported), suggesting no selective suppression of unfavorable results. Longitudinal models, sensitivity analyses (adjusted vs. unadjusted, completers-only vs. full cohort), and full result tables (including CIs and exact p-values) are transparently presented, including results that do not favor the study's suggested conclusion equally with those that do (e.g., P=0.09 for primary continuous VA outcome is reported prominently despite not reaching conventional significance).

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **Some concerns**

*Justification:* While measurement of the primary visual acuity outcome itself is at low risk of bias due to examiner masking and outcome selection appears appropriately pre-specified and transparently reported, there are **multiple domains with "some concerns"** — particularly (1) incomplete transparency regarding the original randomization/allocation concealment process in this specific report, (2) unavoidable deviations from intended intervention due to the open-label design combined with documented off-protocol rescue treatments, and (3) substantial missing outcome data (~25% loss from original randomized cohort) with demonstrated differential baseline characteristics between completers and non-completers. Per the stated rules, "some concerns" in multiple domains that could plausibly compound to lower confidence in the estimated treatment effect on visual acuity warrants an overall rating of **some concerns** rather than "low risk," though it does not rise to "high risk" given the extensive sensitivity analyses and adjustments the authors performed to mitigate these issues.
