Here is my assessment of the manuscript's risk of bias for the endpoint **intentions to consume the RDIFV**, evaluated step-by-step according to the specified criteria.

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low risk**
(Justification: The authors state that the intervention and control booklets were placed in a single pile in an order determined by a true random number generator, and then handed out sequentially in a lecture theatre. While the concealment mechanism is rudimentary, assuming the covers of the booklets were identical, the distributors and participants would not have foreseen the allocation. Furthermore, baseline characteristics (pre-intervention cognitions and demographics) did not differ significantly between the groups, suggesting that the randomization process was successful and uncompromised.)

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**
(Justification: The intervention was a brief, leaflet-like task embedded directly within a booklet that participants completed immediately under "examination conditions." Given this highly controlled, single-session administration format, there is virtually no risk that participants did not receive the intended intervention or that substantial cross-contamination occurred during the administration phase.)

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk**
(Justification: While the study reports a high attrition rate of 33% at the 1-week behavioral follow-up, the specific endpoint in question—*intentions to consume the RDIFV*—was measured immediately after the intervention/control task within the same sitting and same booklet. There is no indication of substantial missing data or dropout between reading the leaflet and answering the immediate post-intervention cognitive measures.)

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: high risk**
(Justification: The outcome (intention to consume the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables) is a highly subjective, self-reported measure. Participants were not blinded to their group assignment, as the intervention group engaged in explicit planning and persuasive tasks regarding their fruit and vegetable intake, while the control group answered filler questions about the questionnaire itself. Because the study relies on self-reported intentions immediately following a transparently persuasive task, participants in the intervention arm were highly vulnerable to demand characteristics and social desirability bias. They likely knew exactly what the researchers wanted them to say, severely compromising the objectivity of the outcome measurement.)

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**
(Justification: There is no reference to a published protocol or prospective trial registration (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) that would allow verification of the pre-specified primary and secondary endpoints, or the planned statistical analyses. Without a pre-registered analysis plan, we cannot rule out the possibility that the reported analyses were selected from multiple conducted tests, raising some concerns about selective reporting.)

**6. Overall rating: high risk**
(Justification: The study is judged to be at high risk of bias overall. While the randomization and missing data domains for this specific immediate outcome are acceptable, the reliance on a subjective, self-reported intention scale from unblinded participants who were just subjected to a highly transparent, persuasive intervention introduces a critical and unavoidable measurement bias (Domain 4).)
