# Risk of Bias Assessment: Neuropsychological Test Performance
## (Weight Loss Intervention in Elderly Obese Patients with MCI)

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: The manuscript states this was a "1:1, randomized study" but provides no description of the randomization sequence generation method (e.g., computer-generated, random number tables) nor of allocation concealment procedures. Table 1 shows reasonable baseline balance between groups, which is reassuring, but this alone cannot compensate for the complete absence of methodological detail on sequence generation and concealment. The registry indicates masking was applied only to "OUTCOMES_ASSESSOR" — there is no mention of allocation concealment from the personnel enrolling participants. Given the nutritional counseling intervention is inherently unblindable to participants and treating staff, concealment at the point of allocation is critical and is not addressed. I flag this as "some concerns" due to insufficient reporting rather than clear evidence of a flawed process.

---

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: HIGH RISK**

Justification: Multiple deviations are documented that affect the integrity of the intended comparison:
- The core design assumption failed: the "intensive" (nutritional counselling) group did NOT lose significantly more weight than the "conventional" group (P = .428) — the two groups were supposed to represent different degrees of caloric restriction, but this key intervention-outcome contrast collapsed.
- Both groups apparently received unplanned weight-loss motivation: the authors admit that "the process of recruiting volunteers included information on the risks of obesity" which "may have increased motivation to lose weight in both groups" — a contamination/co-intervention effect not part of the original design.
- Adherence to the intensive intervention itself was compromised ("mobility difficulties of the elderly decreased adherence to group meetings, and nutritional counseling was a mild intervention").
- Critically, due to this failure, the authors performed a **post-hoc, unplanned analytic pivot**: abandoning the per-protocol group comparison and instead pooling groups to analyze ΔBMI as a continuous predictor. This is an analysis of a substantially different (and non-randomized, confounded) exposure variable, not the originally randomized comparison. This is a major deviation with directly outcome-relevant consequences (introducing confounding since BMI change was no longer randomly assigned).

This constitutes a fundamental deviation from the intended intervention/analysis, undermining causal inference for the reported "cognitive improvement" association.

---

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: LOW RISK**

Justification: Of 80 randomized, 75 completed follow-up (5 dropouts: 4 lost, 1 death from pneumonia, unrelated to intervention) — a 6.25% attrition rate, well below thresholds of concern. Dropout was low and reasonably balanced (37/40 and 38/40 per Figure 1). No evidence suggests missingness was related to the outcome (cognitive performance) or differed systematically by group. This is unlikely to meaningfully bias results.

---

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — Neuropsychological Test Performance: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: 
- The outcome assessors were reported (per registry) as masked ("SINGLE" masking, outcomes assessor blinded), which is reassuring for an objective, examiner-administered outcome.
- However, several of the neuropsychological instruments and sub-measures rely substantially on subjective self- or informant-report (e.g., IQCODE — an *informant*-reported measure of cognitive decline) which cannot be meaningfully blinded to the intervention, since patients/informants know whether they participated in intensive weight-loss counselling sessions (highly salient, time-intensive group meetings over 12 months) versus standard care. This creates a plausible expectation/placebo bias specifically for IQCODE and possibly for performance on effort-dependent cognitive tests if participants were more motivated post-intervention.
- No description is given regarding whether the psychometrists administering tests were kept unaware of participants' weight-loss status (which might be visually apparent given actual weight change, undermining assessor blinding despite formal "masking").
- No mention of standardized timing, test-retest/practice effects control (e.g., alternate forms), which the authors themselves acknowledge as a potential confound they merely argue against qualitatively rather than through design (alternate form use, control group comparison having failed due to point 2 above).

Given assessor blinding was nominally in place but likely imperfect (due to visible weight change) and given that subjective/informant components (IQCODE) are included in the composite "global cognition" domain, I rate "some concerns" rather than "low risk."

---

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: HIGH RISK**

Justification: Comparing the registered protocol to the published report reveals substantial discrepancies:
- The registered primary outcome was the RCT comparison (medical care vs. caloric restriction) for "Neuropsychological test performance" — a between-groups analysis. This primary comparison **failed to show significant differences** ("without difference between the groups," confirmed in Supplemental Table 2 with only one significant time×group interaction, for gait speed, an outcome unrelated to cognition).
- Rather than reporting this null result as the primary finding, the authors pivoted to a secondary, unplanned, and confounded continuous-exposure analysis (ΔBMI as continuous predictor, pooling both arms) explicitly justified post hoc ("we decided to a posteriori pool..."). This new analysis then became the emphasized result in the abstract, results, and conclusions ("Intentional weight loss...was associated with cognitive improvement").
- Numerous additional exploratory subgroup analyses (age × ΔBMI, APOE4 × ΔBMI interactions) and correlational analyses (leptin, HOMA-IR, CRP, diet macronutrients vs. multiple cognitive tests) were conducted without correction for multiple comparisons (explicitly acknowledged: "it was not possible to carry a multiple comparisons correction"). Many outcomes were tested (Table 4 alone tests 6 biomarkers/diet variables against 7 cognitive measures = 42 correlations), inflating false-positive risk, with selective narrative emphasis on the significant associations.
- The original protocol also lists "Diagnosis of dementia" as a co-primary outcome; this is not reported at all in the results, a form of selective outcome omission.

This pattern — failed primary RCT comparison, unplanned pivot to a confounded secondary analysis, extensive unadjusted multiple testing, and omission of a registered primary outcome — represents a high risk of selective outcome reporting bias.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **HIGH RISK**

**Justification for overall rating:** While missing data risk is low, this trial exhibits high risk of bias due to deviations from intended intervention (failure of the randomized comparison itself, with a post-hoc pivot to a non-randomized continuous exposure analysis) combined with a high risk of bias in selection of the reported result (abandonment of the pre-specified primary outcome comparison in favor of exploratory analyses, omission of a co-primary outcome, and extensive uncorrected multiple testing). Per the rating rules, a "high risk" judgment in even one domain — and here in two independent domains — is sufficient to warrant an overall "high risk" rating. The central causal claim of the paper (weight loss "associated with cognitive improvement") is not supported by the actual randomized comparison, which showed no between-group difference; it instead rests on a secondary, confounded, hypothesis-generating analysis presented with the confidence of a primary finding.
