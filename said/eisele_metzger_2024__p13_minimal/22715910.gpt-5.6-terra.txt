**Endpoint assessed: Inhaler Use Technique** — measured using a facilitator-completed nine-step observational checklist (score 0–9), for the participant’s relevant inhaler device(s). For participants using more than one inhaler, the mean number of mistakes was reportedly used in analysis. Device-specific outcome results were not reported separately; therefore, the judgement applies to the reported composite inhaler-technique outcome.

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
The article and registry describe the study as randomized, with 92 participants allocated among four groups (community video, knowledge video, both videos, pamphlet). However:

- No method used to generate the allocation sequence is reported. There is no statement that a computer-generated random sequence, random-number table, or another genuinely random mechanism was used.
- There is no information on allocation concealment. In particular, the report does not state whether assignment was administered centrally, by opaque sequentially numbered envelopes, or by an independent person. Thus, recruiters or personnel enrolling participants may have been able to anticipate or influence allocation.
- The authors state that baseline comparisons found no statistically significant group differences. This is not an adequate substitute for information about random-sequence generation and concealment, particularly in a small trial of approximately 20–22 participants per group.
- The registry describes *single masking of participants*, but this is difficult to reconcile with the interventions: participants would ordinarily know whether they watched one video, two videos, or read a pamphlet. The manuscript does not explain how participant masking was achieved.
- There are inconsistencies in group numbering between the article, tables/figure, and registry. Although this does not itself prove faulty randomization, it reduces confidence that allocation and subsequent analysis were implemented and reported unambiguously.

Accordingly, randomization is claimed but inadequately documented, and allocation concealment is entirely unreported.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
All groups received an educational intervention, but in distinctly recognisable formats. Participants were likely aware of the format and quantity of material they received, despite the registry’s claim of participant masking.

Step-by-step concerns are:

- Participants allocated to one video, two videos, or a pamphlet would be able to identify their intervention. Thus, any effects of expectations, motivation, or differential effort in practising inhaler technique cannot be excluded.
- The intervention included educational content directly relevant to the endpoint. Participants who received video material, especially both videos, may have had more engagement or opportunity to rehearse the required technique than pamphlet recipients.
- The report does not state whether delivery was standardized beyond provision of the assigned materials; for example, it does not describe monitoring of intervention completion, viewing duration, repeat viewing, reading adherence, or contamination between groups.
- There is no account of whether participants received additional inhaler teaching from clinicians, family, or facilitators during follow-up, nor whether such co-interventions differed by group.
- On the favourable side, all participants appear to have been assessed with the same stated checklist, and the facilitators were said to be unaware of the study hypothesis. There is no direct evidence that participants crossed over between assigned intervention formats or that the trial personnel deliberately altered care because of allocation.

Nevertheless, because blinding was implausible, fidelity and contamination were not reported, and the educational intervention could affect behaviour beyond its specified content, there are some concerns.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
Missing data are inadequately accounted for and may be substantial for the inhaler-technique analysis.

- Of 92 enrolled intervention participants, 85 completed the study: seven participants (7.6%) did not complete follow-up. The flow diagram states that three were excluded and four discontinued intervention, but provides neither their group allocation nor their reasons for exclusion/discontinuation.
- The manuscript does not report the number with observed inhaler-technique data at baseline and at the 3-month follow-up in each randomized group.
- The reported inhaler-technique model has degrees of freedom of 1 and 71, implying that the analytic sample was materially smaller than the 85 completers, or at least that not all randomized participants contributed to that analysis. The source and distribution of this additional apparent loss are not explained.
- No comparison of baseline inhaler technique or participant characteristics between those with and without follow-up data is provided.
- No intention-to-treat analysis, multiple imputation, sensitivity analysis, or other method to address missing outcome data is described.
- Attrition is particularly problematic in an educational study of performance technique: those unable, unwilling, or less successful in learning or performing the inhaler technique may plausibly be more likely not to complete assessment. Therefore, it is quite possible that missingness was related to the true outcome.

Because the amount and reasons for missing inhaler-technique data are unclear, and the analysis apparently excludes participants without adequate justification or sensitivity analyses, this domain is at high risk of bias.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — Inhaler Use Technique: some concerns**  
The outcome was an observed performance measure rather than a participant-reported subjective outcome. This is an important strength: lack of participant blinding is less problematic here than it would be for self-reported symptoms, satisfaction, adherence, pain, or perceived benefit.

However, important measurement concerns remain:

- Inhaler technique was assessed using a standardized nine-step observational checklist, which is an appropriate structured method for measuring this endpoint.
- The manuscript states that two observers—the interviewer/community facilitator and research coordinator—judged technique during face-to-face interviews. This potentially improves assessment, but the procedures are insufficiently described.
- It is not reported whether outcome assessors were blinded to assigned intervention. In practice, blinding seems unlikely: the same staff conducted in-person intervention-related sessions and interviews, and may have known whether the participant had watched particular video(s) or received a pamphlet.
- The facilitators were reportedly unaware of the study hypothesis. This is helpful but is not equivalent to being unaware of intervention assignment. Knowledge of allocation can influence how strictly a checklist is applied, whether clarification or encouragement is offered, and how borderline steps are scored.
- The report does not specify whether the two assessors rated independently, whether disagreements were resolved by consensus, whether any inter-rater reliability was assessed, or whether assessors were trained and calibrated.
- Different inhaler types were included (e.g., metered-dose inhaler, TurboHaler, Diskus, HandiHaler), yet the article reports only a combined score. The validity and comparability of averaging errors across different devices are not established. Nor are device-specific results supplied.
- Repeated use of the identical assessment at baseline, immediate post-test, and follow-up may itself improve performance through practice and feedback. This would tend to improve technique in all groups, and is especially relevant because all groups improved substantially.

The checklist and direct observation reduce the likelihood of major measurement bias, but the absence of clear assessor blinding and reliability procedures, combined with repeated testing and pooled device-specific scores, leaves some concerns.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high risk**  
The available registration information raises serious concerns that the reported inhaler-technique result was selectively chosen from multiple possible analyses and time points.

- The ClinicalTrials.gov record identifies **medication adherence** as the primary outcome. It does not identify inhaler-use technique as a primary or secondary outcome, does not pre-specify the nine-step checklist score, and does not pre-specify the reported composite/mean-error approach for participants using more than one inhaler.
- The registered primary-outcome time frame is recorded as “on year,” whereas the article emphasizes results from baseline to the 3-month post-intervention follow-up. The manuscript notes an immediate post-test and a telephone assessment six months after follow-up, but results for inhaler technique at these other planned/collected time points are not reported.
- The article states that identical assessment tools were used at three interviews, yet only selected pretest and 3-month follow-up results are presented in the main results.
- Numerous analytic choices were available: four intervention groups versus video/print comparisons; time effects; adjusted ANCOVA models; interaction terms; analyses by sex, ethnicity, age, and education; and different approaches for participants using multiple inhalers. There is no protocol or statistical analysis plan specifying which inhaler-technique contrast, model, covariate adjustment, or time point was primary.
- The reporting is internally confusing. For example, text, tables, and registry labels inconsistently identify which numbered group received which video. This prevents confident identification of the prespecified comparison, if any.
- The report focuses heavily on within-group and overall improvement over time, although all groups received asthma education and repeated technique assessments. Such analyses do not provide a clean randomized comparison of one intervention format against another.

In the absence of a pre-specified inhaler-technique outcome, time point, and analysis plan—and given selective presentation from several assessments and analyses—the risk of selective reporting is high.

**6. Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
The overall rating is **high risk of bias**. Although the endpoint was measured by structured observation rather than patient self-report, the result is undermined by high risk of bias due to missing outcome data and selection of the reported result. Additional concerns arise from undocumented allocation concealment, implausible participant masking, possible unblinded outcome assessment, and inadequate reporting of intervention fidelity and analytic decisions.
