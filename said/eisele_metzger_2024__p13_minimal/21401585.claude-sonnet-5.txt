# Risk of Bias Assessment: INCITE Trial (Sakzewski et al.)
## Outcome: Quality of movement of the impaired upper limb (MUUL)

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: LOW RISK**

Justification: Randomization was preceded by a matched-pairs strategy (age, sex, side of hemiplegia, MUUL score within 10%), which is a legitimate technique to reduce baseline imbalance rather than a source of bias. Allocation used a computer-generated random number list with concealed envelopes opened by non-study personnel — this constitutes adequate allocation concealment. Baseline comparison (Table I) shows no meaningful group differences in MUUL scores (67.1 vs 70.8) or other prognostic variables, consistent with successful randomization. No evidence of manipulation or foreknowledge of allocation is present.

---

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: This is an inherently unblinded behavioural intervention (participants/families and treating therapists could not be blinded to CIMT vs BIM), which is unavoidable given the nature of the intervention and does not by itself constitute high risk provided outcome assessment is blinded (addressed under Domain 4). However, several deviations are noted: (a) camps were run **consecutively rather than concurrently** (BIM camps first, then CIMT), introducing a potential time/order confound and possible learning effects in delivery (e.g., staff experience, seasonal/practice effects) between arms; (b) only 58% of participants received the full intended 60 hours of therapy, with ~40% in each group receiving reduced dosage (54 hours) due to holidays/illness, and this was not perfectly balanced across arms. These deviations were described and analyzed via intention-to-treat, but the non-concurrent delivery design is a notable departure from an ideal parallel-group trial structure that could differentially affect implementation fidelity.

---

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: LOW RISK**

Justification: Of 64 randomized children, 63 received allocated intervention with only one dropout (day 2, BIM group, due to pre-existing emotional/behavioural issues unrelated to the intervention or outcome). Retention to 26-week follow-up appears high (per CONSORT diagram), and analysis was conducted on an intention-to-treat basis. The pattern of missingness is not plausibly related to the outcome (MUUL) value, and the amount of missing data is minimal and balanced.

---

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of outcome — MUUL: LOW RISK**

Justification: The MUUL is a performance-based (observer-scored), not participant-self-reported, outcome, reducing the concern that would normally apply to subjective patient-reported outcomes in unblinded trials. Critically, outcome videos were scored by occupational therapists **masked to group allocation** and in **random order**, which directly protects against differential assessment bias despite the inevitable lack of blinding of participants/treating therapists. The authors additionally established excellent intra-rater reliability for the MUUL specifically for this study (ICC 0.93, SEM 2.7%), supporting measurement precision. This is a well-controlled, low-risk outcome assessment process.

---

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: LOW RISK**

Justification: The trial protocol was prospectively published (Boyd et al., BMC Neurology 2010, ref 17) prior to outcome reporting, and the MUUL is explicitly identified there and in the Methods as a primary outcome measure (alongside the AHA), consistent with sample size calculations based on MUUL as primary endpoint. The results for MUUL are reported fully across all pre-specified time points (baseline, 3, 26 weeks) with pre-specified statistical models (GEE), and results are presented regardless of statistical significance (e.g., non-significant 3-week difference is reported alongside significant 26-week difference). No evidence of selective reporting for this specific outcome.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: While randomization, missing data handling, outcome measurement (via masked assessors), and outcome reporting are all judged low risk, the domain "deviations from intended interventions" raises some concern due to the sequential (non-concurrent) delivery of the two arms and unequal/incomplete dosing in a substantial minority of participants in both groups. This single domain with "some concerns" is sufficient to downgrade the overall judgment from "low risk," per the rule that at least one domain rated "some concerns" precludes an overall "low risk" rating. It does not, however, reach "high risk," as the deviation is unlikely to have substantially distorted the between-group comparison for this objectively and blindly assessed outcome.
