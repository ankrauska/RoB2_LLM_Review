# Risk of Bias Assessment: Length of Hospital Stay

**Study:** Prospective randomized comparison of LAVH vs AH for uteri >200g (Schneider et al.)

---

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns**

*Justification:* The authors state that "procedure type was assigned by a computer-generated randomization list, and concealment of randomization was done by telephone inquiry," which supports adequate random sequence generation and allocation concealment at the point of enrollment. However, there is a significant imbalance in surgeon seniority between groups: 71.4% of LAVH patients were operated on by an attending physician vs. only 40% in the AH group (p<0.05, explicitly reported as significant by the authors). This imbalance—despite proper randomization—raises concern that randomization may have failed to produce comparable groups, or that there was some issue with allocation execution (e.g., scheduling based on surgeon availability influencing group assignment in practice). This baseline imbalance in a key prognostic factor (surgeon experience) is concerning for the interpretation of the length-of-stay outcome, as surgeon experience plausibly affects operative complications and recovery.

---

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns**

*Justification:* No blinding of patients or care providers was possible or attempted, which the authors themselves acknowledge ("patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment"). For an outcome like length of hospital stay, this is important because "each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital." This subjective discharge criterion, combined with unblinded allocation and unblinded caregivers, creates a high potential for the intervention to influence care decisions/discharge timing beyond the biological effect of the surgery itself (e.g., differing expectations, differing enthusiasm of surgical teams, or systematic differences in encouragement to go home). There is no mention of a standardized, objective discharge protocol/criteria that was applied equally in both arms. This is a substantive deviation from a rigorous intended protocol for an outcome so directly susceptible to non-biological influence.

---

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Low risk**

*Justification:* Length of hospital stay is an objective, hospital-recorded outcome unlikely to be missing for any of the 48 randomized patients (28 LAVH, 20 AH). All patients appear accounted for in Table 2 data reporting for this outcome, with no dropouts or exclusions reported affecting this specific endpoint. There is no indication that any patient was lost prior to discharge (a hospital-based, hard endpoint), and thus missingness is unlikely to bias this result.

---

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — Length of Hospital Stay
**High risk**

*Justification:* This outcome is nominally "objective" (calendar days), but the actual determination of hospital stay was governed by a **subjective, patient-driven discharge criterion**: "Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital." Given that patients were unblinded to their treatment allocation (unavoidably, since incision/approach differs), and given the well-documented and likely differing expectations between "minimally invasive" laparoscopic surgery and open abdominal surgery, patients' subjective readiness for discharge could easily be influenced by their knowledge of which procedure they received rather than by their objective physiological recovery. This directly conflates a nominally objective outcome with a highly subjective, unblinded assessment process, creating high risk of bias in measurement/ascertainment of this specific outcome. Additionally, the discharge policy is stated to be influenced by non-clinical factors (German fee-per-day reimbursement structure), further weakening the objectivity of this metric as a true reflection of clinical recovery.

---

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Low risk**

*Justification:* Length of hospital stay was explicitly pre-specified as the **primary outcome measure** in the Methods section, with an a priori sample size calculation based on this endpoint ("We defined the primary outcome measure as length of hospital stay... we calculated that we would need a sample size of 20 cases per group"). The result is reported fully and consistently in both the Results section and Table 2, with actual data provided (median, IQR) matching the pre-specified analysis plan. There is no indication of selective reporting, post-hoc redefinition, or suppression of unfavorable results for this outcome; it is prominently reported as expected and highlighted as the key finding.

---

## 6. Overall Rating for "Length of Hospital Stay": **High risk**

*Justification:* While randomization procedures were nominally adequate and outcome reporting was pre-specified and complete, the combination of (a) a substantial and clinically important imbalance in surgeon experience between groups (raising doubts about the randomization's success in creating comparable groups), (b) an unblinded design with patient-driven discharge criteria highly susceptible to bias from knowledge of treatment allocation and differing expectations regarding LAVH vs AH, and (c) explicit acknowledgment by the authors of these limitations affecting recovery/discharge, collectively and substantially undermine confidence in this specific outcome. The subjective nature of the discharge decision-making process in an unblinded trial is the primary driver pushing this outcome to a high-risk rating, as it represents a direct pathway by which the "difference" in length of stay could be at least partly an artifact of patient/surgeon expectation rather than a true difference in physiological recovery.
