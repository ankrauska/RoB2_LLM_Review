1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - Eligible open-label responders were allocated 1:1 to escitalopram continuation or placebo using a **computer-generated randomisation list**. This is an appropriate random sequence-generation method.  
   - Allocation was implemented using sequential randomisation numbers in blocks of four. The randomisation details were stated to be unknown to investigators and held in **sealed opaque envelopes**. Although the operational safeguards for envelopes (for example, tamper-proof and sequential opening) are not fully described, the reported arrangements are broadly consistent with allocation concealment.  
   - Tablets were described as identical in appearance, taste, and smell, and investigators, study personnel, and participants were said to remain blinded throughout the double-blind phase.  
   - Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics at randomisation were reported as similar between groups.  
   - There is a minor numerical ambiguity: 163 plus 157 equals 320 randomised patients, yet the text says that two randomised patients were excluded for not meeting the response criterion while also describing the ITT population as 320. This inconsistency is unsatisfactory, but there is no indication that it reflects a failure of random sequence generation or allocation concealment.  

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - The assigned interventions were clear: continuation of the established escitalopram dose versus tapering/switching to placebo. The latter necessarily involved discontinuation of escitalopram and is therefore part of the intervention comparison rather than, in itself, a protocol deviation.  
   - Placebo and active tablets were matched, and formal blinding procedures were reported. There is no report of important differential use of prohibited treatments, differential adherence, or systematic departures from assigned treatment.  
   - Nevertheless, the credibility of blinding is questionable. During the first two weeks after randomisation, dizziness occurred in 15.9% of placebo-assigned patients versus 0.6% of escitalopram-assigned patients, and nausea was also more frequent in the placebo group. These symptoms are compatible with discontinuation effects and could have revealed treatment allocation to patients and/or investigators.  
   - Such possible unblinding is particularly relevant because the endpoint includes subjective symptom ratings and an investigator-judged “lack of efficacy” component. Knowledge or suspicion of assignment could alter symptom reporting, clinical management, and decisions that a patient had relapsed.  
   - The authors performed analyses censoring relapses during the first 7 or 14 days and reported preserved statistical significance. This is reassuring but does not fully resolve possible unblinding or behavioural/clinical consequences occurring after that period.  

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - For the time-to-relapse outcome, patients who met relapse criteria were withdrawn, which is consistent with the event definition and does not itself constitute missing outcome data.  
   - Non-relapse withdrawals during the double-blind period were reported in 13/163 (7.9%) escitalopram patients and 14/157 (8.9%) placebo patients. These proportions are relatively modest and similar between groups.  
   - However, the manuscript does not provide sufficiently detailed reasons for every non-relapse withdrawal by treatment group, nor does it demonstrate that censoring was non-informative with respect to subsequent relapse risk. In a time-to-event analysis, censoring patients who leave the study assumes that their subsequent risk of relapse is similar to that of patients remaining under observation; this assumption is not examined adequately.  
   - The primary analysis is described as using an “ITT” population, but the text is internally unclear about the two patients randomised without meeting the response criterion. It is therefore not wholly clear whether every randomised patient contributed appropriately to the primary analysis.  
   - The reported similarity in non-relapse withdrawal proportions reduces concern, but the lack of detailed accounting and assessment of informative censoring prevents a low-risk judgement.  

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: some concerns**  

   **Outcome: time to relapse from the start of double-blind treatment**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - Relapse was a composite outcome: either (a) an increase of at least 5 points in Y-BOCS total score from randomisation, or (b) “unsatisfactory treatment effect”/lack of efficacy judged by the investigator.  
   - The principal component was Y-BOCS deterioration: 116 of 119 relapses (97%) were reportedly attributable to the Y-BOCS criterion. The Y-BOCS is a clinician-rated and partly interview-based assessment of obsessive-compulsive symptoms; it is not a fully objective outcome.  
   - Investigators received rater training, and the same investigator rated a participant whenever possible. These measures improve consistency and reduce random measurement error.  
   - Investigators and participants were nominally blinded, and the active and placebo tablets were matched. This is an important protection against differential outcome assessment.  
   - However, the early marked imbalance in dizziness and nausea after switch to placebo makes successful maintenance of blinding uncertain. If investigators or participants inferred that escitalopram had been withdrawn, this could influence reporting of symptoms, Y-BOCS ratings, and the investigator’s discretionary judgement of “lack of efficacy.”  
   - The investigator-judgement component is especially vulnerable to bias because it is not operationalised in detail and permits subjective classification of relapse. Although it accounted for only 3% of reported relapses, it remains part of the prespecified composite endpoint.  
   - Consequently, measurement bias is not judged high because blinding, matching, training, and standardised rating procedures were used, but there are important residual concerns about functional unblinding and subjective assessment.  

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - The manuscript explicitly identifies time to relapse from the start of double-blind treatment as the prospectively defined primary efficacy endpoint. It also prespecifies the relapse definition and the main analytical approach: log-rank comparison, Kaplan–Meier curves, and Cox proportional-hazards estimation.  
   - The reported primary analysis is consistent with that description: a log-rank test and hazard ratio are presented for time to relapse. This supports the credibility of the reported analysis.  
   - The authors also report several supportive analyses, including per-protocol analysis, analyses censoring early relapses, and a post-hoc analysis restricted to the Y-BOCS relapse criterion. The post-hoc analysis is appropriately identified as such.  
   - However, no trial registration entry, protocol, or statistical analysis plan is provided for independent comparison with the published outcomes and analyses. It is therefore not possible to verify whether the endpoint definition, censoring rules, handling of discontinuations, analysis population, and choice among multiple potential analyses were fully prespecified.  
   - The inconsistency surrounding the number randomised and the stated ITT population adds uncertainty about which analysis set was selected for reporting.  

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The study has credible random sequence generation, reported concealment, and nominal blinding. However, there are **some concerns in multiple domains**: possible functional unblinding caused by discontinuation symptoms; subjective and partly discretionary relapse measurement; incomplete reassurance that censoring/non-relapse withdrawals were non-informative; and inability to verify prespecification against a protocol or registration record. Taken together, these concerns substantially reduce confidence in the estimated effect on time to relapse, particularly because the trial compares continued escitalopram with drug discontinuation and uses a symptom-based relapse outcome.
