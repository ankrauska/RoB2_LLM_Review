1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - Participants were said to be “randomly allocated” to Spinomed or posture-training support (PTS), but the method was simply to pick the name of an orthosis from a bag containing the two names. This might generate an unpredictable sequence if properly implemented, but the report gives no adequate details: who prepared the slips, whether numbers of slips were balanced, whether selection was supervised, or whether assignments could be seen or influenced.  
   - Crucially, concealment of the forthcoming allocation is not established. A bag draw can be vulnerable to manipulation unless opaque, identical slips are used and the process is independently controlled.  
   - The unequal group sizes (10 Spinomed; 8 PTS) are compatible with unrestricted random allocation, but do not reassure us about allocation concealment.  
   - “Single blinded” is asserted, but neither the person blinded nor the mechanism for blinding is identified. This does not resolve the concern about allocation concealment.  
   - Measured baseline characteristics and baseline balance score were broadly similar, but baseline similarity cannot demonstrate that randomization and concealment were adequate, particularly in a trial of only 18 participants.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The intervention was the immediate wearing of a visibly different orthosis, compared within each allocated group with assessment without the orthosis. Participants could not realistically be blinded to whether they were wearing an orthosis, nor to which type of orthosis they had received.  
   - Participants were informed about the study aim and procedure, and were given time to learn the tests. Knowledge that the orthosis was expected to improve balance could plausibly alter effort, caution, posture, or task performance during the sensory organization test (SOT). Such changes may be particularly important for an immediate, performance-based balance outcome.  
   - No sham orthosis, placebo condition, or credible method to equalize expectations was used. The authors explicitly acknowledge the absence of a placebo orthosis.  
   - On the favourable side, the intervention was fitted by a certified orthotist, participants had a stated 1.5-hour adaptation period, testing was conducted shortly afterwards, and all 18 allocated participants apparently completed the assessments. There is no reported evidence of cross-over to the other orthosis, non-adherence, or differential additional treatment.  
   - Nevertheless, the report does not describe whether testing staff encouraged participants uniformly, whether orthosis fitting was standardized and verified, or whether participants’ understanding and behaviour during testing differed by condition. Therefore, there are concerns that awareness of treatment condition could have led to performance-related deviations relevant to the observed balance score.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk**  
   - Eighteen participants were allocated (10 Spinomed and 8 PTS), and the balance-score tables report data for all 10 and all 8 participants both without and with their allocated orthosis.  
   - There is no indication that balance-score data were missing after allocation, that participants withdrew, or that particular test results were excluded.  
   - The study evaluated immediate effects during a single testing procedure, so extensive follow-up loss was not an issue.  
   - The recruitment flow before allocation is not fully described, but this does not indicate missing *post-randomization* balance-score data among the randomized participants. On the information available, missing outcome data are unlikely to have biased the result.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — balance score: high risk**  
   - The balance score was obtained using the NeuroCom EquiTest SOT, an instrumented and standardized test. The automated force-platform recording and predefined score calculation reduce the scope for selective assessor judgement or manual scoring errors.  
   - However, the outcome is not wholly immune to bias merely because it is instrument-derived. The SOT balance score is generated from the participant’s postural performance. It can be affected by attention, confidence, fear of falling, motivation, effort, familiarity with the apparatus, and response to instructions.  
   - Participants necessarily knew whether they were wearing an orthosis or no orthosis, and could recognize the type of orthosis. They were also informed of the research purpose. No sham orthosis or blinding of participants was possible or attempted. This creates a substantial risk that expectations and altered test behaviour contributed to the measured difference in balance score.  
   - The claimed “single blinding” is inadequately reported. It is not stated whether the outcome assessor was blinded, how this could have been maintained while an obvious orthosis was worn, or whether the assessor delivered standardized instructions without knowing condition.  
   - Randomizing the order of testing with and without orthosis, allowing a 1-hour interval, and randomizing SOT stages are useful attempts to limit learning and fatigue. They do not, however, remove bias arising from participants’ and possibly assessors’ awareness of the visible intervention.  
   - Thus, although the instrument itself is objective, measurement of this performance-based outcome is at high risk of bias for the clinically prominent comparison of wearing an orthosis versus not wearing one. The concern is less severe for the direct Spinomed-versus-PTS comparison because both conditions involve an orthosis, but participants and assessors could still distinguish the devices and hold differing expectations.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - Balance score is explicitly described as the “main outcome measure,” and the sample-size calculation was reportedly based on its standard deviation. This is reassuring.  
   - The manuscript reports balance-score results for both groups, with and without orthosis, and reports the between-group comparison of change scores. Thus, there is no obvious omission of a reported balance-score result within the paper.  
   - Nevertheless, no prospectively registered trial record, dated protocol, or prespecified statistical analysis plan is provided. The ethics approval and funding statement are not substitutes for prospective outcome and analysis specification.  
   - The study measured several balance outcomes (balance score, directional control, and reaction time), tested several within- and between-group contrasts, and used a small sample. Without a prespecified analysis plan, it is impossible to exclude selection of favourable analyses, time points, contrasts, or statistical methods.  
   - The reporting focuses heavily on within-group significance tests, despite the stated comparison between two orthoses. This analytical emphasis can be misleading, and the absence of prespecification prevents confidence that the presented analysis was the intended primary analysis.  
   - Therefore, selection of the reported balance-score result raises some concerns.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall rating is **high risk of bias**. The principal reason is the high risk of bias in measurement of the balance score: participants were necessarily aware of the visible orthosis condition, the outcome depended on their performance in a balance task, no sham or expectation-control procedure was used, and the claimed single blinding is unexplained. This is compounded by concerns about allocation concealment, possible awareness-related performance deviations, and the lack of a protocol or registration documenting prespecified analyses.
