1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low risk**  
   - Allocation was described as using **computer-generated random-number codes**, managed by an **independent study member**. This is compatible with a genuinely random allocation sequence.  
   - Allocation was disclosed only **after induction of anaesthesia**. Thus, neither participants nor the clinical team responsible for preoperative selection and preparation should have been able to predict or manipulate assignment before surgery. This is an unusually robust approach to allocation concealment in a surgical trial.  
   - Randomisation incorporated minimisation for important prognostic factors (age, BMI, diabetes duration, insulin treatment, and ethnicity). Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable, although there were modest numerical differences in sex distribution and baseline HbA1c. These do not suggest a failure of randomisation.  
   - The report does not specify the exact algorithm used for minimisation or whether a random element was incorporated. Nevertheless, the use of independent, computer-generated codes and post-anaesthetic disclosure provides sufficient reassurance that allocation was concealed and random.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**  
   - The trial was intended to be double blind for participants and outcome assessors. Although surgeons could not realistically be blinded, assignment was disclosed only after anaesthesia, reducing the opportunity for differential preoperative management.  
   - The procedures were standardised as far as practical: port placement and leak testing were identical, and the operative descriptions were prespecified.  
   - Diabetes medications were discontinued after surgery and subsequently restarted according to a stated standard endocrinology protocol. Importantly, endocrinologists making these medication decisions were reported to be **blinded to treatment assignment**. Since remission required both HbA1c attainment and absence of glucose-lowering medication, blinded and protocol-driven medication management is particularly important.  
   - There is no reported evidence of differential co-interventions, systematic treatment switching, or departures from assigned surgery before the 1-year diabetes-remission assessment. One LSG participant underwent revision to SR-LRYGB for sleeve stricture, but the report does not indicate that this meaningfully affected the remission analysis or that analyses were conducted according to treatment received rather than original allocation.  
   - Blinding after bariatric surgery may be difficult to sustain, especially if complications, investigations, or reoperations disclose the procedure. However, no evidence is presented that unblinding resulted in differential diabetes management or outcome assessment.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - At 1 year, outcome data were available for all 56 SR-LRYGB participants but only 53 of 58 LSG participants. Thus, **5/58 (8.6%)** LSG participants were lost to follow-up, compared with **0/56** in the SR-LRYGB group.  
   - The missingness is therefore differential by treatment group. The stated reason was failure to contact participants and/or failure to attend appointments after 3 months; no information is provided on their HbA1c values, medication use, reasons for disengagement, postoperative complications, or whether their outcomes were related to diabetes remission.  
   - The primary remission analyses appear to be complete-case analyses: denominators were 56 and 53, rather than all randomised participants (56 and 58). No intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis, multiple imputation, or plausible best-/worst-case analysis was reported.  
   - Although the amount of missing data is not large, the total sample is small and the observed between-group differences in remission are also small. The five missing LSG outcomes could modestly alter estimated remission proportions.  
   - There is insufficient evidence to conclude that the missingness was unrelated to the outcome; however, the proportion missing is not so large as to make a high-risk judgement unavoidable.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low risk for each HbA1c-defined remission outcome**  

   **a. T2D remission defined as HbA1c ≤38 mmol/mol (5.6%) without glucose-lowering medication: low risk**  
   - HbA1c is an objective biochemical laboratory measurement, unlikely to be materially influenced by participant or assessor expectations.  
   - The medication-free component could potentially be influenced by clinician decisions; however, diabetes medication review was undertaken by endocrinologists reported to be blinded to allocated surgery and following a standard protocol.  
   - Participants and outcome assessors were also reported to be blinded. Therefore, outcome measurement is unlikely to have differed systematically between groups.

   **b. T2D remission defined as HbA1c <42 mmol/mol (6.0%) without glucose-lowering medication: low risk**  
   - The same objective HbA1c measurement and blinded, protocolised medication-management arrangements apply.  
   - The threshold was applied identically in both groups, with no indication that knowledge of treatment allocation influenced laboratory testing, data extraction, or classification of medication use.

   **c. T2D remission defined as HbA1c <48 mmol/mol (6.5%) without glucose-lowering medication: low risk**  
   - This outcome differs only in the prespecified HbA1c threshold. HbA1c and medication-use status are ascertainable from laboratory and medical records.  
   - The risk that outcome assessment was affected by knowledge of intervention is low because assessors and endocrinologists were stated to be blinded.

   **d. T2D remission defined as HbA1c <53 mmol/mol (7.0%) without glucose-lowering medication: low risk**  
   - This is likewise based on an objective laboratory value plus documented absence of diabetes medication.  
   - The reported blinding and standard medication-restart protocol reduce the risk of differential classification between surgical groups.  
   - The article/table contains a minor numerical inconsistency: the text describes the <53 mmol/mol threshold as 7.0%, while the table labels it as <7.0% (52 mmol/mol). This is a reporting/definition clarity issue, but it does not by itself indicate differential or biased outcome measurement.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The prospective registry specifies “remission of type 2 diabetes mellitus/glycaemic control” as the primary outcome, but gives no sufficiently detailed definition, HbA1c threshold, analytic method, or time point to verify fully whether the reported 1-year analyses followed the registered plan.  
   - The article states that the primary outcome was HbA1c <42 mmol/mol without glucose-lowering therapy **at 5 years**, whereas this paper reports a 1-year analysis. The 1-year remission outcomes are described as being reported to facilitate comparison with prior studies. Thus, the present endpoint is not clearly the trial’s originally designated primary endpoint.  
   - Four alternative HbA1c thresholds were reported. Reporting all four thresholds is preferable to selectively presenting only the most favourable one, and the results are consistent across thresholds. Nevertheless, the availability of several thresholds and the lack of a fully specified registry outcome leaves uncertainty about whether all planned definitions, time points, and analyses were reported.  
   - The article reports the crude 1-year proportions and Fisher exact-test comparisons, but the registry does not permit confirmation that this was the prespecified analysis.  
   - There is no clear evidence of deliberate selective reporting, but the available protocol/registration information is insufficiently specific to exclude it confidently.

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   - Randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding arrangements, and objective HbA1c measurement were generally strong.  
   - However, there are **some concerns** due to differential missing 1-year outcome data (five LSG participants versus none after SR-LRYGB), with no sensitivity analyses, and due to uncertainty about selective reporting because the registration was nonspecific and the paper presents interim 1-year results using four remission thresholds whereas the stated primary outcome was at 5 years.
