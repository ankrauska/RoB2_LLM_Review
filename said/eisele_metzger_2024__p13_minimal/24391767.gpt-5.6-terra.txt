1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - The study reports a computer-generated 1:1 random sequence and use of sealed envelopes for allocation concealment. These are appropriate in principle.  
   - However, the report does not state whether envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, and opened only after irrevocable enrolment. “Sealed envelopes” alone is insufficiently specific to exclude foreknowledge or manipulation of allocation.  
   - The present report is restricted to the 106 participants with an indication for spinal MRI, of whom 93 actually underwent imaging. The restriction to spinal indication appears clinically determined rather than post-randomization, and the baseline characteristics among the analysed patients were broadly similar. This is reassuring, but does not fully establish balance in the original randomized spinal subgroup or in those ultimately contributing image-quality data.  
   - Patients could not be blinded to scanner allocation, but patient blinding is not an essential protection against bias in the randomization process itself.  
   - **Judgement:** some concerns, principally because allocation concealment is incompletely described and the analysed population is a subset of the randomized cohort.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: high risk**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - The intended randomized comparison was initial assignment to open versus short-bore MRI.  
   - The protocol allowed patients who could not tolerate their assigned scanner because of claustrophobia to be cross-referred to the other scanner; 25 examinations were undertaken after cross-referral, and 16 involved conscious sedation.  
   - Thus, a substantial proportion of the analysed examinations were not performed on the scanner originally allocated. The analysis is effectively based on the scanner on which imaging was eventually obtained, rather than a comparison preserving randomized assignment.  
   - Cross-referral was determined by inability to tolerate the initially assigned scanner. Claustrophobia, anxiety, pain, and the need for sedation can plausibly affect movement and motion artefact, and therefore the image-quality outcomes. This is not a benign or random crossover.  
   - Sedation and cross-referral may improve the probability of completing scanning and may affect motion-related image quality. These post-randomization management decisions were inherently linked to both allocated scanner and likely image quality.  
   - Although the authors report subgroup analyses excluding cross-referred examinations and among sedated patients, these are reported only briefly (“data not shown”) and cannot restore the randomized comparison or demonstrate absence of bias.  
   - **Judgement:** high risk, because substantial, outcome-relevant departures from assigned intervention were incorporated in the primary image-quality analysis.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - Of 106 randomized patients with a spinal-MRI indication, only 93 underwent MRI and contributed to the reported image-quality analysis. Thus, outcome data were unavailable for 13/106 (12%) of the relevant randomized spinal subgroup.  
   - In addition, one participant aborted short-bore MRI after acquisition of one sequence; that sequence was excluded from the primary analysis.  
   - Missingness was not random: failure to undergo or complete MRI was explicitly related to claustrophobia and tolerance of the allocated scanner. These factors may be associated with movement, sedation, scan duration, and image quality.  
   - The investigators attempted to reduce missing imaging through cross-referral and sedation. However, this changes the intervention actually received and does not solve the missing-data problem for the randomized comparison.  
   - There is no participant-level accounting of which image-quality outcomes and sequences were missing by randomized group, no appropriate analysis of missing outcomes under plausible assumptions, and no sensitivity analysis addressing the potential effect of the 13 patients without imaging.  
   - **Judgement:** high risk, because a non-trivial amount of outcome data is missing for reasons closely related to intervention tolerability and potentially to image quality.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome:**

   **4a. Qualitative overall image quality: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - Overall image quality was rated on a five-point ordinal scale by two examiners in consensus. This is a subjective outcome.  
   - The investigators state that assessors were blinded to imaging technique and patient identity at the time of analysis. This is an important safeguard.  
   - Nevertheless, the scanners differed substantially in field strength, coil type, noise, signal characteristics, and image appearance. These intrinsic image features may have enabled experienced assessors to infer scanner type despite nominal blinding. The success of blinding was not evaluated.  
   - Ratings were reached by consensus rather than through independently recorded assessments; consequently, inter-rater reliability and the effect of assessor disagreement cannot be examined.  
   - **Judgement:** some concerns.

   **4b. Qualitative contrast: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - Contrast was judged using the same subjective five-point rating process and the same nominally blinded consensus assessors.  
   - Blinding reduces concern, but scanner-specific image appearances could plausibly reveal group assignment.  
   - Window-level parameters were automatically adjusted by assessors for each image, rather than fixed across images. This may reflect clinical practice, but introduces assessor-mediated discretion into a subjective comparison of contrast.  
   - **Judgement:** some concerns.

   **4c. Qualitative contour sharpness: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - Qualitative contour sharpness was assessed subjectively by blinded consensus readers.  
   - As with the other qualitative ratings, apparent scanner identity may be inferable from image appearance, and no evidence is provided that blinding was maintained.  
   - Consensus scoring without independent ratings limits assessment of reproducibility.  
   - **Judgement:** some concerns.

   **4d. Qualitative image noise: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - Image noise was graded subjectively by blinded consensus readers.  
   - Noise is conspicuous and directly related to scanner field strength and coil configuration; therefore, assessors could plausibly identify the scanner from the image, threatening effective blinding.  
   - Nonetheless, the direction of any bias is uncertain, and nominal blinded assessment is a meaningful protection.  
   - **Judgement:** some concerns.

   **4e. Qualitative artefacts: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - Artefacts were rated subjectively by the blinded consensus assessors.  
   - Motion artefact was common and is plausibly influenced by the post-randomization deviations—cross-referral, scan duration, claustrophobia, and sedation. That concerns the intervention/deviation domain, but also means assessors may have encountered characteristic scanner- or management-related patterns.  
   - The nominal blinding is reassuring, but not enough to establish that scanner identity was concealed in practice.  
   - **Judgement:** some concerns.

   **4f. Quantitative signal-to-noise ratio (SNR): low risk**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - SNR was calculated using prespecified formulae from signal intensities and standard deviations measured in defined regions of interest.  
   - The quantitative analysis was performed by one examiner blinded to imaging technique and patient identity.  
   - Region-of-interest placement involves some judgement, but the report describes placement in corresponding anatomical levels, in areas without signal abnormality, with anatomy-standardized sizes.  
   - Differences in field strength, coil configuration, gradient performance, and acquisition parameters are features of the scanner interventions and can legitimately produce SNR differences; they are not measurement bias.  
   - **Judgement:** low risk.

   **4g. Quantitative contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR): low risk**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - CNR was derived according to stated formulae from the quantitative signal measurements.  
   - Measurements were undertaken by a blinded examiner and involved defined tissue comparisons.  
   - Although ROI selection is not entirely free from judgement, there is no indication of differential measurement between groups, and scanner-related differences in CNR are part of the intervention effect being investigated.  
   - **Judgement:** low risk.

   **4h. Quantitative contour sharpness: low risk**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - Contour sharpness was measured using a defined ImageJ line-profile method: the distance needed for signal to rise from 25% to 75% of the grayscale profile.  
   - The method is comparatively objective, is explicitly described, and was performed by a blinded examiner.  
   - Although selecting the contour and placing the line profile require some judgement, the use of a standardized orientation and explicit calculation limits scope for differential assessment.  
   - **Judgement:** low risk.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   **Step-by-step justification:**  
   - The trial was registered prospectively, but the registry identifies claustrophobic reactions preventing MRI as the primary outcome and clinical-management impact as a secondary outcome. It does not specify the present image-quality outcomes, their detailed definitions, spinal-subgroup restriction, sequences, statistical analyses, or timepoints.  
   - This manuscript is a later, derived analysis of the larger CLAUSTRO trial and reports only the spinal-imaging subgroup. The selection of this anatomical subgroup is understandable clinically, but it was not clearly identifiable as a prespecified registered outcome analysis.  
   - The article refers to a published protocol and reports a broad set of qualitative and quantitative image-quality parameters. However, from the materials supplied, it cannot be verified that all reported image-quality parameters, anatomical strata, tissue contrasts, sequences, and subgroup analyses were prospectively specified.  
   - Numerous related outcomes and comparisons were possible: overall quality, contrast, sharpness, noise, artefacts, SNR, several CNR contrasts, several contour interfaces, sequence types, spinal regions, cross-referral exclusions, and sedation subgroups. This creates scope for selective emphasis even if many results are presented.  
   - **Judgement:** some concerns, because prospective specification of this derived image-quality analysis and its exact reported results cannot be established.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   At least two domains are at **high risk**: deviations from intended interventions and missing outcome data. The central comparison is materially compromised by cross-referral to the alternative scanner, frequent sedation, analysis according to the scanner eventually used rather than necessarily randomized assignment, and exclusion of patients unable to complete imaging. These problems are particularly consequential because tolerance, movement, and sedation can directly affect image-quality parameters.
