1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
   - Eligible patients were those who had achieved complete response, partial response, or stable disease after six induction cycles of paclitaxel/gemcitabine. The randomized comparison therefore appropriately concerns the post-induction maintenance strategy in this selected population.  
   - Allocation was reported as 1:1 using a “random permutation method,” with central registration and random assignment coordinated by the Korean Cancer Study Group data centre. Central allocation makes foreknowledge or manipulation of the next assignment unlikely.  
   - Randomization was stratified by clinically important prognostic factors: visceral disease, previous adjuvant taxane treatment, induction response, and hormone-receptor status.  
   - Baseline characteristics were broadly balanced. There were some numerical imbalances—for example, more patients with three or more metastatic sites in the maintenance group (40.5% versus 28.7%) and more hematogenous lung metastases (31.9% versus 16.5%). These differences may occur by chance in a trial of this size and do not, by themselves, demonstrate a failure of randomization. Indeed, some imbalance appears to favour the observation group.  
   - The study was openly conducted, but lack of participant or clinician blinding is not, in itself, a defect in sequence generation or allocation concealment. The central allocation procedure is the relevant protection against selection bias here.  
   - A minor reporting inconsistency is that the manuscript gives ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT00532857 whereas the supplied registry record is NCT00561119. This is concerning for traceability, but does not provide evidence that randomization was compromised.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The trial was explicitly unmasked: maintenance chemotherapy versus observation cannot realistically have been blinded. Consequently, patients and clinicians knew the assigned strategy.  
   - The intended intervention was continued paclitaxel/gemcitabine until progression, unacceptable toxicity, or withdrawal of consent; observation patients were to receive no anticancer therapy before progression. Hormonal therapy was prohibited in both groups before progression. Thus, the protocol did attempt to preserve a clear contrast between strategies.  
   - Nevertheless, departures from the maintenance strategy were substantial. In the maintenance group, 33/116 patients stopped chemotherapy at physician discretion despite no documented progression; 13 stopped because of adverse events and 14 withdrew. The equivalent categories in the observation group cannot be directly comparable because observation involved no active maintenance treatment, but this illustrates that delivery of prolonged chemotherapy depended importantly on unblinded clinician and patient decisions.  
   - These departures are plausibly related to the open-label context, toxicity, and physician judgment. They could alter progression-free survival, particularly if stopping treatment led to altered surveillance, clinical thresholds for declaring progression, or subsequent management.  
   - On the other hand, the efficacy analysis included all randomized participants according to intention to treat. This is the appropriate principal analysis for the effect of assignment to a maintenance-chemotherapy policy, and the report states that patients stopping maintenance without progression did not receive other anticancer treatment before documented progression. There was also no post-progression crossover to paclitaxel/gemcitabine in the observation arm.  
   - Therefore, the intention-to-treat analysis limits, but does not eliminate, concern about deviations in this unblinded maintenance-versus-observation trial.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - All 231 randomized patients were reportedly included in the efficacy analysis according to the intention-to-treat principle. This is reassuring.  
   - Progression-free survival was censored at the last clinical contact for patients lost to follow-up or at the analysis cutoff date. Radiographic assessments were scheduled every six weeks, which should have generated regular information on progression status.  
   - However, the report does not state the number randomized patients with missing PFS information, the number censored in each group, their reasons for censoring, the timing of censoring, or whether censoring differed materially by randomized group. The CONSORT material and discontinuation table do not supply a complete account of PFS censoring.  
   - Patient withdrawal occurred in 14 maintenance and 13 observation patients, and other non-progression discontinuations occurred in both groups. It is not clear whether, and for how long, these participants continued outcome assessment after stopping protocol treatment.  
   - Since PFS depends on detection and documentation of progression, censoring related to illness severity, treatment toxicity, withdrawal, or differential follow-up could be informative. There is insufficient information to establish that the missing/censored outcome data were unlikely to bias the estimated hazard ratio.  

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—progression-free survival: some concerns**  
   - PFS was the primary outcome and was defined from random assignment to documented disease progression or last visit. Disease response/progression was assessed using RECIST version 1.0, with radiographic studies scheduled every six weeks. Use of a defined radiologic criterion and a nominally common assessment schedule are important safeguards.  
   - However, this was an open-label trial. The registry explicitly records no masking, and the manuscript states that PFS and response duration were assessed by investigators. There is no report of blinded independent central radiology review or blinded adjudication of progression events.  
   - Investigators who knew whether a patient was receiving continued chemotherapy or observation could potentially be influenced when interpreting borderline radiographic changes, determining whether clinical findings constituted progression, deciding whether a scan was required outside the nominal schedule, or assigning the documented progression date. This is especially pertinent because the intervention entails obvious toxicity and treatment contact, while the comparator is observation.  
   - PFS is less subjective than a patient-reported symptom outcome, because it is based substantially on imaging and RECIST criteria. Nevertheless, it is not immune to assessment bias in the absence of blinded central review, particularly in an open-label oncology trial.  
   - The manuscript provides no evidence that imaging frequency, completeness, or adjudication was demonstrably equivalent between groups beyond the stated six-week schedule. Thus, there are concerns, though not enough evidence to conclude that measurement bias definitely produced a materially biased result.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The registry record specifies progression-free survival as the primary outcome, and the manuscript likewise identifies PFS after random assignment as the primary endpoint. This agreement is reassuring.  
   - The manuscript reports the main randomized comparison using an intention-to-treat Kaplan–Meier/log-rank analysis, with a hazard ratio and 95% confidence interval (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.55–0.97), as well as median PFS. These are appropriate and informative presentations of the primary outcome.  
   - The paper also emphasizes the six-month PFS rate, calling it “the primary endpoint” in the Results, whereas the Methods describes the primary endpoint as PFS after random assignment and the sample-size calculation refers to an anticipated difference in the six-month PFS rate. This is not necessarily selective reporting, because both time-to-event PFS and the six-month landmark result are reported; nevertheless, the precise prespecified primary analysis is not completely clear.  
   - The available registry record gives only “progression free survival” with an approximate four-year timeframe. It does not provide a detailed statistical analysis plan, prespecified censoring rules, a specified primary PFS summary measure, or a planned six-month landmark analysis. No protocol version or dated statistical analysis plan is available to verify that the reported analyses were finalized before unblinded outcome data were examined.  
   - Furthermore, the registry has no posted results and lists a much later actual primary-completion date than the manuscript’s 2011 analysis cutoff. This may reflect delayed registry updating rather than selective reporting, but it prevents confident verification against contemporaneous prespecification.  

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The randomization process appears adequately protected and the primary PFS result was analysed by intention to treat.  
   - However, confidence in the PFS estimate is substantially reduced by multiple concerns: an unmasked maintenance-versus-observation comparison; investigator-assessed progression without blinded independent review; insufficient reporting of PFS censoring and missing outcome data; and incomplete ability to verify prespecified analytic choices.  
   - Most importantly, PFS ascertainment was performed by unblinded investigators in a setting in which knowledge of treatment allocation could plausibly affect progression detection or dating. Combined with uncertainty about informative censoring and deviations from intended maintenance treatment, these concerns warrant an overall **high risk of bias** judgement for progression-free survival.
