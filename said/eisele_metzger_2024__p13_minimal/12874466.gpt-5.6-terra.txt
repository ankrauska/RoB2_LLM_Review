1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk** (1) Allocation was generated using a computer-generated randomization list, which is an appropriate random sequence-generation method. (2) The list was used to package study tablets and was maintained by an independent third party not involved in study conduct. (3) Treatment packs were designated for individual patients, and investigators and participants were blinded; thus allocation concealment appears adequate. (4) Baseline SGA scores were closely similar (4.30 in bicarbonate versus 4.37 in placebo), as were the reported baseline clinical and dialysis characteristics. Some chance imbalances in comorbidities existed, but these are compatible with a small randomised trial and do not indicate a defective randomization process.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk** (1) This was a placebo-controlled trial with tablets reported to be identical in appearance, packaging, labelling, and mint flavour. Both participants and investigators were blinded. (2) The clinical management and dialysis treatment were stated to be the same in both groups apart from the assigned study medication. (3) Adherence was monitored by pill count and exceeded 90% among those who continued treatment. (4) Seven participants stopped study medication because of adverse effects or intolerance (4 bicarbonate; 3 placebo), a reasonably balanced number. Importantly, the authors state that follow-up biochemical and nutritional assessments continued according to protocol after treatment discontinuation. (5) The stated analysis was intention-to-treat, irrespective of adherence. There is no persuasive evidence of important intervention departures caused by knowledge of allocation or of inappropriate exclusion of non-adherent participants.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high-risk** (1) The primary SGA outcome was intended to be measured repeatedly through 52 weeks, but the report does not provide the number of participants contributing SGA data at each time point or a participant flow diagram. (2) During follow-up, seven participants died (2 bicarbonate; 5 placebo), two transferred to long-term haemodialysis (one per group), and one bicarbonate-group participant received a transplant. These events could result in appreciable missing SGA data, particularly at later assessments. (3) Death, transfer to haemodialysis, and transplantation are all plausibly related to nutritional status, comorbidity, and the SGA outcome. Therefore, missingness is unlikely safely to be assumed unrelated to unobserved SGA values. (4) Although assessments were reportedly continued after medication discontinuation, this does not resolve missing data after death, transplantation, or transfer to haemmodialysis. (5) The repeated-measures ANOVA analysis is described, but there is no account of how missing repeated SGA observations were handled, no denominator at each visit, no sensitivity analysis, and no analysis addressing informative dropout. Given the small sample and the imbalance in deaths, these omissions could materially distort the estimated longitudinal treatment effect.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—subjective global assessment (SGA) score: low-risk** (1) SGA is inherently partly subjective: it incorporates reported weight change and anorexia, clinical assessment of subcutaneous tissue and muscle mass, and assessor judgement in forming the global score. It could therefore be susceptible to expectation effects if participants or assessors knew treatment allocation. (2) However, participants and investigators were reported to be blinded, with a matched starch placebo designed to have identical appearance and taste. (3) SGA was performed by two trained observers who were explicitly blinded both to treatment allocation and to biochemical results. Blinding to bicarbonate results is particularly important because these could otherwise reveal likely allocation. (4) The observers underwent training and achieved a Cohen’s kappa of 0.84 before the study, indicating good interobserver agreement. (5) There is no direct evidence that blinding was broken. Although bicarbonate may have produced physiological effects that could potentially lead to guesses about assignment, the report provides no evidence that assessors knew or could reliably infer allocation. Therefore, measurement of this subjective outcome is unlikely to have differed systematically between groups.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns** (1) The manuscript clearly identifies overall SGA score as the primary outcome and presents a sample-size calculation based on detecting a between-group SGA difference of 1 point. This is reassuring. (2) The methods also state in advance that longitudinal nutritional outcomes, including SGA, would be analysed using repeated-measures ANOVA with treatment group, time, and Charlson Comorbidity Index. The reported principal result—an effect of treatment on longitudinal change in overall SGA score—is consistent with that plan. (3) Nevertheless, no prospectively dated trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial registration is available in the report to verify that the outcome definition, time points, covariate adjustment, repeated-measures model, and post-hoc contrasts were all specified before outcome data were examined. (4) Multiple SGA components and multiple follow-up time points were analysed, with selected significant post-hoc between-group contrasts highlighted. Although Bonferroni adjustment is said to have been used, the exact set of contrasts and missing-data handling are insufficiently reported. (5) Thus, there is no clear evidence of selective reporting, but the absence of an accessible prespecified protocol or registration leaves some concerns.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias** (The trial has credible randomization, concealment, placebo blinding, and blinded SGA assessment. However, the primary repeated SGA outcome is vulnerable to potentially informative missing data arising from deaths, transfer to haemodialysis, and transplantation, without transparent reporting of numbers assessed at each time point or an appropriate missing-data analysis. This limitation is sufficient to substantially lower confidence in the reported SGA effect.)
