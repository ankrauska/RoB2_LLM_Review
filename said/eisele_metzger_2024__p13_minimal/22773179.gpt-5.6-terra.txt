**Outcome assessed: uncorrected distance visual acuity at 3 months after LASIK (Figure 4; “efficacy”).** This is a paired-eye trial: each of 15 participants had one eye assigned to aspheric ablation (ASA) and the fellow eye to non-aspheric/tissue-saving ablation (TSA).

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
- The investigators state that, for each participant, one eye received ASA and the fellow eye TSA, “assigned at random using Microsoft Excel’s random number function.” This is consistent with a potentially random allocation sequence.  
- However, the report gives no details on how the Excel random-number function was implemented (e.g., generation of a prespecified allocation list, seed, rule for allocating right versus left eye), nor whether the sequence was generated before recruitment. Excel-based randomization can be adequate, but merely naming the software is not a full account of a robust randomization procedure.  
- Crucially, there is no description of **allocation concealment**. The person scheduling or performing surgery may have known which profile would be allocated to each eye before treatment. In a paired-eye surgical trial, predictable or unconcealed allocation could influence treatment timing, operative attention, or other perioperative decisions.  
- Baseline spherical-equivalent refraction was similar between allocated eyes, but this does not remedy the lack of information about concealment. Other prognostic characteristics relevant to visual acuity are not reported by treatment group.  

Therefore, randomization was claimed and baseline balance appears reasonable, but uncertainty about allocation concealment and the exact randomization implementation warrants **some concerns**.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
- The two allocated intervention profiles were clearly specified: ASA versus TSA using the same MEL80 laser, a 6-mm optical zone, and plano target refraction. The surgical platform, microkeratome, flap parameters, and follow-up schedule appear common to both eyes.  
- The surgeon could probably not be blinded to the ablation profile, and the report does not state whether patients or outcome assessors were blinded. Nevertheless, for the visual-acuity endpoint, awareness by the surgeon is unlikely by itself to cause important systematic deviations from the assigned laser profile.  
- There is no report of crossovers, use of an incorrect ablation profile, retreatment before the 3-month assessment, unequal cointerventions, or exclusion from analysis because of events after randomization.  
- Both groups are represented as containing 15 eyes, consistent with analysis of all allocated eyes.  

On the information available, there is no evidence of material deviations from intended interventions that would bias 3-month visual acuity. **Low risk**.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk**  
- The study enrolled 30 eyes from 15 patients, and the visual-acuity figure is reported to include 30 eyes at 3 months postoperatively. Thus, the available report indicates complete outcome data for the planned 3-month visual-acuity assessment.  
- The paper states that 100% of eyes in both groups achieved 20/25 or better, which is consistent with outcome data being available for all 15 ASA and all 15 TSA eyes.  
- No post-randomization exclusions, losses to follow-up, missing visual-acuity measurements, or reasons for missing data are reported.  

Although individual-level data are not supplied, there is no apparent missing-data problem at the reported time point. **Low risk**.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: some concerns**  
- Visual acuity is less subjective than participant-reported symptoms such as pain, glare, or satisfaction. However, Snellen visual-acuity measurement still depends on patient effort, testing conditions, chart design, and examiner procedures.  
- The manuscript says that uncorrected distance visual acuity and corrected distance visual acuity were measured before and 3 months after surgery, but does not describe the acuity-testing protocol: chart type and luminance, testing distance, monocular testing procedures, use of ETDRS/logMAR versus Snellen scoring, whether standard refraction was used where relevant, or whether measurements were repeated.  
- Most importantly, the report does not state that the visual-acuity examiner was masked to which eye had received ASA or TSA. Surgeon and possibly participant knowledge of the profile could also have influenced testing or encouragement, albeit likely modestly for this relatively objective endpoint.  
- The outcome is reported as a cumulative proportion meeting visual-acuity thresholds, rather than providing eye-level results and uncertainty estimates appropriate to the paired-eye design. This is mainly an analysis/reporting issue, but it limits confidence in the presented comparison.  

Because masking and standardized outcome measurement procedures are not reported, the risk of measurement bias for **visual acuity** is judged **some concerns**, rather than low risk.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
- No trial registration, dated protocol, or statistical analysis plan is cited or made available. Accordingly, it is impossible to verify whether visual acuity at 3 months, the selected threshold of 20/25, and the chosen presentation were specified before outcome data were known.  
- The study reports several visual and refractive outcomes, multiple aberration outcomes, and spherical aberration at many analysis diameters. There are also multiple ways of presenting visual acuity (mean logMAR acuity, proportions at 20/20 or 20/25, change in lines, efficacy index, or paired-eye differences). The report does not identify a prespecified primary visual-acuity analysis.  
- Figure 4 reports that both groups had 100% of eyes at 20/25 or better, but does not provide the between-profile effect estimate, paired analysis, confidence interval, or a formal comparison. This selective and threshold-based presentation could conceal potentially relevant differences in more granular acuity outcomes.  
- The manuscript’s stated principal purpose was comparison of spherical aberration, not visual acuity. Thus, the visual-acuity result appears supplementary and cannot be confirmed as prespecified.  

In the absence of protocol/registration evidence and with incomplete reporting of the comparative visual-acuity analysis, there are **some concerns** about selective selection and reporting of the result.

**6. Overall rating: some concerns**  
The overall risk of bias for the **visual-acuity outcome (Figure 4)** is **some concerns**. Randomization was claimed but allocation concealment was not described; masking and standardization of visual-acuity assessment were not reported; and no protocol or registration permits confirmation that the reported visual-acuity analysis was prespecified. There is, however, no evident problem with missing outcome data or deviations from the assigned interventions.
