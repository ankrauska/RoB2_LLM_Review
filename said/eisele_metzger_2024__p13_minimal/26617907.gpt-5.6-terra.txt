1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The report states that 90 participants were “divided into group A and group B (n=45) by using a random number table,” which is, in principle, a genuinely random sequence-generation method.  
   - However, no information is given about **allocation concealment**: who generated the random-number table, who enrolled participants, who assigned treatment, or whether assignments were protected (for example, through central randomization or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes). Thus, clinicians could potentially have foreseen upcoming assignments and influenced enrolment or allocation.  
   - The groups appear broadly comparable for the few reported baseline variables (sex, age, tumour size, and number of nodules). Nevertheless, these comparisons cannot establish that allocation was adequately concealed, and important prognostic variables—such as tumour location, liver-function measures within Child–Pugh A/B, cirrhosis severity, performance status, and vascular involvement—are not reported by group.  
   - Therefore, the stated random-number-table method is reassuring, but the absence of allocation-concealment information leaves **some concerns**.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The assigned interventions were materially different procedures: CT-guided percutaneous microwave ablation under local anaesthesia versus laparoscopic resection under general anaesthesia. Blinding of surgeons and participants was not feasible and is not claimed.  
   - For the endpoint of surgical time, lack of blinding itself is less important than it would be for a patient-reported endpoint, because surgical time is an objective procedural measure.  
   - However, the report provides no information on whether all randomized participants actually received their assigned procedure, whether any participants crossed over, underwent a different procedure after randomization, had an attempted procedure abandoned or converted, or were excluded from the surgical-time analysis. There is also no analysis explicitly stated to be according to randomized assignment.  
   - The procedures are described, but there is insufficient information about standardisation of the intervention, operators’ experience, or whether perioperative decisions that affect duration were prespecified and similarly managed between groups.  
   - Consequently, deviations affecting the comparison cannot be excluded, and the risk is judged **some concerns**.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk**  
   - Surgical time is an intraoperative outcome, ordinarily available for every participant who undergoes the procedure.  
   - Table 1 reports surgical-time results for all 45 participants in group A and all 45 in group B, exactly matching the numbers randomized to each group. No missing surgical-time values are apparent.  
   - The article does not provide a participant flow diagram or explicitly state that no participants were excluded after randomization. Nevertheless, for this immediate procedural endpoint, the complete denominators reported in Table 1 make substantial missing outcome data unlikely.  
   - There is no indication that missingness differed between groups or was related to surgical duration.  
   - Therefore, risk of bias from missing data for **surgical time** is **low-risk**.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — surgical time: low-risk**  
   - Surgical time is an objective outcome, reported in minutes. Unlike pain, satisfaction, or other participant-reported subjective outcomes, it should not be materially influenced by participant expectations or lack of participant blinding.  
   - Although the surgical team could not be blinded to the assigned procedure, the basic measurement—elapsed operative time—is relatively resistant to assessor judgement, provided it was recorded routinely.  
   - The manuscript does not define the start and end points for “surgical time” (for example, skin incision to closure, induction of anaesthesia to recovery, or procedure-room time). This is a limitation for interpretability and comparability, especially because the procedures used different anaesthetic approaches. It is not, however, strong evidence that measurement was differentially biased between groups.  
   - No evidence suggests that outcome assessors selectively altered recorded times or that recording methods differed systematically by group.  
   - Thus, for the endpoint **surgical time**, risk of bias in measurement is **low-risk**, albeit with inadequate reporting of the precise timing definition.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - No trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prespecified outcome hierarchy is provided. It is therefore impossible to verify whether surgical time was specified in advance, how it was intended to be defined, or whether alternative timing definitions or analyses were conducted but not reported.  
   - Surgical time is reported as a mean with standard deviation and compared using a t-test. However, there is no evidence that this analysis approach, handling of potentially skewed data, or any adjustment strategy was prespecified.  
   - The article reports several perioperative and longer-term outcomes, but does not clearly distinguish primary from secondary outcomes. The abstract and conclusions emphasise the apparent advantages of microwave ablation, creating some concern that favourable perioperative outcomes may have been selected for emphasis.  
   - In the absence of a protocol or registration record, selective reporting cannot be ruled out. Therefore, this domain is judged **some concerns**.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The endpoint has **some concerns** in multiple domains: allocation concealment in the randomization process is not reported; adherence to assigned intervention and possible crossover/exclusion are not documented; and no protocol or registration is available to assess selective reporting.  
   - Although missing surgical-time data appear complete and measurement of surgical time is objective, the accumulation of concerns across randomization, deviations from intended interventions, and selection of the reported result substantially lowers confidence in the estimated difference in surgical time.  
   - Overall, the result for **surgical time** is therefore at **high risk of bias**.
