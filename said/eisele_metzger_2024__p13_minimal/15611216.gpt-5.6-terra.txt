1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The authors state that participants were “randomly assigned” and that treatment/control *time blocks* of one week were randomized. This is not straightforward individual randomization: allocation appears to have been organized by calendar week to avoid contamination between residents.  
   - No information is provided on how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table), who generated it, or whether allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants or scheduling study weeks. The assertion of random assignment is therefore insufficient to establish that the allocation process was protected from manipulation or prediction.  
   - The groups were materially imbalanced at baseline on the prespecified, prognostically relevant construct of arousal imbalance: mean 107.4 versus 84.6 minutes/12 hours; *p* = .012. The intervention group also had higher mean baseline agitation (38.97 versus 32.59), although this difference was not conventionally statistically significant. Such imbalance can occur by chance in a small study, but it increases concern about the adequacy of the allocation process, particularly given the week-block allocation.  
   - The reported double-blinding does not remedy uncertainty about concealment at allocation. In particular, nursing staff necessarily had to know which residents were receiving altered activity schedules.  
   - Adjustment for baseline arousal imbalance and MMSE in the ANCOVA may reduce, but cannot fully eliminate, the consequences of baseline imbalance—especially if the model was selected after examining data.  

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: high risk**  
   - The intervention was inherently difficult to blind. Nursing staff implemented individualized changes to each participant’s activity schedule; thus, they knew who was receiving BACE. Staff behaviour, attention, interactions, and other aspects of care may consequently have differed between groups beyond the scheduled activity changes. The authors themselves acknowledge that observed differences could have resulted from “changes in quality of activity or interactions that occurred normally in the setting.”  
   - The control group received no comparable attention, assessment-driven care planning, or sham activity-schedule review. The contrast therefore comprises BACE plus potential additional staff attention and altered staff behaviour, rather than a clean contrast between the intended intervention and an equivalent control condition.  
   - Adherence was incomplete and variable. Of those retained in the intervention group, 72% received the full prescribed dose, 22% received 50–75%, and 6% received less than 50%. This degree of variation is particularly important because the intervention was individualized and could involve one to four activity-schedule changes.  
   - Most seriously, 10 participants were removed from the study because they were not given the intervention, due to short staffing, competing demands, or emergent care needs. Excluding randomized participants because the allocated intervention was not delivered is a per-protocol exclusion, not an intention-to-treat analysis. The reasons for non-delivery may be associated with agitation, clinical instability, or care circumstances, and thus with the outcome.  
   - The analysis does not include all randomized participants according to assigned group. Consequently, the reported effect estimates may be biased away from—or conceivably towards—the null.  

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
   - Of 102 participants who entered the study, only 78 were included in the final analysis: 12 died, 10 were excluded because no intervention was delivered, and 2 were excluded because of erroneous or missing data. Thus, approximately 24% of enrolled participants were absent from the analysed sample.  
   - The numbers and reasons for losses are reported overall, but not by randomized group. The report does not state whether deaths, data errors, or exclusions were balanced between treatment and control groups.  
   - There is no evidence that outcome data were available for nearly all randomized participants, nor is there an appropriate method for dealing with missing data (e.g., multiple imputation, sensitivity analyses, or analyses including all randomized participants).  
   - Exclusion of the 10 participants who did not receive any intervention is especially problematic: this is not merely missingness but outcome analysis conditional on post-randomization treatment receipt. Staffing shortages and emergent needs may plausibly be related to participants’ agitation or other determinants of posttest agitation.  
   - Deaths in a frail population with severe dementia may also be informative. The authors provide no indication of timing, group distribution, or whether deaths occurred before posttest assessment.  
   - Therefore, it is quite possible that the observed Group × Time result is biased by missing and selectively excluded outcome data.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — Agitation Score (Table 4): some concerns**  
   - Agitation was measured through direct observation every 15 minutes during a 12-hour pretest and posttest day. Direct contemporaneous observation is preferable to retrospective staff recall and is an appropriate broad approach for this population.  
   - The manuscript states that data collectors were blinded to treatment/control condition. If successfully maintained, this is an important safeguard against differential outcome assessment. Inter-rater reliability for agitation after training was reported as high (.95 to .984).  
   - Nevertheless, the outcome was assessed using a study-specific 0–100 visual analogue scale after the investigators could not achieve adequate reliability with their initially planned Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument. The replacement measure appears less well established: the report provides intensity parameters but does not provide independent validation, construct validity, responsiveness, or evidence that assessors could apply it without influence from contextual knowledge.  
   - The outcome is observer-rated and includes judgement about whether motor, vocal, or verbal activity is “excessive,” “nonpurposeful,” and not explained by the situation. It is therefore not fully objective.  
   - Although assessors were said to be blinded, the intervention deliberately altered participants’ daily schedules. Observers repeatedly present in care settings may have been able to infer allocation from changed activity patterns, quiet periods, or staff implementation of the individualized schedule. The paper provides no assessment of the success of blinding.  
   - The intervention may also change the context in which agitation is observed—for example, whether a participant is resting, socially engaged, or undertaking activity—which can make a global observer-rated agitation score difficult to disentangle from knowledge of the intervention context.  
   - Accordingly, there are concerns rather than a low-risk judgement, despite reported observer blinding and high inter-rater reliability.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - No trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospectively specified primary-outcome analysis is provided. It is therefore impossible to verify that the reported Table 4 Group × Time ANCOVA was the analysis planned before outcome data were examined.  
   - Agitation is clearly described as the principal outcome in the manuscript, which reduces concern that an entirely unannounced outcome has been selected. However, several analyses of agitation were undertaken: the main repeated-measures ANCOVA; paired within-group tests; analyses restricted to arousal-balance and arousal-imbalance periods; dose analyses; and three exploratory interaction models.  
   - The covariate strategy is not fully stable in the report. The main analysis adjusted for baseline arousal imbalance and MMSE; therapeutic activity sessions were subsequently added as a further covariate but were not significant. It is unclear whether these choices were prespecified or selected after inspection of results.  
   - The article gives slightly inconsistent *p* values for the main interaction: .043 in the abstract and Table 4, and .046 in the Results text. This may be a minor reporting discrepancy, but it is unhelpful when the result is just below the conventional .05 threshold.  
   - Without a protocol or registration record, selective choice among outcome definitions, time-period subsets, covariates, and analyses cannot be excluded.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall risk of bias for the **Agitation Score (Table 4)** is **high risk**. The decisive concerns are the exclusion of randomized participants who did not receive the intervention, substantial overall loss from enrolment to analysis without group-specific accounting or appropriate missing-data handling, and the absence of an intention-to-treat analysis. In addition, allocation concealment and the practical success of blinding are inadequately documented, while the unblinded delivery of an individualized care intervention creates substantial scope for deviations and co-interventions.
