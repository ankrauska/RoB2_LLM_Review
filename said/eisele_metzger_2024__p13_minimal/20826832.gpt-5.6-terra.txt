1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The study states that allocation was performed after baseline assessment by the coordination centre using a computerized random-number generator, with stratification/blocking by age (50–59 and 60–70 years). This is consistent with a genuinely random sequence-generation process.  
   - Allocation occurred after women had consented and completed baseline interviews, reducing the possibility that recruitment decisions were knowingly influenced by assigned group.  
   - However, the report does not describe the allocation-concealment mechanism in sufficient operational detail. It does not state who had access to the allocation sequence, whether the sequence was securely held by the coordinating centre, whether assignment was released only after irrevocable enrolment, or whether block sizes were concealed.  
   - Baseline demographic characteristics were reportedly similar across groups, which offers some reassurance, but this cannot itself establish adequate allocation concealment.  
   - Participants could not be blinded after allocation, as is expected for mailed and personal-contact interventions. This is not, by itself, evidence that randomization was compromised; the concern is the insufficiently described concealment procedure.  
   - **Judgement:** some concerns.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
   - Lack of participant and interventionist blinding was unavoidable because the interventions consisted of mailings, telephone contacts, and home visits. Participants’ knowledge of allocation is intrinsic to the interventions rather than a post-randomization deviation from them.  
   - The trial appears to have delivered the assigned strategies as intended: all women in the low-intensity and high-intensity groups received the mailed intervention; high-intensity participants who had not arranged mammography were intended to receive escalating telephone and/or home-contact efforts.  
   - The fact that only 50% of women in the high-intensity arm received telephone and/or home contact is not necessarily non-adherence: these contacts were deliberately conditional on not obtaining mammography after the mailing. Women who had already arranged or completed screening did not require the further component.  
   - Opportunistic advice was received by approximately 14%–15% of women and was similarly distributed across groups. Thus, differential co-intervention through usual clinical care does not appear substantial.  
   - Clinic and radiology staff were reportedly blinded to allocation, reducing the likelihood of differential access, encouragement, referral, or recording caused by providers’ knowledge of assignment.  
   - The primary analysis used the randomized baseline denominators and electronic records for all randomized women, described as intention-to-treat analysis. This appropriately estimates the effect of assignment to the intervention strategies, rather than only the effect among those who fully received a particular intervention component.  
   - Some cross-contamination between women cannot be excluded in this community setting, and the authors speculate that control women may have sought additional information. However, no evidence is presented of important differential contamination, and such contamination would more likely dilute than spuriously create the observed intervention effects.  
   - **Judgement:** low-risk.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk**  
   - Thirty-eight of 500 women (7.6%) did not complete the final survey. Attrition was broadly similar between groups: 6.6% in standard care, 7.8% in low intensity, and 8.4% in high intensity.  
   - Reasons for survey loss were reported: refusal to complete the final survey (20 women), inability to contact participants (17 women), and one death from cardiovascular disease. There is no indication that losses were strongly related to both intervention assignment and mammography completion.  
   - Crucially, the principal compliance outcome used for the intention-to-treat analysis was based on electronic clinical/radiology records reviewed for **all** randomized women, with the original group denominators retained. Therefore, loss to the follow-up survey did not result in missing data for the main electronically recorded outcome.  
   - The report does not explicitly establish whether women could have obtained mammography outside the affiliated service without its being captured in either electronic source. Nevertheless, the study population had free access through the clinic and the specified outcome was assessed using records intended to capture tests performed in the relevant service. There is no evidence that incompleteness differed by randomized group.  
   - **Judgement:** low-risk for the primary electronic-record outcome.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: assessed separately for each outcome measurement**

   **4a. Compliance with mammogram screening measured using electronic clinical/radiology records: low-risk**  
   - The main reported analysis was based on electronic records: radiology reports of tests performed at the affiliated radiology unit and electronic clinical charts recording test results.  
   - Mammography completion is a relatively objective, binary event. It is not a subjective symptom, attitude, or participant-reported perception particularly susceptible to expectation effects.  
   - Staff at the clinic and radiology unit were blinded to allocation group. Thus, knowledge of whether a woman was assigned to standard care, mail intervention, or mail-plus-contact intervention should not have influenced whether a completed mammogram was recorded.  
   - Investigators conducting the analysis at the coordinating centre were also blinded to group assignment.  
   - The principal residual limitation is that the article does not provide detailed validation of electronic-record completeness, particularly for mammograms potentially performed outside the affiliated system. However, there is no stated reason to expect any such recording failure to differ materially between groups.  
   - **Judgement:** low-risk.

   **4b. Compliance with mammogram screening measured by participant self-report at six months: high-risk**  
   - Participants were not blinded to their intervention group. Women receiving a physician letter, educational booklet, mammography order, telephone contact, and/or home visit would be especially aware that mammography completion was the desired behaviour.  
   - Self-reported screening can be affected by recall error and, particularly here, social-desirability bias. Participants in the active-intervention groups may have been more likely than controls to report that they had undergone mammography, whether or not this had occurred.  
   - The final survey was conducted by interviewers, and the report does not state that these interviewers were blinded to allocation. If interviewers were aware of group assignment, interviewer expectations could further influence questioning, probing, or recording.  
   - Although self-report was supplemented by electronic records, the risk of bias for the self-reported outcome measurement itself remains high because of unblinded participants and uncertain interviewer blinding.  
   - **Judgement:** high-risk.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The methods identify compliance with mammography screening as the main outcome, specify assessment at six months, and describe both self-report and electronic-record measurements. This provides some reassurance that the reported endpoint was not introduced only after inspection of results.  
   - The primary electronic-record analysis is described as intention-to-treat and uses baseline randomized denominators, an appropriate and pre-specified-sounding analytic approach.  
   - However, no trial registration number, publicly accessible protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospectively specified hierarchy between self-report and electronic-record outcomes is provided. It is therefore not possible to verify that the outcome definition, data source, time point, and statistical analysis were selected before outcome data became available.  
   - The article reports multiple possible outcome measurements—self-report and electronic records—and presents several beliefs, attitudes, and process variables. Without a protocol or analysis plan, selective emphasis on the most favourable measurement or analysis cannot be excluded.  
   - **Judgement:** some concerns.

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   - For the principal endpoint of mammography compliance measured using electronic records, there is low risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, and outcome measurement.  
   - Nevertheless, there are **some concerns** about allocation concealment because its operational details are not reported, and **some concerns** about selective reporting because no registration, protocol, or pre-specified analysis plan is available.  
   - The self-reported mammography outcome has high risk of measurement bias, but the main reported intention-to-treat effect is based on the more credible electronic-record outcome. Accordingly, the overall rating for the primary electronic-record endpoint is **some concerns**, rather than high risk.
