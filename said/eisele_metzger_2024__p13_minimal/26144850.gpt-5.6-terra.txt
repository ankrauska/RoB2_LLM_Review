1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The investigators state that 268 three-digit random numbers were selected from random-number tables in advance, recorded in patient registers, and that even numbers were assigned to PB-MDT and odd numbers to ROM-6. This is, in principle, a genuinely random sequence-generation method.  
   - However, allocation concealment is inadequately described. Writing the random numbers in the patient registers *in advance* creates a material possibility that recruiters or clinical staff could have foreseen the next allocation, particularly if they had access to the register before enrolment was irrevocably completed. There is no mention of sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, central allocation, or an independent allocator.  
   - Treatment was explicitly not blinded. Lack of blinding does not itself invalidate sequence generation, but it makes proper concealment at enrolment especially important and is relevant to later domains.  
   - The randomized groups appear broadly similar on reported age, sex, disease duration, number of lesions, and nerve involvement. Nevertheless, reporting of baseline smear status is markedly unequal because smears were not obtained in 29.8% of ROM participants versus 6.0% of MDT participants; this seems to result from differential consent or feasibility rather than randomization, but it does not inspire confidence in the completeness of baseline ascertainment.  
   - Thus, random sequence generation was probably adequate, but allocation concealment has not been established.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: high risk**  
   - This was an unblinded trial of substantially different treatment strategies: ROM-6 was entirely monthly supervised treatment, whereas PB-MDT included self-administered daily dapsone between supervised monthly visits. Consequently, adherence, expectations, management of symptoms, and decisions to discontinue could reasonably differ between groups.  
   - Treatment non-completion was substantial: 27/134 (20.1%) in the MDT group and 19/134 (14.2%) in the ROM-6 group defaulted. Most defaults occurred during the first two months. Reasons included drug intolerance/weakness, gastrointestinal symptoms, foot swelling, pregnancy, stigma, feeling cured, and being lost to treatment. Several of these reasons are plausibly related both to allocated treatment and to subsequent clinical outcome.  
   - The principal 2-year cure result is not analysed according to randomized allocation. It is explicitly reported “of those who completed treatment,” and the 2-year denominators are 100/134 for MDT and 103/134 for ROM-6. Thus, the reported comparison excludes a substantial proportion of randomized participants, particularly those who did not complete their assigned intervention.  
   - No appropriate intention-to-treat analysis is reported, and no analysis addresses the influence of differential adherence/defaulting on cure.  
   - The analysis therefore estimates outcome among a selected subset rather than the effect of assignment to the two regimens. This is a serious bias for the stated endpoint.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
   - At 2 years, cure status was available for only 100/134 (74.6%) randomized MDT participants and 103/134 (76.9%) randomized ROM-6 participants. Outcome data were consequently unavailable for about one quarter of participants in each group.  
   - The missing participants include many treatment defaulters, who were excluded from the principal cure analysis. Missingness is not plausibly unrelated to cure: patients defaulted because of adverse effects/intolerance, apparent improvement (“felt cured”), pregnancy, stigma, or inability to locate them. These reasons may be associated with both intervention group and disease status at 2 years.  
   - Although the authors later attempted to trace some defaulters, this does not resolve missing data at the prespecified 2-year assessment. Moreover, only 9/19 ROM defaulters and 23/27 MDT defaulters were traced at the end of the study, apparently at variable and much later times.  
   - No sensitivity analysis, worst-case analysis, imputation, or analysis including all randomized patients is presented.  
   - Given the amount of missing outcome data and its likely informative nature, the observed cure rates of 97% versus 99% could readily be misleading.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—cure rate at 2 years: high risk**  
   - Cure was based entirely on clinical assessment: complete resolution or flattening/hypopigmentation and reduced lesion size and/or regained sensation. The study acknowledges that diagnosis was purely clinical and that histology was not done.  
   - Assessment included potentially subjective elements: judgement of lesion flattening, infiltration, lesion size/change, activity, and sensory recovery. Sensation was assessed with a ball-point pen, but the manuscript gives no evidence of standardization, reproducibility testing, independent verification, or blinded adjudication.  
   - Participants, paramedical workers, and doctors were not blinded to allocated treatment. The authors explicitly state that blinding was not undertaken. Since ROM-6 and PB-MDT were visibly and administratively different regimens, treatment assignment would have been readily known during follow-up.  
   - Knowledge of treatment allocation could influence both participants’ reporting of improvement and assessors’ interpretation of subtle residual lesion activity or sensory recovery, especially because ROM was being evaluated as a potentially attractive, fully supervised alternative.  
   - Therefore, measurement of this clinically subjective outcome was susceptible to differential assessment bias.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high risk**  
   - The trial was registered only on 22 November 2013, whereas recruitment occurred from 2001 to 2004 and follow-up ended in 2010. This is retrospective registration, after enrolment and apparently after completion of follow-up. It cannot demonstrate that outcomes, time points, definitions, and analyses were prospectively specified.  
   - No protocol or prospectively registered statistical analysis plan is available in the manuscript.  
   - Cure is presented at multiple follow-up times—release from treatment, 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, 60, 61–96, and more than 96 months. The manuscript selectively emphasizes the apparently favourable 2-year result among those followed and/or completing treatment, rather than presenting a clearly defined randomized-population analysis at that time point.  
   - There are inconsistencies and ambiguities in denominators and summaries. For example, the abstract describes cure rates “of those who completed treatment,” yet the 2-year denominators are lower than the number reported as completing treatment, reflecting further losses. The “overall cure rates” are also based on selected numbers (104 and 108), rather than all 134 randomized in each arm.  
   - In the absence of a prospective protocol, with numerous possible assessment times and analysis populations, and with the reported estimate based on completers/available cases, there is a high risk that the reported 2-year cure comparison was selected from multiple possible analyses.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall risk of bias for **cure rate at 2 years** is **high risk**. The principal reasons are the per-protocol/available-case analysis despite substantial and potentially informative non-completion and loss to follow-up, unblinded subjective clinical outcome assessment, and retrospective trial registration with no accessible prospective protocol or analysis plan. These problems substantially lower confidence in the reported 97% versus 99% cure rates.
