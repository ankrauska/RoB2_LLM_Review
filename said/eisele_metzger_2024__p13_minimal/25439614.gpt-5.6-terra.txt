1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns** (The report describes random assignment of 180 eyes to ranibizumab plus prompt laser and 181 eyes to ranibizumab plus deferred laser, which is reassuring, and baseline characteristics among randomized groups appear broadly similar. However, this manuscript does not describe the method used to generate the random sequence or, more importantly, how allocation was concealed before enrolment/assignment. A claim of “random assignment” alone is insufficient to exclude predictable or manipulable allocation. In addition, 14 participants from one site were excluded from all efficacy analyses after central reassessment of baseline OCT eligibility. Although this exclusion appears unrelated to knowledge of 5-year visual-acuity results, exclusion after randomization is undesirable and the effects of this exclusion by allocated group are not presented. Thus, there is insufficient information to judge the randomization process as unequivocally protected from bias.)

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk** (The intended comparison was a treatment-policy strategy: ranibizumab with focal/grid laser given promptly versus ranibizumab with laser deferred for at least 24 weeks and subsequently given only according to prespecified persistence/non-improvement criteria. The protocol specified frequent follow-up and retreatment criteria based on visual acuity, OCT findings, and previous treatment. The observed difference in numbers of ranibizumab injections—median 13 in the prompt-laser group versus 17 in the deferred-laser group—is an expected consequence of the assigned management strategies, rather than necessarily a deviation from them. Similarly, 44% of the deferred group eventually received laser, as permitted by the deferred-laser strategy; this is not contamination in an analysis of the assigned strategies. Treating clinicians and participants were probably aware of whether laser had been given, but visual-acuity examiners were masked. There is little evidence of important non-protocol treatment crossover: other DME treatments occurred in 9 prompt-group completers, seven under protocol-defined futility/failure criteria and two outside protocol, and none in the deferred group. These few departures are unlikely materially to bias the visual-acuity comparison.)

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk** (Missing 5-year visual-acuity data were substantial. Excluding deaths, only 76% of the original prompt-laser group and 74% of the original deferred-laser group completed the 5-year visit; thus approximately one quarter of randomized eyes had no 5-year outcome measurement. The principal reason was non-consent to the 2-year extension, so missingness was not merely an unavoidable administrative loss. Although completion rates were similar between groups, the missingness cannot safely be assumed unrelated to unobserved visual acuity: the authors explicitly acknowledge that this is unknown. Completers and non-completers differed in prognostic characteristics, including age, prior panretinal photocoagulation, lens status, and diabetic-retinopathy severity. The analysis used available-data longitudinal models and adjusted for measured differences, but this relies on assumptions—particularly that data are missing at random conditional on observed covariates—that are not demonstrated. No persuasive sensitivity analysis for informative missingness is reported. Because the 5-year endpoint is missing for about a quarter of randomized eyes and missingness may be related to prognosis and possibly outcome, the risk of bias is high.)

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—visual acuity: low risk** (Visual acuity was measured as best-corrected E-ETDRS letter score, a standardized and objective functional assessment. The investigators state that visual-acuity examiners were masked to treatment assignment. This is especially important because participants and treating clinicians could likely infer allocation from prompt versus absent/deferred laser treatment. Masking of the examiner, together with use of a standardized letter-score procedure, makes differential outcome assessment unlikely. Although patient effort can affect a visual-acuity test, there is no indication that the measurement method, testing conditions, or assessment schedule differed between groups in a way likely to bias the result. Therefore, for visual acuity specifically, outcome measurement is at low risk of bias.)

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns** (The manuscript identifies best-corrected visual acuity at 5 years as the main outcome and reports the principal continuous comparison—mean change in letter score from baseline—as well as categorical visual-acuity outcomes. A protocol was reportedly available on the DRCR.net website, which is favourable. However, the supplied material does not provide the protocol’s prespecified 5-year analysis plan or a statistical analysis plan sufficiently detailed to verify that the exact analysis reported was selected in advance. There were several plausible analytic choices: longitudinal available-data models, analyses restricted to 5-year completers, adjustment for baseline visual acuity alone or for multiple additional variables, visual-acuity truncation at ±30 letters, and multiple categorical thresholds and subgroup analyses. The headline continuous result is reported for both partially and fully adjusted models, but the statistically significant categorical results and subgroup findings receive prominent emphasis. In the absence of a prospectively specified 5-year analytic hierarchy and complete outcome-reporting plan, selective choice among analyses cannot be excluded. This warrants some concerns, not high risk, because the principal continuous result and its confidence interval are reported transparently.)

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias** (The visual-acuity result is at **high risk of bias** because the 5-year endpoint is missing for approximately one quarter of randomized eyes, largely owing to non-participation in the extension phase, with observed differences between completers and non-completers and no adequate demonstration that missing outcomes would not alter the comparison. There are also some concerns regarding allocation concealment/randomization details and selection of the reported analysis. Masked standardized visual-acuity measurement is a substantial strength, but it does not remedy the serious potential bias from missing 5-year outcome data.)
