**Endpoint assessed: muscle damage**, operationalised principally by serum **creatine kinase (CK)** and **aspartate aminotransferase (AST)** in the allopurinol-versus-placebo intervention phase (period B).

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The authors state that crew members were allocated “according to random numbers,” which is compatible with a genuinely random sequence. However, they provide no details on how the random-number sequence was generated, who generated it, or whether it was implemented correctly.  
   - Crucially, **allocation concealment is not described**. The report says that sailors were blinded to group assignment, but does not state whether the person enrolling sailors and assigning treatment could foresee the next allocation. Participant blinding is not equivalent to adequate concealment before assignment.  
   - The reported post-allocation groups were materially imbalanced in sailing role and therefore likely exercise exposure: the allopurinol group included 8 high-intensity workers among 13 sailors, whereas the placebo group included only 2 high-intensity workers among 10 sailors. Such imbalance can occur by chance in a very small trial, but it is concerning because role intensity is strongly related to CK and AST levels in this crew.  
   - Baseline CK was also considerably higher in the allopurinol group (445.8 U/L) than in the placebo group (275.8 U/L). No adjusted analysis was undertaken. This does not prove faulty randomization, but it increases concern that the groups were not adequately comparable at baseline.  
   - Therefore, the random sequence is asserted but insufficiently documented, concealment is unknown, and important baseline/exposure imbalance exists.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - Sailors were reportedly blinded, and the comparison group received placebo. This is a useful safeguard against differential behaviour by participants, particularly for an intervention taken before racing.  
   - However, the report does not state whether coaches, team managers, those administering medication, race-selection personnel, laboratory staff, or investigators were blinded. Knowledge of assignment by staff could influence training, workload assignment, inclusion in races, co-interventions, or encouragement.  
   - There is no information on adherence: no record of tablets dispensed, tablets taken, timing actually achieved, vomiting/adverse effects, or biochemical confirmation of xanthine-oxidase inhibition. The intended regimen was 300 mg/day of allopurinol 3 hours before racing, but actual receipt of that regimen is not demonstrated.  
   - More importantly, the groups had very different distributions of high-intensity sailing jobs. Since physical work intensity is a major determinant of CK and AST, any differential racing exposure during period B could obscure or mimic a treatment effect. The authors do not report the number of races, time on board, specific tasks, training load, or substitutions for each treatment group.  
   - The reported analysis compares pre- and post-race values **within each group**, rather than estimating the randomized between-group difference in change. This analytical approach does not adequately protect against baseline imbalance or differences in exercise exposure.  
   - Thus, although participant blinding and placebo use are reassuring, adherence, co-interventions, staff blinding, comparable exposure, and an appropriate effect estimate are insufficiently addressed.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - Four sailors “finally declined to take part” in period B. The report does not make clear whether these withdrawals occurred before or after random allocation, which treatment group(s) they had been assigned to, why they declined, or whether any pre- or post-race blood results were available.  
   - The final reported sample sizes are 13 in the allopurinol group and 10 in the placebo group (23 total), consistent with four of 27 crew members not contributing to the intervention analysis. However, the handling of these four individuals is not transparently reported.  
   - No participant flow diagram, denominators at each stage, reasons for non-participation by group, or analysis based on all randomized participants is presented.  
   - Among the analysed participants, the text appears to provide pre- and post-race CK and AST group results, but individual-level completeness cannot be verified from the paper.  
   - In period A, one participant with exceptionally high CK values was excluded from calculations after observing the results. Although this exclusion is described in the non-intervention phase rather than explicitly in period B, it demonstrates a willingness to exclude outcome data post hoc and heightens concern about outcome-data handling.  
   - The amount of apparently unavailable period-B data is modest, but its distribution and relation to treatment and outcome are unknown. Therefore, the risk is judged as some concerns rather than low risk.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low risk for CK; low risk for AST**  

   **4a. Serum creatine kinase (CK): low risk**  
   - CK is an objective laboratory biomarker, measured spectrophotometrically using commercially available reagents on an automated analyser.  
   - The reported intra-assay coefficient of variation was 4.5%, suggesting reasonable analytical precision.  
   - Measurement is unlikely to be materially influenced by a sailor’s awareness of treatment assignment; unlike pain or perceived exertion, CK is not participant-reported or inherently subjective.  
   - The timing of sampling—3 hours after racing—is potentially problematic for interpreting the biological meaning of CK, since CK may peak later after exercise. This is a limitation of outcome timing and construct validity, but it should affect both randomized groups similarly and is not, by itself, strong evidence of differential measurement bias.  
   - Laboratory-staff blinding is not reported. Nevertheless, because CK measurement was automated and objective, knowledge of allocation is unlikely to have materially altered the measured value.  

   **4b. Serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST): low risk**  
   - AST was likewise measured objectively by spectrophotometric assay on an automated analyser, with an intra-assay coefficient of variation of 4.2%.  
   - Participant blinding is not essential for the validity of this laboratory measurement, and absence of reported laboratory blinding is unlikely to have substantially influenced an automated enzyme result.  
   - AST is a non-specific marker: it may reflect hepatic, cardiac, or skeletal-muscle sources and is therefore a less specific indicator of skeletal muscle damage than CK. This is an important limitation in interpretation of the endpoint, but not necessarily a bias in the measurement process itself.  
   - The same concerns about sampling timing apply, but there is no indication that timing differed between randomized groups.  

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high risk**  
   - No trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospectively specified primary outcome is provided. Consequently, it is impossible to determine whether CK, AST, MDA, time points, subgroup analyses, and statistical comparisons were specified before the data were examined.  
   - The study reports numerous biomarkers (CK, AST, and MDA), multiple time points, two racing periods, several participant subgroups based on work intensity, correlations, and several alternative comparisons. This creates substantial scope for selective choice of favourable analyses.  
   - The main claimed intervention effect is based on a statistically significant **within-allopurinol-group** decrease in CK (445.8 to 333.6 U/L; p < 0.05), while the placebo group did not show a statistically significant within-group change. The appropriate randomized comparison is the **between-group difference in change**, which is not reported. “Significant in one group but not significant in the other” is not evidence that the treatment effect differs between groups.  
   - AST showed no apparent treatment effect, and MDA decreased non-significantly. Nevertheless, the discussion emphasises that allopurinol “was proven to be effective” and that CK is the “most sensitive” marker. This selective emphasis on the favourable CK finding, without a prespecified hierarchy or appropriate between-group analysis, is particularly concerning.  
   - The post hoc exclusion of an individual with extremely high CK values in period A further raises concern about data-dependent analytic decisions.  
   - Accordingly, there is a high risk that the reported result was selected from multiple available outcomes, time points, analyses, and exclusions.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The overall rating is **high risk of bias**. The principal reason is **high risk of bias in selection of the reported result**: no prospective protocol or registration is available; multiple outcomes and analyses were available; and the claimed benefit relies on an inappropriate within-group significance comparison rather than a randomized between-group estimate.  
   - In addition, there are some concerns regarding allocation concealment and baseline imbalance, deviations from intended intervention/exercise exposure, and missing outcome data. These limitations further reduce confidence that the observed fall in CK was caused by allopurinol rather than baseline differences, workload differences, regression to the mean, selective analysis, or chance.
