# Risk of Bias Assessment: Inhaler Use Technique Outcome

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns.** The manuscript states subjects were "randomly assigned" but provides no detail on the randomization method (e.g., random number generation, block randomization), nor any description of allocation concealment procedures. There is no information on who generated the allocation sequence or who enrolled participants versus who assigned them to groups. Baseline characteristics (Table 1) show substantial imbalance in age and education by ethnicity (noted explicitly in the Discussion as post-hoc explanation for differential improvement), raising suspicion that randomization may not have adequately balanced these prognostic factors, or that ethnicity/age were not accounted for in the randomization scheme. The protocol registration only states "allocation: randomized" without further methodological detail. Given the absence of any concealment description and the observed baseline imbalances used post-hoc to explain differential results, I have some concerns here.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns.** This was a single-participant-blinded trial per the registry (participants were not aware of comparator group content), but this is behaviorally impossible to fully conceal—patients obviously knew whether they watched a video or read a pamphlet. There is no ITT analysis described; the manuscript reports that 85/92 completed the study, and analyses appear to be per-protocol (only completers analyzed, "df = 1 and 71" suggests exclusion of dropouts/missing data rather than imputation for randomized participants). No mention of adherence checks (i.e., confirming participants actually watched the full video or read the pamphlet fully) or contamination between groups (participants may have discussed materials within community). No described handling of protocol deviations.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Some concerns.** Of 92 enrolled, 85 completed the study (7 dropped, ~7.6%), per Figure 1, with "3 excluded and 4 discontinued." There is no evidence of a reasons-for-dropout analysis, no comparison of baseline characteristics between completers and non-completers, and no sensitivity analysis (e.g., worst-case scenario or multiple imputation) to assess whether missingness was related to the outcome (inhaler technique). The degrees of freedom in statistical tests (df = 71, 76) indicate a shifting denominator across analyses, suggesting inconsistent handling of missing data across different outcome measures without clear explanation.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: Inhaler Use Technique
**High risk.** This outcome was assessed via direct observation using a 9-step checklist administered by "community facilitators" and a "research coordinator," who were described as blinded to "study hypothesis" but were NOT blinded to group allocation — the facilitators conducted the intervention (showing videos/pamphlets) AND then assessed the inhaler technique in the same visit. This creates a substantial risk of assessor detection bias, as the assessors necessarily knew which intervention the participant received. Although this is an observer-rated (not self-reported) outcome, and thus different from purely patient-reported outcomes like pain, it remains a semi-subjective checklist scored by a non-blinded assessor, who could be influenced (consciously or unconsciously) by knowledge of group assignment, especially given the study's hypothesis that videos would outperform pamphlets. No description of a blinded second-rater adjudication process, nor of inter-rater reliability statistics between the "two observers" mentioned in the Discussion. This is a critical flaw for this specific outcome.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**High risk.** The registered primary outcome per ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01474928) is "medication adherence" (described specifically as "patients' understanding of physicians' instructions"), yet the published manuscript treats multiple outcomes (inhaler technique, symptom knowledge, trigger knowledge, understanding physician instructions) as co-equal primary results, without pre-specifying inhaler technique as a primary/secondary outcome in the registration. There is no evidence of a published, timestamped analysis plan detailing exactly which comparisons (pairwise group comparisons, subgroups by ethnicity/gender/age/education) would be conducted for the inhaler technique outcome. Multiple subgroup analyses (by gender, ethnicity, age, education) are reported post-hoc with selective emphasis on statistically significant findings (e.g., "Chinese patients showed significantly greater improvement…females showed greater improvement…") without correction for multiple comparisons, suggestic strong potential for selective reporting/outcome-hunting. The registration's completion date (2014) versus publication (2012 in J Asthma per reference list) also raises inconsistency questions regarding what was pre-specified before data collection was complete.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk of bias**

**Justification:** While randomization and missing data domains raise "some concerns," the outcome measurement domain for Inhaler Use Technique is rated high risk due to non-blinded assessors directly involved in delivering the intervention and then scoring the outcome — a critical source of detection bias in a trial explicitly hypothesizing video superiority. Combined with high risk of selective outcome reporting (this outcome is not clearly the pre-registered primary outcome and multiple subgroup comparisons appear analyzed post-hoc without control for multiplicity), the cumulative effect substantially undermines confidence in the reported effect for this specific outcome.
