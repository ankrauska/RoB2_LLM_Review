**Endpoint assessed: participant-reported pain on the 0–10 visual analogue scale (VAS), post-treatment.**  
The relevant comparison is among ultrasound, dexketoprofen phonophoresis, and dexketoprofen iontophoresis after 20 treatment sessions.

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
- A random component is described: a statistician generated a numbered allocation list using random permuted blocks. This is an acceptable method of sequence generation, and the statistician was reportedly independent of data collection and analysis.  
- However, the report does not explain how allocation was concealed from the personnel enrolling participants and assigning treatment (for example, central randomisation, a secure electronic system, or consecutively numbered opaque sealed envelopes). A numbered list alone does not establish concealment; it may be foreseeable or accessible to recruiters/treating staff.  
- Baseline VAS means were reasonably similar (5.7, 5.7 and 6.3), and other reported baseline characteristics were broadly balanced. This is reassuring but cannot remedy inadequate reporting of allocation concealment.  
- The registry describes participant masking, whereas the paper calls the trial “single-blind” and specifically states that the rehabilitation doctor performing assessments was blinded. These descriptions do not clearly establish that allocation was concealed at enrolment or that participant blinding was feasible.  
- Therefore, random sequence generation appears adequate, but the absence of a clear allocation-concealment procedure leaves **some concerns**.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
- The interventions were delivered according to explicit standardised protocols. All groups received the same co-interventions—standardised exercises and cryotherapy—and the intended physical-agent treatment differed by randomised group.  
- The authors state that all participants completed 20 sessions, and there is no reported evidence of treatment crossover, differential discontinuation, non-adherence, or additional home-exercise instructions. This is favourable.  
- Nonetheless, credible participant blinding is doubtful. Ultrasound/phonophoresis involves ultrasound application for 5 minutes, whereas iontophoresis involves direct electrical current for 20 minutes. Participants are likely able to distinguish these procedures through their duration and sensations. The paper does not describe sham procedures, identical treatment duration, concealment of device settings, or any assessment of the success of masking.  
- For a subjective pain outcome, awareness of intervention can influence expectations, reporting of pain, treatment-seeking, and engagement with the shared exercise programme. The report does not document whether such deviations occurred differentially between groups, nor does it describe monitoring of concomitant analgesic use or activities during treatment. Indeed, the authors acknowledge that participants’ daily activities were not monitored.  
- There is insufficient evidence to conclude that deviations caused important bias, but the practical failure—or at least uncertainty—of participant blinding creates **some concerns**.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
- The trial enrolled 99 participants, with 32, 33 and 34 allocated to the three groups. The authors state that all participants completed 20 treatment sessions.  
- However, completion of treatment sessions is not the same as complete availability of post-treatment VAS data. The manuscript does not provide a participant flow diagram in the supplied text, numbers analysed for VAS at each time point, numbers with missing post-treatment measurements, reasons for any missing data, or whether the analysis included all randomised participants.  
- No imputation strategy, sensitivity analysis, or explicit intention-to-treat analysis is reported.  
- The reported group means and standard deviations may imply near-complete data, but this cannot safely be assumed. In a small three-arm study, even a small amount of differential missing subjective pain data could influence results.  
- Thus, there is no direct evidence of substantial missing post-treatment VAS data, but reporting is inadequate to establish a low risk. **Some concerns** are warranted.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (pain VAS post-treatment): high risk**  
- The VAS is a participant-reported, inherently subjective pain outcome. It is particularly vulnerable to expectation and performance effects if participants know, or can infer, the intervention received.  
- A blinded rehabilitation doctor conducted the visits using standardised protocols. Blinding of the doctor is useful, but it does not adequately protect a self-reported pain score from bias arising from the participant’s knowledge and expectations.  
- The registry states that participants were masked, but the manuscript gives no credible account of how this was achieved. The interventions are conspicuously different: ultrasound and phonophoresis use an ultrasound device for 5 minutes, whereas iontophoresis uses direct electrical current for 20 minutes. No sham ultrasound, sham current, matched procedure duration, or masking assessment is described.  
- The manuscript’s statement that the trial was “single-blind” is ambiguous, but its explicit description of a blinded outcome doctor suggests that this may have been assessor blinding rather than reliable participant blinding. The registry and manuscript also provide inconsistent or insufficient masking detail.  
- Because post-treatment pain reporting could plausibly be materially affected by participants’ awareness of treatment assignment, and because the reported masking is not credible or adequately documented, measurement of **post-treatment VAS pain is at high risk of bias**.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
- The ClinicalTrials.gov record identifies VAS pain as the primary outcome and DASH and Constant scores as secondary outcomes. The manuscript reports VAS at baseline, post-treatment and 1 month post-treatment, broadly consistent with those registered outcome domains.  
- However, the supplied registry information does not specify outcome time points, a detailed statistical analysis plan, the precise primary contrast among the three groups, adjustment methods, or the intended handling of multiple comparisons.  
- The paper reports numerous analyses: within-group changes, between-group comparisons, three outcomes, and multiple time points. For the VAS, the post-treatment ultrasound-versus-iontophoresis contrast is statistically significant, whereas no difference remains at one month. Without a prespecified analysis plan, it is not possible to exclude selective emphasis on particular time points, comparisons, or analyses.  
- The abstract and conclusion make broad claims that all treatments improve outcomes, despite the principal comparative question being which treatment is more effective. This increases concern about interpretive and reporting flexibility, although it does not prove selective non-reporting.  
- Accordingly, the match between the registered primary outcome and reported VAS outcome is reassuring, but the lack of prespecified time-point and analysis details leaves **some concerns**.

**6. Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
The overall rating is **high risk of bias** for post-treatment VAS pain. The decisive problem is the **high risk of bias in measurement of this subjective participant-reported outcome**, because participant blinding was either implausible or inadequately documented despite materially different procedures. This concern is compounded by uncertainty about allocation concealment, missing outcome-data handling, deviations associated with likely treatment awareness, and absence of a sufficiently detailed prespecified analysis plan.
