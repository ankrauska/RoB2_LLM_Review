1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   The report states that allocation was “random” and was conducted confidentially by clinic administration, with medication supplied in numbered boxes. Both participants and clinicians were said to be blinded, and matching placebo was used. These features are reassuring and make overt manipulation of allocation less likely.  
   
   However, the random-sequence generation method is not described: there is no account of a computer-generated list, random-number table, block randomization, or other genuinely random procedure. Nor is it clear precisely how allocation concealment was maintained before enrolment and assignment. The planned allocation was 25 active versus 24 placebo participants, whereas the eventual randomized numbers were 25 versus 24, but six placebo participants were subsequently excluded. Baseline characteristics among the analysed participants appear broadly comparable, but this cannot remedy the inadequate description of sequence generation and concealment. Therefore, there are **some concerns**.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
   The trial was described as double-blind, with matching placebo and blinded numbered medication boxes. Participants and clinicians were reportedly unaware of treatment assignment. Olanzapine doses and concomitant medication were stated to have remained constant during follow-up, which is important because changes in antipsychotic exposure or co-medication could materially affect body weight.  
   
   Although adherence to topiramate/placebo is not quantified and topiramate-related symptoms (for example paresthesia) could potentially have compromised blinding in some participants, the manuscript reports only isolated mild symptoms and provides no evidence of systematic unblinding, treatment switching, differential co-intervention, or departures from the allocated regimen. The principal problem is not a documented deviation from treatment but the exclusion of participants with missing measurements; that is addressed under missing outcome data. For the effect of assignment to intervention, this domain is judged **low-risk**.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high-risk**  
   This is the most serious defect in the trial. Of 49 randomized women, six (12.2%) failed to attend more than one assessment and were excluded entirely from analysis. Crucially, **all six exclusions occurred in the placebo group**. Thus, analysed data were available for all 25 topiramate participants but only 18 of the 24 placebo participants.  
   
   The investigators performed a complete-case analysis: “their data were not further analyzed.” There was no intention-to-treat analysis, no imputation, no sensitivity analysis, and no reporting of the weights or reasons for withdrawal of the six placebo-assigned women beyond non-attendance. Since missingness is entirely differential by randomized group and the outcome is weight change, it is entirely plausible that the missing placebo participants had outcomes different from the observed placebo participants. Excluding them may exaggerate or otherwise distort the apparent advantage of topiramate. The reported “response rate” of 87.8% is misleading insofar as it obscures the complete concentration of attrition in one arm. This domain is **high-risk**.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — weight: low-risk**  
   **Outcome assessed:** body weight, measured repeatedly every two weeks and at week 10.  
   
   Weight is an objective outcome, considerably less vulnerable than subjective outcomes to participants’ expectations or assessor judgement. The study describes both clinicians and participants as blinded, and the medication/placebo arrangement was intended to preserve blinding. Although the manuscript does not specify the scale used, calibration, clothing conditions, fasting status, or whether the same staff measured all participants, these omissions are unlikely by themselves to create a differential measurement error large enough to account for the reported difference in weight change.  
   
   The fact that topiramate can cause recognizable adverse effects creates a theoretical possibility of unblinding, but only isolated mild symptoms were reported and there is no evidence that weight assessors became aware of allocation or measured weight differently between groups. Accordingly, measurement of **weight** is judged **low-risk**. This judgement concerns measurement only; it does not mitigate the high risk arising from differential missing weight data.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   The manuscript identifies “weight checks” as a primary outcome measure, which provides some support that weight was prospectively intended as a principal endpoint. Weight findings are reported, including final group values, change values, confidence intervals, and a P value. The authors also state that intermediate results were not analysed during the trial.  
   
   Nevertheless, no prospectively dated trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prespecified primary time point is available. The study collected weight at multiple two-week intervals and also assessed numerous questionnaire domains repeatedly. The analysis is inadequately specified: it is unclear whether the reported Mann–Whitney tests compared individual change scores, final values, another summary measure, or whether the analysis was predefined. The presentation selectively emphasizes highly significant results, while complete outcome data and analyses for all randomized participants are absent. For the weight endpoint, there is insufficient information to rule out selection among time points, analyses, or population definitions. This domain is therefore **some concerns**.

6. **Overall rating: high-risk**  
   The overall risk of bias for the effect of topiramate on **weight** is **high-risk**. The decisive reason is the handling of missing outcome data: six randomized participants were excluded, all from the placebo group, with no intention-to-treat analysis or credible demonstration that their missing weight outcomes could not have biased the treatment estimate. In addition, there are some concerns about the inadequately described randomization process and absence of protocol/registration information to verify prespecified analyses.
