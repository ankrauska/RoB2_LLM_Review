# Risk of Bias Assessment: Pain VAS Outcome

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns.** The manuscript states "simple random selection" in the abstract but then describes "non-probabilistic sampling" combined with "sex-stratified randomization" in the Methods — these statements are internally contradictory and raise doubt about whether true random sequence generation was used. Allocation concealment was reportedly achieved via sealed envelopes, which is reassuring. However, baseline VAS scores differ somewhat (7.0 vs 7.4, though not statistically significant), and several joint mobility baseline variables show borderline/non-significant imbalances (e.g., extension p=0.054, left side bending p=0.050, right side bending p=0.060) that the authors themselves acknowledge as "differences...observed" but dismiss without robust justification. The lack of clarity on the actual randomization method (simple vs. stratified, no description of random number generation) and the borderline baseline imbalances raise some concern, though not enough to be classified as high risk given the concealment method described.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns.** Patients and the outcome assessor were blinded, which is a strength. However, the physiotherapist administering treatment could not be blinded ("The blinding of the physiotherapist who applied the treatment was not possible"), which the authors acknowledge as a limitation. Given that treatment fidelity/adherence to protocol could differ subtly between an unblinded therapist delivering active vs. sham ultrasound (e.g., verbal cues, encouragement, extraneous manual therapy elements), there is a realistic risk of deviation from the intended intervention that could affect the patient-reported VAS pain outcome. This is a per-protocol vs ITT concern is minimal (analysis was ITT and no dropouts), but the unblinded treating therapist remains a source of concern for performance bias affecting a subjective outcome.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Low risk.** The manuscript explicitly states "During the study no patient was lost or dropped out," and the flowchart confirms 27/27 patients were retained and analyzed in each group throughout all time points (pre-test, session 6, session 11, discharge). Analysis was by intention-to-treat with complete data. There is no indication of missing VAS data at any assessment point.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — VAS Pain
**Some concerns.** VAS pain is a patient-reported subjective outcome. While patients were reportedly blinded to allocation (active vs. sham ultrasound), and the outcome assessor was also blinded, the treating physiotherapist was NOT blinded (acknowledged limitation). Since this physiotherapist interacts directly with patients during 25 treatment sessions, there is a realistic risk that non-verbal cues, differential encouragement, or inadvertent unblinding of patients could have occurred, given the long treatment duration and close patient-therapist contact. Additionally, no formal check of the success of patient blinding (e.g., asking patients to guess their allocation) is reported, so we cannot be confident blinding was maintained in practice. This raises legitimate concern for a subjective, self-reported outcome like VAS pain.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Some concerns.** No trial registration or published protocol is referenced/cited anywhere in the manuscript, making it impossible to verify whether the outcome (VAS pain measured at 4 time points: pre-test, session 6, session 11, discharge) and the specific statistical comparisons reported (unilateral/one-tailed significance tests) were pre-specified. The use of one-tailed ("unilateral") significance testing for the improvement index (Tables 2 and 3) is unusual and not justified in the Methods, raising suspicion of possible post-hoc selection to achieve significance. Additionally, multiple outcome variables (VAS, algometer x4 sites, 6 mobility measures) were tested at multiple time points without correction for multiple comparisons, and the narrative selectively emphasizes the "significant" VAS finding at discharge while downplaying non-significant mobility findings. Without access to a pre-registered protocol, selective outcome reporting cannot be excluded.

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** While no single domain was rated "high risk" outright, there are "some concerns" in four of the five domains (randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, measurement of outcome, and selection of the reported result). This pattern of concerns is particularly concerning because they compound each other for this specific subjective outcome: an unblinded treating therapist (domain 2) directly threatens the integrity of a patient-reported pain measure (domain 4); ambiguous randomization description undermines confidence that baseline differences are due to chance (domain 1); and the use of unconventional one-tailed testing without a visible pre-specified protocol raises credible suspicion of outcome-reporting flexibility (domain 5). Collectively, these multiple concerns substantially lower confidence in the reported VAS pain result, meeting the criteria for "high risk" per the stated overall-rating rules.
