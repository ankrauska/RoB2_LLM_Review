# Bias Assessment: Patient Acceptance of the Appliance (Primary Outcome)

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Low risk**

Justification: The randomization sequence was computer-generated using an Excel-based random number function with permuted block sizes (6 and 4) with randomized order, which constitutes an adequate random sequence generation method. Allocation concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes concealed from researchers and assessors. Baseline characteristics (Table I) appear generally balanced between groups, though this was not entirely detailed with p-values in the article, this is a minor concern. No evidence of subversion of allocation is apparent. This domain is well-executed.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns**

Justification: This trial explicitly acknowledges that blinding of clinicians and patients to the intervention was impossible — patients could feel/see whether they had mini-implants inserted. While this is inherent to the intervention type and unavoidable, it raises the potential for performance bias since patients' expectations, reporting behavior, and possibly clinician management of the appliance might differ based on knowledge of group assignment. There is no mention of a formal analysis of whether deviations occurred (e.g. differential care, additional reassurance, or differential handling of the FMI group due to the novel mini-implant procedure) that could have influenced the primary outcome of patient acceptance. No adjustment for this deviation (e.g., per-protocol vs ITT comparison) is discussed beyond stating ITT was used because there were no dropouts.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Low risk**

Justification: All 32 randomized patients completed the trial with no dropouts. The CONSORT flow diagram confirms 16/16 patients analyzed per group, and it is explicitly stated that "all questions were answered correctly by the patients and their parents" with no missing responses. This is exceptionally low risk of bias due to missing data for this outcome.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (Patient Acceptance)
**High risk**

Justification: Patient acceptance is a highly subjective, participant-reported outcome, assessed via a self-report Likert-scale questionnaire. Critically, patients were NOT blinded to their treatment allocation (explicitly stated: "Blinding of the clinicians and patients to the interventions in each group was impossible"). This is highly problematic for a subjective outcome like "acceptance," because:
- Patients' perceptions and reporting of acceptance may be influenced by their awareness of receiving the "enhanced" (mini-implant) treatment versus the "standard" treatment, potentially introducing differential reporting bias (e.g., patients receiving the novel/additional treatment may feel obligated to report higher satisfaction, or conversely may focus more on the discomfort of the extra procedure).
- Although the outcome *assessor* who extracted and analyzed the data was blinded, this does not mitigate the fundamental problem that the outcome itself is generated directly by unblinded patients. Blinding the assessor cannot correct for bias introduced at the point of data generation by unblinded participants.
- The questionnaire, while adapted from a previous validated study (Bowman et al.), was not independently validated for this specific population/context, and no test-retest reliability data are provided for the translated version used in this Egyptian cohort.

This combination of subjective outcome + unblinded participants generating the outcome data constitutes a high risk of bias for this specific outcome measurement, per the Cochrane RoB2 framework criteria explicitly flagged in the instructions.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Some concerns**

Justification: The trial was not registered, and the protocol was not published prior to trial commencement, meaning there is no independent verifiable pre-specification of outcomes, analysis plans, or all timing-points for outcome assessment against which the reported results can be checked. The authors state "there were no outcome changes after commencement of the trial," but this is a self-report with no external documentation to confirm it. The primary outcome is reported clearly and comprehensively (Table VII, Figures), and multiple secondary outcomes are reported without evidence of selective omission — this is reassuring. However, the absence of prospective registration/protocol remains a meaningful limitation preventing full verification of selective reporting, warranting "some concerns" rather than "low risk."

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** While randomization and missing data domains show low risk of bias, the outcome of "patient acceptance" is a subjective, self-reported outcome measured in a context where blinding of participants was impossible — a fundamental and unavoidable limitation directly acknowledged by the authors. This alone triggers high risk per the specified criteria for participant-reported subjective outcomes without participant blinding. Compounding this, the "some concerns" ratings for deviations from intended intervention (given inherent unblinding of care providers/patients) and selection of reported result (absence of registration/protocol) further reduce confidence in the primary outcome estimate. The overall assessment is therefore **High risk** of bias for the patient acceptance outcome, driven primarily by the unblinded, subjective nature of outcome measurement in a study where such blinding was structurally impossible.
