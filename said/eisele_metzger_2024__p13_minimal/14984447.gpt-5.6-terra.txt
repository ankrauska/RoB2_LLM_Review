1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The report states that participants were stratified by income category and “randomly assigned” in a patient-blinded fashion to no-cost (NC) or control (C) groups, with 31 participants per group. Baseline characteristics in Table 1 appear broadly comparable, including HbA1c, reported baseline SBGM frequency, diabetes type, insurance status, income category, and insulin regimen. This offers some reassurance that major measured prognostic factors were balanced.  
   - However, the method used to generate the random allocation sequence is not reported. There is no indication of whether a computer-generated sequence, random-number table, shuffled envelopes, alternation, or another method was used. Therefore, it cannot be verified that allocation was truly random.  
   - Allocation concealment is also inadequately described. “Patient-blinded” allocation does not establish that the person enrolling participants could not foresee or influence the next assignment. No central randomization, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or equivalent concealment procedure is reported.  
   - The asserted patient blinding is implausible after allocation: NC participants received 100 strips per month free of charge, whereas control participants received only an initial package of 50 strips and were required to purchase further supplies. Participants would necessarily know whether they were receiving free strips.  
   - Thus, although baseline balance is reasonably reassuring, inadequate reporting of sequence generation and allocation concealment leaves **some concerns** about bias arising from the randomization process.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The intended intervention appears to have been provision of adequate glucometer strips free of charge, compared with requiring participants to purchase strips after a one-time initial supply. The difference in access to strips was deliberately created and is therefore part of the intervention rather than, in itself, a protocol deviation.  
   - Participants could not realistically be blinded to the intervention. Knowledge that strips were free, or conversely that one had to pay for strips, could influence self-monitoring behaviour, engagement with diabetes self-management, motivation, dissatisfaction, attendance, and decisions to withdraw. For SBGM frequency, altered behaviour is partly the intended mechanism; however, awareness could also generate additional behavioural differences beyond the simple availability of strips.  
   - The investigators attempted to limit differential clinical management: the research nurse reportedly did not provide feedback on meter readings, random glucose, or HbA1c results, and physician visit frequency was intended to remain unchanged. These measures reduce concern about investigator-driven co-interventions.  
   - Nevertheless, participants could view their own glucometer readings and may have received HbA1c results or diabetes-management advice from their usual clinicians. It is not reported whether insulin changes, clinician contacts, dietary interventions, or diabetes education differed between groups. Importantly, insulin dose increased substantially in the control group, suggesting that treatment management changed differentially during follow-up, although the reasons and timing are not adequately documented.  
   - The differential withdrawal rate—19% in NC versus 48% in controls—also indicates that the intervention assignment affected continued participation. This is principally an issue of missing data, but it may also reflect difficulties in maintaining the assigned strategy.  
   - Overall, there is no clear evidence of major protocol violations, but absence of effective participant blinding and insufficient information on co-interventions leave **some concerns**.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
   - Attrition was substantial and strongly differential: by the end of follow-up, 25/31 (81%) NC participants remained, compared with only 16/31 (52%) control participants. Thus, 6/31 (19%) NC participants and 15/31 (48%) control participants withdrew or were lost.  
   - Such loss is sufficiently large to threaten validity for both SBGM frequency and HbA1c. The available-case data at later visits are particularly vulnerable to bias because nearly half of the control group was no longer contributing observed outcomes.  
   - The reasons for withdrawal are not presented separately by randomized group or linked to participants’ outcomes. The discussion speculates that control participants who considered strips too costly may have dropped out. If true, missingness is likely related to intervention assignment and potentially to SBGM behaviour, socioeconomic barriers, glycaemic control, or motivation—all factors related to the outcomes.  
   - The authors used an intention-to-treat analysis with last observation carried forward (LOCF). LOCF is not a reliable remedy for this degree of differential attrition. It assumes that a participant’s unobserved future SBGM frequency or HbA1c remained equal to the last measured value, an assumption that is especially implausible over 12 months in diabetes management and when discontinuation may be related to cost, monitoring, or glycaemic deterioration.  
   - No sensitivity analyses using plausible alternative assumptions, no multiple-imputation analysis, and no analyses addressing missing-not-at-random mechanisms are reported.  
   - Therefore, missing outcome data are very likely to have biased the estimated effects on both SBGM frequency and HbA1c. This domain is at **high risk of bias**.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome**

   **4a. SBGM frequency: low risk**  
   - SBGM frequency was measured by downloading the glucometer memory rather than relying solely on participant self-report. This is an objective and appropriate measurement method, and it reduces recall and social-desirability bias.  
   - The same type of glucometer and the same download procedure appear to have been used in both groups. The research nurse downloaded the meter memory at scheduled visits and reportedly did not analyse data until study completion.  
   - Although participants knew whether they received free strips, the outcome itself was machine-recorded. Knowledge of allocation could change actual monitoring behaviour, but that is a behavioural effect of the intervention, not bias in measurement of the frequency of tests performed.  
   - The report does not discuss whether participants could use other meters, manually enter data, or perform tests without recording them in the study meter. However, participants were required to discontinue their prior glucometer, and no evidence is presented that differential outcome mismeasurement occurred.  
   - Accordingly, measurement of **SBGM frequency is judged low risk of bias**.

   **4b. HbA1c: low risk**  
   - HbA1c is an objective biochemical outcome, measured using a specified analyser (DCA 2000). The same measurement approach appears to have been used for both randomized groups and at each scheduled visit.  
   - The outcome is not dependent on participant judgement or self-report. Consequently, lack of participant blinding is unlikely to bias the laboratory measurement itself.  
   - The report does not explicitly state whether laboratory personnel or the research nurse were blinded to treatment allocation. However, HbA1c measurement is automated and not readily susceptible to assessor expectation or selective interpretation.  
   - There is no indication that the measurement method differed between groups.  
   - Therefore, measurement of **HbA1c is judged low risk of bias**.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - No prospectively registered protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prespecified primary-outcome hierarchy is provided. The article says that SBGM frequency, average glucometer-recorded glucose, random blood glucose, and HbA1c were measured at each visit, but it is unclear which analysis, time point, and effect estimate were specified in advance as primary.  
   - Numerous outcome-related analyses and time points were available: SBGM frequency over follow-up, SBGM during the first 4 months, HbA1c over time, HbA1c at 6 months, random glucose, mean glucometer glucose, insulin dose, correlations between SBGM and HbA1c, and analyses based on testing at least twice daily.  
   - The manuscript emphasizes selected findings, including higher SBGM frequency “during the first 4 months” and the HbA1c difference being “most apparent at the 6th month.” These time-specific emphases raise concern that favourable results may have been selected from repeated assessments.  
   - Several analytic approaches are described—general linear modelling with baseline and time covariates, LOCF intention-to-treat analysis, and repeated-measures analysis among completers—but the correspondence between each reported result and a prespecified analysis is unclear. Results from alternative analyses are not fully reported.  
   - The absence of a protocol does not prove selective reporting, but it prevents verification that the reported comparisons were chosen before outcome data were examined.  
   - Therefore, there are **some concerns** regarding selection of the reported result.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The overall rating is **high risk of bias**, principally because of substantial and highly differential missing outcome data (19% attrition in the no-cost group versus 48% in the control group), coupled with an inadequate LOCF approach and no persuasive analysis showing that missingness could not materially alter the findings.  
   - Additional concerns arise from inadequately reported randomization and allocation concealment, unavoidable lack of participant blinding, incomplete information on co-interventions, and absence of a prospective protocol or clearly prespecified primary analyses.  
   - The machine-recorded SBGM frequency and laboratory-measured HbA1c themselves were measured with low risk of measurement bias. However, objective measurement does not overcome the serious risk that differential dropout and uncertain analytic selection distorted the estimated effects.
