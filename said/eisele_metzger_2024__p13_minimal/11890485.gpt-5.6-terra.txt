1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - Allocation was described as patients (or caregivers) drawing one of 100 sealed, nontransparent envelopes prepared by the dementia family care coordinator. This is compatible with random allocation only if the envelopes had been filled according to an adequately generated unpredictable random sequence and were securely prepared and opened sequentially or otherwise protected from manipulation. Neither the method used to generate the allocation sequence nor these safeguards is reported.  
   - Allocation concealment is particularly questionable because the coordinator who prepared the envelopes was also responsible for delivering the intervention and collecting/documenting intervention-related information. “Sealed nontransparent envelopes” alone do not establish that assignments could not be foreseen, selected, or manipulated.  
   - The final 14 participants were allocated in a planned 2:1 intervention:control ratio, rather than the initially used approximately 1:1 ratio. Unequal allocation is not itself biased if randomly implemented, but the altered ratio was introduced to offer more patients the intervention and further reduces confidence in the consistency and protection of the allocation process.  
   - Baseline characteristics appear broadly similar, although there were numerically more patients with severe dementia in the intervention group (36% versus 24%) and fewer with moderate dementia (24% versus 38%). This does not prove a failure of randomization in such a small trial, but it does not resolve the inadequate reporting of sequence generation and concealment.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**  
   - Blinding of patients, caregivers, the care coordinator, and treating services was not feasible: the intervention was an overt, intensive nurse case-management and support programme.  
   - The substantial differences in contact, counselling, advocacy, service coordination, training courses, home visits, and access to the coordinator are the intended intervention, rather than unintended departures from it. The intervention was explicitly designed to respond to crises that might otherwise result in institutionalization.  
   - There is no evidence that control participants received the coordinator-led programme, or that intervention participants systematically failed to receive it. The coordinator documented contacts and services, supporting that the intervention was actually delivered, albeit with intensity varying according to need.  
   - No analysis explicitly addressing adherence or contamination is reported. Nevertheless, for the relevant pragmatic effect of assignment to this support programme, there is no clear evidence of important deviations from the intended interventions that would bias the result.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk**  
   - The authors report no dropouts during the two-year follow-up. All 100 randomized participants are accounted for as remaining at home, being institutionalized, or dying at home.  
   - Institutionalization and death were ascertained from the Social Insurance Institution register, reducing the likelihood that outcome status was unavailable because of loss to follow-up.  
   - Death before institutionalization occurred in 9/53 (17%) intervention participants and 8/47 (17%) control participants. These deaths were not missing observations; they are known competing events. The authors censored follow-up at death in the time-to-institutionalization analysis. That analytic choice may affect interpretation of the estimand, because death prevents later institutionalization, but it is not missing outcome data and deaths were balanced between groups.  
   - An apparent internal inconsistency should be noted: the supplied description of Figure 1 states that 37 intervention participants were at home at two years, whereas the reported 17 institutionalizations and 9 deaths imply 27—not 37—remaining at home (and 27/53 is consistent with the stated 51%). This appears likely to be a transcription/typographical error, but it warrants clarification. It does not, on the available information, demonstrate missing outcome data.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—time to institutionalization: low risk**  
   - The outcome was the time from enrolment to placement in long-term institutional care. Institutional placement is a relatively objective, administratively recorded event, and ascertainment was from the Social Insurance Institution register rather than relying solely on participant or caregiver report.  
   - Decisions on permanent institutionalization were reportedly made by local groups of experts—usually a chief physician, head nurse, and social worker—who were blind to treatment allocation and generally unaware of trial participation. This is an important protection against differential outcome assessment.  
   - Patients and caregivers necessarily knew their allocation, and caregiver willingness or perceived ability to continue care can influence requests for institutionalization. Moreover, the intervention was specifically intended to help manage such circumstances. These features describe an expected causal mechanism of the intervention, rather than biased ascertainment of whether institutionalization occurred.  
   - The care coordinator was unblinded and involved in arranging services, but there is no indication that she adjudicated or recorded institutionalization differently by group; the endpoint was obtained from an external register. Thus, for this outcome, differential measurement bias is unlikely.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - Time to institutionalization is explicitly identified as the main outcome measure in the methods section, and a corresponding time-to-event analysis is reported. This supports that the principal outcome was prospectively intended.  
   - However, no protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial registration is provided. It is therefore impossible to verify whether the reported Cox model, the time-by-treatment interaction, the chosen time points (6, 12, and 24 months), and the subgroup analyses by dementia severity were specified before examination of the data.  
   - The main reported result is somewhat difficult to interpret. The Cox analysis reports a treatment hazard ratio of 0.12 (*P*=.042) together with a treatment-by-time interaction of 1.18 per month (*P*=.028), indicating that any early apparent benefit diminished and reversed over time. Yet the abstract and conclusion emphasize that placement “can be deferred,” despite similar cumulative institutionalization by two years (32% intervention versus 30% control) and a slightly lower Kaplan–Meier probability of remaining in community care at 24 months in the intervention group (0.63 versus 0.68).  
   - The statement that severely demented participants “benefited the most” is based on descriptive subgroup survival curves, without a reported formal interaction test. This is vulnerable to selective emphasis, especially in a small study.  
   - These issues do not establish selective non-reporting, but the absence of prespecified analysis documentation and the selective-sounding emphasis on early and subgroup findings raise some concerns.

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   The trial has important strengths for this endpoint: complete follow-up, register-based ascertainment, and apparently blinded institutionalization decision-makers. However, the randomization process is insufficiently reported—especially sequence generation and allocation concealment involving envelopes prepared by the intervention coordinator—and there are unresolved concerns about selective analytical emphasis in the absence of a protocol or registration. These concerns do not, on the available evidence, amount to high risk of bias, but they preclude a low-risk judgement.
