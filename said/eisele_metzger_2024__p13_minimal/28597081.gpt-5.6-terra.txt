1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
   - Participants were described as randomly allocated in parallel to bright light, blue light, or control.  
   - The sequence was generated using an online randomization tool employing permuted blocks with randomly varying block sizes (3, 6, and 9), which is an appropriate random method.  
   - Allocation concealment appears adequate: the sequence was transmitted by a person not involved in the study using serially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes.  
   - Although baseline data by group are only partly available in the supplied report, there is no clear evidence of important baseline imbalance suggesting a failure of randomization.  
   - Two of 74 randomized participants were retrospectively excluded because of invalid pre-study peak-oxygen-uptake measurements. This is undesirable after randomization, but the stated reason is unrelated to acoustic reaction time or to the post-intervention reaction-time result and is unlikely to indicate manipulation of allocation or outcome data.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
   - The interventions were administered in distinct rooms and consisted of 60 minutes of the assigned light exposure following the same pre-exposure procedures. The test supervisor was blinded to allocated light condition.  
   - Participants could not realistically be blinded to the immediate light environment. The trial registry also specifies no masking. Participants knew their own condition, although they reportedly did not know the details of the other conditions.  
   - Lack of participant blinding could in principle affect effort on a performance task. However, there is no evidence that participants differentially received additional treatment, altered the testing procedure, or had unequal contact with study personnel according to allocated group.  
   - The actual illuminance reaching participants’ eyes varied substantially and was lower than intended, particularly in the bright-light arm; there was also overlap between the blue-light and control exposure levels. This is an important limitation of intervention fidelity and may dilute contrasts between groups. Nevertheless, it does not appear to represent post-randomization deviations selectively induced by staff or participants, and the primary group comparison retained participants in their allocated groups.  
   - Therefore, for the effect of assignment to the study light conditions, risk of bias from deviations is low, though the estimates may be imprecise or underestimate effects of reliably delivered high-intensity light.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk**  
   - Of 74 randomized participants, 72 were included in the reaction-time analyses: 24 in each group.  
   - The two exclusions were attributed to invalid measurements of peak oxygen uptake during the eligibility fitness test, not to missing acoustic reaction-time measurements, adverse effects, intervention response, or knowledge of reaction-time results.  
   - The report gives no indication of missing post-exposure reaction-time data among the 72 analysed participants. Each participant’s outcome was derived from repeated trials during the computer task.  
   - The small proportion excluded (2/74; 2.7%) and the apparently outcome-independent reason for exclusion make it unlikely that missing data materially biased the result.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — acoustic reaction time: some concerns**  
   - Acoustic reaction time was measured using a computer-based simple reaction-time task: an acoustic signal appeared at random 3–7-second intervals during five minutes and participants responded by pressing the space bar. This is a relatively objective and standardized performance measure.  
   - Reaction times exceeding 500 ms were predefined as lapses and excluded; the median of the remaining reaction times was calculated for each participant. The same method was used before and after exposure and across groups.  
   - The outcome assessor/test supervisor was blinded to light condition, reducing the risk that administration or recording of the test was influenced by knowledge of allocation.  
   - However, participants were not blinded to their light exposure. Acoustic reaction time, although instrument-recorded, requires voluntary speeded responding and can be influenced by expectancy, motivation, arousal, or perceived assignment. The hypothesized effects of bright and blue light on alertness and reaction time were particularly likely to be understood by participants.  
   - The outcome is less vulnerable than a participant-reported subjective outcome such as pain or enjoyment, but it is not wholly immune to performance/expectancy effects. The incomplete participant blinding therefore leaves **some concerns**, rather than high risk, for measurement bias in acoustic reaction time.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The registry (NCT02203539) identifies reaction time in milliseconds as a secondary outcome, consistent with the manuscript’s statement that reaction time was a pre-specified secondary outcome. Thus, the outcome itself does not appear to have been introduced after the trial.  
   - The manuscript reports the principal between-group analysis using ANCOVA adjusted for pre-exposure reaction time, which is a reasonable analysis for the pre/post design. It reports comparisons of bright versus control, blue versus control, and bright versus blue, with confidence intervals and P values.  
   - However, the publicly supplied registration information does not specify the reaction-time measurement time point, the handling of repeated trial-level data/lapses, the use of median reaction time, the covariate-adjusted ANCOVA model, contrasts, or the additional analyses based on recorded light intensity.  
   - The manuscript states that the statistical methods were “pre-specified and registered,” but this cannot be verified from the registration record provided. Since reaction time was a secondary outcome and several potentially selectable analysis choices existed, the absence of a sufficiently detailed accessible prospective analysis plan leaves some concerns about selective selection of the reported analysis/result.  
   - There is no direct evidence of selective non-reporting, but the available protocol/registration information is insufficiently detailed to exclude it confidently.

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   Randomization, handling of missing data, and deviations from assigned intervention are at low risk of bias. However, participants were not blinded for a performance-based reaction-time outcome, and the registered record does not provide enough detail to verify that the specific reported reaction-time analysis was prospectively specified. These concerns do not amount to high risk, but they prevent a low-risk overall judgement.
