# Risk of Bias Assessment: Response Rate Endpoint
## Sir Ian Chalmers, Clinical Trials Reviewer

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process:**
**Some concerns.** The manuscript states that a random sample of 7200 pharmacists was drawn and "randomized" into delivery method/length/placement/gender subgroups of 300 each, but no description is provided of the actual sequence generation method (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number tables) for allocation to delivery method. There is also no mention of allocation concealment procedures. Given that the sampling frames differed by group (MMS for postal/hybrid vs. Statlistics for email), there is a real possibility that randomization to delivery method was not performed from a single unified random process, but rather that different vendor-supplied lists were used for different arms — this raises concern about comparability of baseline characteristics by design rather than by chance. Indeed, Table 1 shows notable imbalance in ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White: 86.3% postal vs. 68.7% email) and practice site (hospital: 19.0% postal vs. 35.0% email), suggesting the randomization/sampling process may not have produced fully comparable groups.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions:**
**High risk.** The three delivery-method arms received an unequal number of follow-up contacts by design: postal mail participants received an initial mailing, a second full mailing, and a reminder postcard (3 total contacts); email participants received an initial message plus two follow-up reminders (3 total contacts); but the hybrid group received only an initial postcard and one follow-up postcard (2 total contacts, with no third reminder). Since response rate is the primary endpoint being compared across these arms, this asymmetry in intervention intensity is a substantial deviation that confounds the comparison — the hybrid group was structurally disadvantaged, which likely explains at least part of its markedly lower response rate (3.2%). This is not a deviation from protocol per se, but a built-in inequality in delivered intervention intensity that undermines a fair comparison of "delivery method" as the causal variable.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data:**
**Some concerns.** Of 7200 sampled, 233 questionnaires were undeliverable (handled reasonably by removing them from the denominator). However, "response rate" was defined strictly as returned questionnaires with *no missing data*, deliberately excluding partially completed returns (9.9% dropped for postal, 9.4% for email, and no hybrid figure given) from the numerator. This definition means that outcome ascertainment itself discards a nontrivial and possibly differential fraction of responders across arms, and no sensitivity analysis (e.g., including partial responses) or non-response analysis was conducted to assess whether this exclusion differentially biased the comparison. The authors themselves acknowledge the "absence of non-response analysis and missing-data response analysis" as a limitation.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (response rate):**
**Low risk.** Response rate is an objective, mechanically ascertained count (number of returned/completed questionnaires ÷ eligible respondents) rather than a subjective participant-reported outcome such as pain or satisfaction. Lack of blinding of participants to their assigned delivery method (which is unavoidable and irrelevant here, since participants necessarily know whether they received mail, email, or a hybrid approach) does not introduce measurement bias into this particular objective outcome. There is no scope for differential outcome assessment by unblinded assessors, as receipt/non-receipt of a completed questionnaire is not open to interpretation.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result:**
**Some concerns.** No trial registration, published protocol, or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is referenced anywhere in the manuscript. While the reported response rate results appear consistent with the stated study aims and methods section, the absence of any prospectively registered protocol makes it impossible to confirm that response rate (and its subgroup breakdowns) was analyzed and reported exactly as pre-specified, without post hoc selection of comparisons or thresholds (e.g., choice of chi-square test, subgroup stratifications reported).

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk of bias**

**Justification:** Although the outcome measurement itself (objective response counts) is low risk, and randomization concerns are only moderate, the domain of deviations from intended interventions is high risk: the three delivery-method arms were not administered with equivalent follow-up intensity (hybrid participants received only two contact attempts versus three for postal and email arms). Since delivery method is the key exposure being compared for the response-rate endpoint, this asymmetry directly confounds the primary result and cannot be dismissed as a minor concern. Combined with unexplained baseline imbalances across arms (domain 1), incomplete accounting for missing/partial data (domain 3), and the absence of a pre-registered protocol (domain 5), the cumulative concerns substantially lower confidence in the reported response-rate comparison between delivery methods.
