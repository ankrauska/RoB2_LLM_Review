# Sir Ian Chalmers' Risk of Bias Assessment
## CAESAR Trial (Lee et al., Clin Ther 2011) — Primary Endpoint: Change in mean sitting DBP from baseline after 4 weeks

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: SOME CONCERNS**

The study used "mixed block randomization (4 and 6 blocks)" but no method of allocation concealment (e.g., central randomization, sequentially numbered opaque envelopes) is described anywhere in the manuscript or protocol. In an open-label study with small, disclosed block sizes (4 and 6), investigators enrolling patients could plausibly predict upcoming allocations, especially towards the end of blocks — a classic concealment failure. Compounding my suspicion, despite "random" allocation the combination-therapy group was significantly older (51.0 vs 47.8 years, P=0.02) — a baseline imbalance that, while possibly due to chance, is exactly the kind of signal one worries about when concealment is inadequate. I cannot rule out selection bias in who was entered and to which arm.

---

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: SOME CONCERNS**

This was an open-label design (protocol masking = "NONE"), explicitly acknowledged by the authors themselves as "a potential source of bias." There was no placebo control, so both patients and treating physicians knew group allocation. While a forced-titration design at week 4 limits deviation in dosing, open-label knowledge of assignment could influence concomitant care, encouragement, or patient behaviour (e.g., diet/salt intake, adherence effort) differentially between arms, particularly relevant for a hemodynamic outcome sensitive to lifestyle and effort. The ITT analysis was appropriately defined and used, which mitigates but does not eliminate this concern.

---

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: LOW RISK**

Of 233 randomized patients, 224 comprised the ITT set (111/117 combination; 113/116 monotherapy) — attrition was modest (~5%) and quantitatively similar between arms. Reasons for exclusion (failure to meet BP criteria, consent withdrawal) were reported and do not appear to be related to the outcome itself. The flow diagram (Figure 2) is transparent, and results were consistent between ITT and per-protocol analyses (Tables II/III), suggesting missing data did not meaningfully bias the effect estimate.

---

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (mean sitting DBP at 4 weeks): SOME CONCERNS**

DBP is a semi-objective outcome — measured with a validated automated device (Omron) using a standardized protocol (5-min rest, triplicate/quintuplicate measures, discarding extremes), which is reassuring. However, this was an **open-label trial**; the personnel taking blood pressure measurements were not blinded to treatment allocation. Even with automated devices, know­ledge of allocation can subtly influence technique (patient positioning, rest time compliance, cuff placement, recording of borderline readings) — a well-documented source of bias in unblinded antihypertensive trials. The authors themselves flag the open-label design as a limitation for safety data; the same logic extends to BP measurement, which is the primary efficacy outcome. This is not as severe as a purely participant-reported subjective outcome (e.g., pain), but it is not free from assessor/observer bias either.

---

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: SOME CONCERNS**

The primary outcome as reported in the manuscript (change in mean sitting DBP at 4 weeks) matches the pre-registered primary outcome (NCT00621153) in definition, timeframe, and population size (111 vs 113). However:
- The registry reports the primary result as **LS mean** (-17.0 vs -14.1, ANCOVA-adjusted), whereas the manuscript text and Table II report raw mean (SD) values (-17.8 vs -14.1) — a discrepancy in numbers that, while explainable by different modeling, is not reconciled or explained in the manuscript, raising a question about which analysis was truly pre-specified as primary and whether the more favourable of two possible expressions was emphasized.
- All secondary outcomes registered prospectively (SBP change, hs-CRP, compliance, target-goal proportions) are marked "NOT_POSTED" in the registry, yet are fully reported in the publication — meaning I cannot independently verify from the registry that these were reported without selective emphasis on significant findings (e.g., SBP, target-goal attainment) while non-significant/unfavourable findings (BUN, uric acid increases with HCT) were reported with muted interpretation in the discussion.
- Post hoc subgroup analyses (Table IV) are appropriately labelled as post hoc, which is transparent, but their inclusion alongside primary results in a manuscript funded and partly assisted by AstraZeneca (sponsor) warrants some caution regarding emphasis in reporting.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: No single domain reaches "high risk," but there are concerns in the randomization process (undisclosed concealment method, small block sizes, unexplained baseline age imbalance), deviations from intended intervention (open-label design with potential differential co-intervention), outcome measurement (open-label BP assessment, susceptible to observer bias despite automated device), and selective reporting (discrepancy between registered LS-mean estimate and manuscript raw-mean estimate; incomplete registry posting of secondary outcomes). Cumulatively, these concerns — particularly the compounding effect of unblinded outcome assessment coupled with unclear allocation concealment — are sufficient to warrant an overall rating of "Some Concerns" rather than "Low risk," though they do not individually rise to a level that would justify "High risk" for this specific outcome.
