1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The authors state that 241 women were “prospectively randomized using computer-generated random numbers,” which is an appropriate method for generating an unpredictable allocation sequence.  
   - However, allocation was implemented using sealed envelopes by a specialist nurse on day 2 or 3 of menstruation. The report does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, identical, sealed securely, or opened only after enrolment. Thus, concealment of allocation cannot be verified.  
   - The absence of reported concealment safeguards is important because knowledge or potential prediction of the next assignment could influence inclusion, timing of enrolment, or allocation.  
   - Baseline characteristics presented (age, duration of infertility, BMI) appear reasonably similar between groups, which provides limited reassurance, but cannot establish adequate allocation concealment.  
   - **Judgement: some concerns.**

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - Blinding of participants, clinicians, nurses, ultrasound personnel, and outcome assessors is not reported. Given that the preparations were different injectable gonadotrophins, this was probably an open-label trial.  
   - The protocol allowed gonadotrophin doses to be “adjusted individually” based on serum estradiol and transvaginal ultrasound findings. Individual clinical dose adjustment is part of the intended treatment strategy, but in an unblinded trial it also permits clinician behaviour to differ by assigned preparation.  
   - Decisions concerning dose escalation, timing of hCG, and cancellation of cycles for decreasing estradiol or more than three follicles may have been influenced by knowledge of treatment allocation. These decisions could affect the opportunity to conceive and hence the clinical pregnancy rate.  
   - The authors report one cycle per participant and analyse 81, 80, and 80 participants in the respective groups, which is consistent with analysis according to random allocation. There is no explicit evidence of crossovers, non-adherence, or exclusion after randomization.  
   - Nevertheless, because the trial appears unblinded and important co-interventions/management decisions were discretionary, there are unresolved concerns about departures from the intended intervention strategy.  
   - **Judgement: some concerns.**

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - The reported clinical pregnancy counts sum to 42 pregnancies among all 241 randomized women: 21/81, 11/80, and 10/80. Thus, the denominators for the principal outcome appear to include all randomized participants.  
   - The text states that only one cycle per patient was performed, and no explicit losses to follow-up or exclusions from the clinical-pregnancy analysis are reported. This is reassuring.  
   - However, the CONSORT flow diagram as reproduced is incomplete/internally unclear: it is described as splitting into two arms despite the study having three randomized groups, and the article text does not provide a clear group-specific accounting of cycle cancellations, withdrawals, failure to receive insemination, or any outcome-assessment failures.  
   - Cancellations are specified in the treatment protocol but their number and distribution by treatment group are not reported. Although all randomized women seemingly remain in the pregnancy-rate denominators, the lack of transparent participant flow prevents firm confirmation that outcome data were available and handled consistently for every participant.  
   - **Judgement: some concerns.**

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — clinical pregnancy rate: low risk**  
   - Clinical pregnancy was defined as pregnancy documented by transvaginal ultrasound six weeks after IUI. This is a relatively objective outcome, less prone to participant expectation or reporting bias than subjective outcomes such as pain, satisfaction, or treatment preference.  
   - Blinding of ultrasound assessors is not reported, and the trial was likely open label. Nevertheless, it is unlikely that knowledge of the assigned gonadotrophin preparation could materially alter whether an intrauterine clinical pregnancy is visualized on transvaginal ultrasound.  
   - The outcome definition is stated prospectively in the methods and is applied identically across groups.  
   - There is no indication that different measurement methods, timing, diagnostic thresholds, or assessors were used between groups.  
   - **Judgement: low risk.**

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The manuscript identifies clinical pregnancy rate as the primary outcome in the methods section, and it reports this outcome for all three groups with numerical event counts and a P value. This is favourable.  
   - However, no trial registration, accessible protocol, or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is reported. Therefore, it is not possible to verify that the stated primary outcome, its definition, analysis population, time point, and comparison were specified before results were known.  
   - The study involves three treatment groups and several outcomes. The report presents an overall three-group P value, while the conclusion focuses on the apparently superior rFSH group. The planned pairwise comparisons and any adjustment for multiple comparisons are not described.  
   - The clinical pregnancy result is marginally statistically significant (P = 0.046; reported as 0.04 in the abstract), making confidence especially dependent on prespecification and transparent analysis choices.  
   - **Judgement: some concerns.**

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   - The clinical-pregnancy outcome itself was measured objectively and is at low risk of measurement bias.  
   - However, there are some concerns regarding allocation concealment, likely unblinded discretionary treatment management, incomplete transparency of participant flow/missing data, and lack of a protocol or registration allowing verification of pre-specified analyses.  
   - No single issue is sufficiently documented to warrant a definite high-risk judgement, but the combination of concerns lowers confidence in the estimated apparent advantage of follitropin alfa for clinical pregnancy.
