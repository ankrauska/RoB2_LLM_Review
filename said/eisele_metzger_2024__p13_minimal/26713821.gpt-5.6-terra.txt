1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns** (The study is described as a 1:1 randomized trial, with 80 participants allocated 40:40, and the baseline table shows no important apparent imbalances in measured characteristics. However, neither the manuscript nor the provided registration record states how the random sequence was generated—for example, computer-generated sequence, random-number table, or permuted blocks. More importantly, there is no description of allocation concealment, such as central randomization or consecutively numbered opaque sealed envelopes. This is particularly material in a small, single-centre trial. The trial was registered as single-masked, with outcome assessors masked, but that is not evidence that group assignment was concealed before enrolment. Thus, random allocation is asserted but cannot be verified as truly random and adequately concealed.)

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns** (Participants and treating personnel could not realistically be blinded to allocation: one group attended 26–28 nutritionist-led group sessions promoting caloric restriction, while the control group received usual medical care. Both groups were also advised to undertake physical activity and received geriatric consultations approximately every two months. Such co-interventions were intended to be common to both groups, but actual adherence to the exercise advice, contamination by dietary advice or weight-loss efforts in the conventional-care group, and attendance/adherence to counselling are not adequately reported.)

   (The problem is more serious for the analysis actually emphasized in the paper. The planned randomized comparison was the comparison of conventional care versus conventional care plus nutritional counselling. That comparison showed no statistically significant group-by-time interaction for most cognitive tests, and the groups lost similar amounts of weight: mean BMI change −2.1 versus −1.3 kg/m²; P=.428. The investigators then explicitly decided *a posteriori* to pool the randomized groups and relate cognitive change to individual BMI change. This post-randomization observational analysis does not estimate the effect of randomized assignment to counselling/caloric restriction. BMI loss may reflect motivation, health status, changes in comorbidity treatment, dietary changes outside the assigned intervention, physical activity, or other prognostic factors. Covariate adjustment cannot reliably remove these sources of confounding in a sample of 75. Hence, although there is no clear evidence of an unbalanced protocol deviation deliberately induced by investigators, the principal interpretation departs substantially from the intended randomized intervention comparison.)

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns** (Seventy-five of 80 randomized participants completed 12-month follow-up: 37/40 in conventional care and 38/40 in intensive counselling. Thus, outcome data were missing for 5/80 (6.25%) participants, with slightly more missing data in the conventional-care group. One participant died of pneumonia and four withdrew, but the report does not identify withdrawal reasons by randomized group, whether cognitive outcomes were obtained before withdrawal, or whether missingness was related to cognitive trajectory, intervention adherence, weight loss, illness, or treatment assignment.)

   (The amount of missing data is modest and is unlikely by itself to overturn a large, robust randomized effect. However, no intention-to-treat analysis with an appropriate approach to missing data, sensitivity analysis, or comparison of completers with non-completers is reported. The cognitive analyses appear to be based on the 75 completers. Because the primary cognitive outcomes are susceptible to deterioration, illness, and dropout in this elderly MCI population, it is not possible to conclude that missingness was unrelated to unobserved cognitive outcome values. This warrants some concerns rather than low risk.)

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome:**

   The registration record states that the **outcomes assessor** was single-masked. This is an important protection for performance-based neuropsychological tests, provided masking was maintained. Nevertheless, participants were aware of whether they attended repeated nutritional counselling sessions, and the report does not document the success of assessor masking, use of alternate test forms, or whether assessors remained unaware of participant comments that might reveal allocation. Repeated testing also creates possible practice effects; this affects interpretation of change over time, though it need not cause differential bias if measurement was identically conducted in both groups.

   * **CAMcog global cognition: some concerns** (CAMcog is a structured, assessor-administered cognitive performance test. Assessor masking was reportedly used, reducing the likelihood of differential scoring. However, participants were unblinded, and performance can be influenced by effort, expectations, and repeated-test familiarity. No information is given on maintenance of assessor masking or alternate forms. The risk is not clearly high, but low risk cannot be confidently assigned.)

   * **RAVLT delayed recall (A7): some concerns** (This is a standardized participant-performance memory measure, with relatively objective scoring and reportedly masked outcome assessment. However, the participant’s unblinded status and repeat administration after 12 months can affect effort and practice-related performance. There is no information on alternate word lists or assessor-mask integrity.)

   * **RAVLT total learning (sum A1–A5): some concerns** (As for RAVLT delayed recall: scoring is objective and assessor masking is a strength, but participants were aware of allocation and repeated assessment may be influenced by practice and expectations. The manuscript does not describe alternate-form use.)

   * **RAVLT immediate recall (A6) and recognition, where analysed: some concerns** (These are performance-based memory outcomes subject to the same considerations: masked assessor reported, but unblinded participants and unclear handling of repeated testing/alternate forms.)

   * **Digit span forward: some concerns** (A structured performance test with relatively objective scoring. Assessor masking reduces risk, but the participant was unblinded and effort/engagement may be influenced by expectations. Details sufficient to establish low risk are absent.)

   * **Digit span backward: some concerns** (The same judgement applies. It is a performance-based attention/working-memory measure, but is not immune to differential effort by participants aware of their intervention status.)

   * **Phonemic verbal fluency: some concerns** (The number of valid words can be scored objectively by a masked assessor, but performance is affected by motivation and participant effort. Participants were not blinded and no information is provided on standardization beyond naming the test.)

   * **Semantic verbal fluency: some concerns** (Same reasoning as for phonemic fluency: relatively objective performance scoring and assessor masking, but unblinded participants and no evidence concerning assessor-mask preservation.)

   * **Modified Wisconsin Card Sorting Test categories: some concerns** (This is a structured performance measure with a more objective scoring framework. Outcome-assessor masking is favourable. Nevertheless, participant knowledge of allocation, possible differential encouragement or engagement, and lack of detail about administration and mask maintenance leave some concerns.)

   * **Trail Making Test A: some concerns** (Completion time is relatively objective, and assessor masking is reported. Yet the test requires participant effort and repeated exposure; participants were unblinded, and no detailed safeguards against differential encouragement, assessor unmasking, or practice effects are reported.)

   * **Trail Making Test B: some concerns** (As for Trail Making Test A. This outcome is especially relevant because several exploratory associations with diet and BMI were reported for TMB, but measurement conditions and repeated-test procedures are insufficiently described to establish low risk.)

   * **IQCODE cognitive complaints: high risk** (IQCODE is an informant-reported, subjective assessment of cognitive decline rather than a directly scored neuropsychological performance test. The registration says only that the outcome assessor was masked; it does not state that informants were blinded to the participant’s allocated nutritional counselling. In this behavioural intervention, participants and likely their close informants could know whether repeated counselling and weight-loss efforts were occurring. Expectations about benefit could therefore influence subjective ratings. The manuscript provides no evidence that informants were masked or that this outcome was collected in a way protected from knowledge of allocation. For this subjective outcome, the risk of differential measurement bias is high.)

   * **Composite/domain scores for global cognition, verbal memory, language/fluency, executive function, and attention: some concerns** (These were created post hoc using principal-components analysis across multiple individual tests. Although based mostly on the performance outcomes above, the construction of the composites after data collection adds analytical discretion. The composites also combine tests with differing susceptibility to practice and participant-effort effects. The underlying assessor masking is helpful, but the measurement and construction of these derived outcomes do not justify a low-risk judgement.)

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high risk** (The registry identifies “Neuropsychological test performance” generically as a primary outcome, but supplies no prespecified individual cognitive tests, cognitive domains, time-point-specific analyses, primary contrast, covariate model, multiplicity strategy, or hierarchy among outcomes. Therefore, the registration does not permit verification that the reported cognitive analyses were selected in advance.)

   (The manuscript reports numerous individual cognitive tests, several derived cognitive domains, BMI-change analyses, age and APOE4 subgroup/interactions, and correlations with multiple biomarkers and dietary variables. It explicitly acknowledges that, because both randomized groups lost similar amounts of weight and there was no group difference in most cognitive outcomes, the investigators **decided a posteriori** to pool groups and test BMI change as a continuous exposure. This is a major post hoc change from the randomized comparison to an observational association within the pooled sample.)

   (The authors further state that, owing to the exploratory nature of the analyses and small sample, they did not correct for multiple comparisons. Numerous nominally statistically significant findings are consequently available by chance alone. The prominent conclusion—intentional weight loss was associated with cognitive improvement—rests principally on these post-randomization, pooled, selectively analysable associations rather than on a clearly prespecified and successful randomized group comparison. This is high risk of bias in selection of the reported result.)

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias** (The overall risk is high because the principal cognitive claims rely on a post hoc pooled analysis of BMI change after the randomized intervention contrast was largely null, alongside extensive uncorrected multiple testing and insufficiently prespecified outcome/analysis selection. There are also some concerns regarding randomization concealment, missing outcome data, deviations from the intended randomized comparison, and measurement of several participant-performance outcomes; IQCODE is at high risk of measurement bias because it is subjective and informant blinding is not documented.)
