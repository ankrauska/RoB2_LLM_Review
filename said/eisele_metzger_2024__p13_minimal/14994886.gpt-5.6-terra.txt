1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns** (The report states only that patients were “randomly selected” for the 0.014- or 0.016-inch wire. It does not describe the random-sequence generation method—for example, computer-generated random numbers or a random-number table—and does not report allocation concealment. Thus, it cannot be established that allocation was genuinely unpredictable to the treating orthodontist at the time of assignment. Group sizes were broadly similar (56 versus 53), but this is not sufficient evidence of adequate randomization or concealment. No baseline clinical characteristics relevant to pain, such as degree of crowding, are reported by group; indeed, crowding was not assessed.)

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk** (The allocated wire appears to have been inserted and ligated as intended in all participants. The appliance system was the same in both groups, and the report states that no extra-oral appliances, palatal arches, or quad-helix appliances were used during the experimental period. The endpoint considered—time of *initial* pain—is assessed before later analgesic use could plausibly alter whether or when pain was first noticed. Although no blinding procedures are reported, there is no evidence that knowledge of assignment led to departures from the assigned wire intervention or to differential co-intervention before initial pain was reported.)

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk** (The authors explicitly state that questionnaires were completed by, and returned from, all 109 participants. For the initial-pain endpoint, the reported numbers reconcile with the randomized group totals when those reporting no pain are included: 50 reporting pain plus 6 reporting no pain in the 0.014-inch group (56 total), and 47 plus 6 in the 0.016-inch group (53 total). There is therefore no apparent loss of outcome data for this endpoint.)

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: some concerns** (Initial pain was a subjective, participant-reported outcome, recorded using a questionnaire apparently completed retrospectively and returned at the next appointment. The time of first pain was reported in hourly categories. The manuscript does not state that participants were blinded to wire size, nor that questionnaires were completed contemporaneously when pain began; both omissions matter for a subjective outcome susceptible to expectation and recall effects. It is not certain that patients knew the wire diameter, so a high-risk judgement cannot be made confidently on that basis alone. Nevertheless, absence of reported participant blinding and delayed self-report leave material concern that reported onset time could have been influenced by expectations or recall.)

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns** (No protocol, trial registration, statistical analysis plan, or pre-specified primary outcome is available. The investigators measured numerous outcomes—initial-pain timing, pain presence and VAS intensity at eight periods, location-specific pain, analgesic consumption, daily-life effects, and gender comparisons. For initial pain, the article presents the distribution across time categories and states that there was no statistically significant between-group difference, which is reassuring to a limited extent. However, without pre-specification, it is not possible to determine whether the selected analysis, categorisation of onset time, or presentation of results was chosen after examining the data.)

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias** (There are some concerns in several important domains: inadequately described randomization/allocation concealment, potentially biased measurement of a subjective participant-reported endpoint, and absence of a protocol or registration to exclude selective reporting. Taken together, these concerns substantially reduce confidence in the reported comparison of initial pain between wire sizes.)
