1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The investigators state that they used “computerized block randomization” and that allocation was concealed in envelopes. Computer generation is an appropriate method for producing a random sequence.  
   - However, the report provides no details on who generated the sequence, who prepared the envelopes, whether envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, and tamper-proof, or whether they were opened only after irrevocable enrolment. These omissions prevent confidence that allocation concealment was maintained.  
   - Baseline pain intensity was broadly comparable across groups (mean 69.9, 67.2, and 70.3 mm; reported *P* > 0.05), which does not suggest a major failure of randomization. Nevertheless, absence of a statistically significant baseline difference is not proof that allocation was adequately concealed.  
   - The claim that participants were blinded is not convincing. Participants allocated to TENS or IFT received an additional 20-minute electrical treatment, whereas control participants did not. Thus, the comparison with standard care alone could readily reveal allocation. Moreover, TENS and quadripolar IFT use visibly different electrode configurations and potentially distinguishable sensations.  

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: high risk**  
   - The trial compared standard care alone with standard care plus an additional 20 minutes of either TENS or IFT. Thus, treatment groups differed not only in the electrical modality but also in treatment attention, contact time, equipment use, sensory experience, and likely expectations of benefit. There was no sham TENS/IFT procedure for the control group.  
   - Although all groups received the same stated standard-care package and home exercise/postural programme, the principal investigator both delivered every intervention and assessed every outcome. This creates substantial scope for differential encouragement, enthusiasm, therapist–participant interaction, reinforcement of adherence, or other departures from equivalent care according to allocated treatment.  
   - Blinding of participants was unlikely to have been successful, particularly for the control versus electrical-treatment comparisons. Knowledge of allocation can alter participants’ expectations, reporting of pain, adherence to home exercises, and behaviour during treatment.  
   - Home-exercise diaries were checked by the unblinded principal investigator, but adherence data by group are not reported. It is therefore not possible to determine whether adherence to the common home programme, or any additional care, differed across groups.  
   - The analysis appears to be according to randomized groups, and all 105 randomized participants were analysed. Nonetheless, the combination of implausible participant blinding, no credible sham/control for additional electrical-treatment attention, and unblinded delivery by the outcome assessor makes bias from deviations highly likely for the subjective pain endpoint.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk**  
   - The study reports that all 105 enrolled participants completed the study and were included in the analysis: 35 in each group. The flow diagram is consistent with no reported losses after randomization.  
   - Pain intensity data are presented for all randomized participants at baseline, immediately after the first treatment, before the eighth treatment, and one week after the eighth treatment.  
   - There is therefore no evident missing-data problem for the reported pain outcomes, and no need for assumptions or imputation methods that could introduce bias.  
   - This judgement relies on the accuracy of the authors’ assertion of complete follow-up; the report does not provide individual-level data or a detailed accounting of missed visits, but there is no indication that outcome data were missing.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — pain intensity: high risk**  
   - Pain intensity was a participant-reported, inherently subjective outcome measured on a 0–100-mm visual analogue scale. Such an outcome is especially vulnerable to expectation and performance effects when participants know, or can infer, their assigned intervention.  
   - Effective participant blinding is implausible. Control participants received only hot packs, exercises, myofascial release, and home advice, while TENS and IFT participants additionally received a conspicuous 20-minute electrical treatment with electrodes. The different electrode arrangements for TENS and IFT would also tend to disclose which active modality was received.  
   - The outcome was collected by the principal investigator, who was explicitly both the treating therapist and the assessor. The assessor was therefore unblinded and had an evident interest in the relative performance of the interventions. Even though participants marked a VAS, assessor behaviour, instructions, interaction, and the clinical context can influence subjective pain ratings.  
   - The primary contrasts were assessed immediately after treatment and after a short treatment period. These are precisely the circumstances in which non-specific effects—attention, expectation, novelty of electrical equipment, and desire to please the therapist—may be large.  
   - The authors themselves acknowledge that use of the principal investigator for both procedures and measurements, and absence of a placebo for controls, were limitations. For pain intensity, these limitations are not minor: they create a serious risk that the observed differences reflect biased measurement and expectation effects rather than differential effects of TENS and IFT.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The trial is stated to have been registered with the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry, which is encouraging. However, the registry identification number, registration date, prespecified primary outcome, outcome time points, and prespecified analysis plan are not provided in the manuscript. Consequently, the reported analyses cannot be checked against the registered protocol.  
   - The sample-size calculation was based on improvement in range of motion, not pain intensity. This raises uncertainty about whether pain intensity was a prespecified primary outcome, secondary outcome, or one among several outcomes selected for emphasis after results were known.  
   - Pain was measured at three post-baseline time points, and cervical range of motion was measured in three planes at the same time points. The report presents numerous hypothesis tests, with no stated adjustment for multiplicity across outcomes and time points. The paper emphasizes statistically significant results at all time points, but does not identify a single prespecified primary time point or contrast.  
   - The methods do describe the pain VAS and all stated measurement occasions, and Table 2 reports results for each planned post-baseline time point. Thus, there is no direct evidence that a measured pain time point was omitted. Nevertheless, in the absence of accessible registration/protocol details and a prespecified analytical hierarchy, selective choice of analyses and emphasis remains a legitimate concern.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall rating is **high risk of bias**. The decisive problem is the **high risk of bias in measurement of the subjective pain outcome**, compounded by **high risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions**. Participants were very unlikely to have remained blinded, the control group had no sham electrical treatment or equivalent additional attention, and the same unblinded investigator administered treatment and collected outcomes. These defects are particularly consequential for self-reported pain measured immediately and shortly after treatment.
