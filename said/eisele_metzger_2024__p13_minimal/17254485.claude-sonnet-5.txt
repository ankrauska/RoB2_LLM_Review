# Risk of Bias Assessment: "Embolization combined with EVL for esophagogastric variceal bleeding"
## Outcome assessed: Rate of emergency hemostasis

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: The authors state that "a random number table was produced through Microsoft Excel" and patients were allocated according to this table — this is an acceptable method of sequence generation. Baseline characteristics (Table) are well balanced across multiple variables (sex, age, Child-Pugh score, portal vein width, variceal grade, presence of active bleeding), which is reassuring. However, there is **no description of allocation concealment** — no mention of sealed envelopes, central randomization, or any mechanism preventing the recruiting clinician from knowing/predicting the upcoming allocation before enrolling a patient with active variceal hemorrhage. In an emergency bleeding setting, decisions about eligibility/enrollment timing could be influenced by foreknowledge of allocation. Because concealment is not described, and this is an open-label, unblinded interventional trial, there is a plausible risk that enrollment/assignment could have been manipulated.

---

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: The interventions are procedural and cannot be blinded to the treating physician (this is inherent and expected). More concerning is that in the control group, 10 of 20 patients with active esophageal variceal bleeding whose hemorrhage was not controlled by embolization alone underwent **rescue EVL immediately**, i.e., a deviation from the assigned "staged" protocol (EVL at 2 months). While clinically reasonable/necessary, this deviation is directly relevant to the hemostasis outcome, as it artificially could affect how "emergency hemostasis" is defined/counted (initially failed cases were rescued with the comparator's actual intervention). There is no explicit statement of an intention-to-treat analysis policy, and it is unclear whether these rescued patients were still counted as "hemostasis failures" (87.8%) or reclassified as successes after rescue. This ambiguity in handling protocol deviations that affect the very outcome being measured raises concern.

---

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: LOW RISK**

Justification: All 172 randomized patients (90 therapy, 82 control) appear to be accounted for in the results with no losses to follow-up or exclusions reported for this outcome. Outcome data for emergency hemostasis is complete for both groups.

---

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (rate of emergency hemostasis): SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: This outcome (endoscopically observed cessation of active bleeding) is assessed by the same endoscopist who performed the procedure — there is no mention of an independent, blinded outcome assessor. Given that blinding of participants and personnel is impossible in this open, procedural trial, and that the assessment of "hemostasis" during endoscopy involves an element of clinical/visual judgment (rather than a fully objective, automated measurement), there is potential for assessment bias favoring the therapy group, particularly since the treating physician has a vested interest/expectation in the combined technique. This is not a hard, blinded laboratory endpoint (e.g., mortality or lab value); it relies on real-time subjective interpretation by an unblinded operator.

---

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: No trial registration, published protocol, or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is referenced or available for verification. The manuscript reports the two primary comparative outcomes (emergency hemostasis, early rebleeding) consistently between Methods and Results, and complication data is also reported, arguing against gross selective outcome reporting. However, in the absence of any prospectively registered protocol, it is impossible to confirm that these were the only outcomes measured/analyzed, or that no more favorable time-point/definition of "hemostasis" was selected post hoc, especially given the ambiguous handling of the 10 rescued patients described in the point above.

---

## 6. Overall rating: **HIGH RISK**

Justification: While no single domain reaches an unambiguous "high risk" rating in isolation, there are "some concerns" across **multiple critical domains** — randomization/allocation concealment, deviations from intended intervention (with direct relevance to how the hemostasis outcome was operationalized/counted), unblinded/subjective outcome assessment, and absence of a pre-registered protocol to verify selective reporting. The compounding of these concerns, particularly the unresolved ambiguity regarding how "rescued" control-group patients were counted in the hemostasis endpoint combined with unblinded assessment of an endpoint requiring clinical judgment, substantially lowers confidence in the reported 100% vs 87.8% hemostasis result. I would not accept this result at face value without clarification from the authors and access to a trial protocol.
