**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**
**Justification:** The authors state that "a Microsoft Excel random number generator was used to randomize participants into the two conditions." While the method of sequence generation is adequate, there is no mention of allocation concealment mechanisms (e.g., sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes or centralized allocation). The baseline characteristics (Table 1 and 2) do not indicate significant imbalances, but the lack of explicit information regarding allocation concealment raises some concerns that the assignment could have been foreseen.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**
**Justification:** The study procedures were completed within a single 1-hour testing session in a laboratory. The intervention consisted of the study investigator reading an information sheet alongside each participant individually. Given the very brief, immediate, and highly controlled nature of the intervention setting, there is no evidence to suggest that participants deviated from the intended interventions or that there were issues with non-adherence. 

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk**
**Justification:** The authors report that five participants' data were excluded (two due to age, one missing demographics, and two who did not complete the outcome measures correctly). This leaves a final sample of 88 participants (44 per group). The proportion of missing data is very small (5 out of 93, ~5%) and the reasons provided are not likely to be related to the true outcome. Therefore, missing outcome data does not pose a significant risk of bias.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (List of attributions task (LAT)): high-risk**
**Justification:** The primary outcome (LAT) relies on participant-generated responses. Participants were randomized to receive either information about various causes of daytime fatigue (intervention) or generic sleep information (control) immediately before completing the post-intervention LAT. The participants were inherently aware of the intervention they received (i.e., they were not blinded). Given that the outcome is highly subjective and asks participants to list attributions for fatigue right after being explicitly taught about fatigue attributions, the measurement is highly susceptible to demand characteristics and performance bias. Although the independent coder of the written responses was blinded, the participants generating the data were not, which introduces a high risk of bias in the measurement of this outcome.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**
**Justification:** There is no indication that the study protocol was prospectively registered in a clinical trials registry (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) prior to patient enrollment, nor is a published protocol referenced. While the reported outcomes align with the stated hypotheses and methods within the manuscript, the absence of a pre-specified, publicly available protocol makes it impossible to definitively rule out selective reporting of outcomes or specific analyses (e.g., the exact formulation of the aggregate scores: NumberAttributionScore, FrequencyScore, ImportanceScore).

**6. Overall rating: high-risk**
**Justification:** The study is judged to be at a high risk of bias overall. While missing data and deviations from the intended interventions were adequately handled or negligible, the trial suffers from a high risk of bias in the measurement of the outcome (LAT) due to the subjective nature of the participant-reported task combined with a lack of participant blinding to a highly transparent psychoeducational manipulation. Additionally, there are some concerns regarding the lack of allocation concealment and the absence of a registered protocol.
