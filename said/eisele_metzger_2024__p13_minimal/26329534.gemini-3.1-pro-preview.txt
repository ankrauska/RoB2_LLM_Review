**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: high-risk**
The authors claim that a "random sample" was used and that participants were "randomized" to delivery methods. However, the methodology explicitly states that different commercial databases were used to source the groups: the Medical Marketing Service (MMS) provided the sample for the postal mail and hybrid groups, while Statlistics provided the sample for the email group. Consequently, true randomization from a single population was not performed; instead, different sampling frames were assigned to different interventions. This fundamental flaw is starkly reflected in Table 1, which demonstrates massive baseline imbalances between the groups (e.g., the proportion of Black respondents is 3.2% in the postal mail group vs. 26.4% in the email group; hospital pharmacists make up 19.0% of the postal group vs. 35.0% of the email group). This guarantees a high risk of selection bias, as the groups were fundamentally different from the outset. Furthermore, no details on sequence generation or allocation concealment are provided. 

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**
There is no indication of crossover between the groups or that participants received a delivery method other than the one to which they were assigned. Although some surveys were undeliverable (which is common in this type of research), the delivery methods were strictly implemented according to the source databases. 

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk**
The primary endpoint being evaluated is the "response rate" itself. In this context, whether a participant returns a survey or not is the actual outcome being measured, rather than a missing outcome. The authors clearly defined the denominator (adjusted for undeliverables) and the numerator (fully completed questionnaires). While partial responses were discarded (9.9% in postal and 9.4% in email), this criteria was applied uniformly across groups to calculate the strict completion response rate, leaving little room for bias in missing outcome data regarding the binary metric of "response".

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low-risk**
The measurement of the response rate is a purely objective, binary metric (survey returned completely filled out vs. not returned/incomplete). There is no subjective clinical assessment involved, and the outcome cannot be realistically influenced by the lack of blinding of the investigators or participants to the delivery method. 

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: low-risk**
The reported outcomes (response rate, average response time, and economic cost) are standard, pre-specified metrics for methodological survey research. The results for the response rate are reported comprehensively for all subgroups (stratified by delivery method, length, item placement, and gender) without any evidence of cherry-picking or selective reporting of analyses.

**6. Overall rating: high risk**
The trial is judged to be at high risk of bias overall. This is entirely driven by the critical flaws in the randomization process (Domain 1). Because the investigators sourced the intervention groups from completely different commercial databases rather than randomizing a single unified cohort, the study acts more like a deeply flawed observational comparison than a true randomized controlled trial. The massive baseline demographic imbalances between the groups substantially lower any confidence that the observed differences in response rates are due to the delivery method rather than underlying differences in the populations sampled.
