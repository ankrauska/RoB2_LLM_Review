1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns** (Patients were said to have been stratified by recent self-reported substance/alcohol use and duration of negative urine screens and then “randomized” to the three groups. However, the method used to generate the random sequence—such as a computer-generated sequence or random-number table—is not reported. Crucially, allocation concealment is not described: there is no information about central randomization, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or any other procedure preventing recruiters or staff from foreseeing assignments. Baseline characteristics by randomized group are also not presented, preventing an assessment of whether randomization achieved comparable groups. Of particular concern, 5 of 78 randomized patients withdrew immediately after learning their assigned condition—4 assigned to routine care and 1 assigned to office-based MMM—and were excluded from the reported intent-to-treat sample. This post-randomization, markedly uneven exclusion is not itself proof of defective sequence generation, but it raises concern that the analysed groups no longer faithfully reflect the randomized comparison.)

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: high risk** (The interventions could not realistically be blinded: participants knew whether they had routine clinic care, monthly clinic-based MMM, or monthly physician-office MMM. Knowledge of allocation could affect willingness to remain in treatment, which is the endpoint under review. More importantly, the analysis did not retain all randomized participants in their assigned groups. Although 78 patients were randomized, the stated “intent-to-treat” analysis included only 73 who remained after five immediate post-allocation withdrawals. Four of these exclusions were from routine care, compared with one from office-based MMM and none from clinic-based MMM. For treatment retention, immediate withdrawal after assignment is directly relevant outcome information, not an ignorable pre-treatment event. Excluding these participants, especially disproportionately from routine care, can inflate apparent routine-care retention and bias group comparisons toward no difference. The stepped-care escalation following drug-positive urine or failed recall appears to have been specified as part of the intended treatment strategy and was applied across conditions; that feature is not the principal concern. The serious problem is the non-intention-to-treat exclusion of post-allocation withdrawals.)

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk** (Among the 73 participants included in the authors’ analysis, treatment-retention status at six months appears available for all participants: 19/21 routine-care, 24/27 clinic-based MMM, and 23/25 office-based MMM completed six months, and reasons for the seven departures are described. Retention is generally ascertainable from treatment records, and there is no indication that six-month status was unknown for analysed participants. The five patients excluded after randomization are a serious analysis/assignment problem, addressed under deviations from intended intervention, rather than unexplained missing six-month retention data within the analysed cohort. Nonetheless, their exclusion should not be mistaken for a proper intention-to-treat analysis.)

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—treatment retention: low risk** (Treatment retention is a relatively objective administrative outcome: whether a participant remained in the randomized treatment arm through six months. It is not a subjective participant-reported outcome and is unlikely to be materially distorted by lack of participant blinding. Although staff were presumably aware of treatment assignment, there is no indication that retention status was assessed using subjective judgement, different definitions across groups, or differential surveillance. The reported denominators and completion counts permit verification of the stated six-month retention percentages. The issue is not outcome measurement but the inappropriate exclusion of five allocated participants before the reported analysis.)

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns** (The article explicitly reports only the first six months of an ongoing 12-month trial. Treatment retention was identified in the methods as an intended comparison, and the authors report a survival-analysis comparison with a non-significant result, which reduces concern that only a favourable statistical analysis was selected. However, no trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, prespecified retention definition, or prespecified analysis time points are available in the supplied report. Therefore it cannot be verified whether six-month retention, rather than the planned 12-month retention outcome, was selected for reporting because of the available results. The reporting also labels the analysis as intent-to-treat despite excluding five randomized participants after allocation, which further undermines confidence that the reported result is the prespecified estimand.)

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias** (The result is at high risk of bias principally because five participants randomized after baseline assessment were excluded from the purported intention-to-treat retention analysis after learning their allocation. This exclusion was strongly imbalanced—four routine-care versus one office-based MMM—and is directly capable of biasing the comparison of treatment retention. Additional concerns arise from the absent description of sequence generation/allocation concealment and the lack of protocol or registration information.)
