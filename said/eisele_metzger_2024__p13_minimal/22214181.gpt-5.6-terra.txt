1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - Participants were said to have been randomized using a Microsoft Excel random-number generator. This is potentially an acceptable method of generating an unpredictable sequence, although the manuscript does not specify the Excel function, version, seed, sequence-generation procedure, or whether equal allocation was imposed.  
   - Crucially, there is no description of **allocation concealment**. The investigator conducted the individual sessions and read the allocated information sheet with each participant. It is therefore unclear whether the investigator could know or predict the forthcoming assignment before a participant had irreversibly entered the study.  
   - Baseline characteristics and baseline LAT scores were reportedly similar between groups. This is mildly reassuring, but it cannot establish that allocation was concealed or that the randomization process was adequately protected.  
   - The reported age test is internally suspect—*t*(86) = 2.89 would not ordinarily correspond to *p* = .38—which further reduces confidence in the reported baseline-comparison details, although it does not itself prove defective randomization.  
   - Therefore, the random sequence may have been random, but inadequate reporting of allocation concealment leaves **some concerns**.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The intervention was delivered face-to-face by the investigator, who read either fatigue-cause information or generic sleep information alongside each participant. This makes receipt of the assigned material likely, but there is no documented fidelity assessment: no checklist, recording, independent monitoring, duration data, or confirmation that the materials were delivered consistently.  
   - Participants necessarily knew whether they had just been given a list of non-sleep causes of fatigue or generic information about sleep. No attempt was made to blind participants to the relevant contrast, and no assessment was made of expectations, perceived study purpose, or demand characteristics.  
   - The authors themselves acknowledge that demand characteristics and priming may have influenced post-intervention LAT responses. Their argument that the sleep-information control might also prime sleep attributions does not rule out differential expectancy: the fatigue-information arm was explicitly supplied with candidate responses that the subsequent outcome task invited them to list.  
   - There is no evidence of differential withdrawal or of participants receiving interventions other than those intended. However, the lack of fidelity and expectation assessment means that it remains unclear whether the observed contrast reflects the intended effect of the informational intervention, rather than participants complying with perceived experimental demands.  
   - Accordingly, this domain has **some concerns**, rather than low risk.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - Of the initially recruited participants, five were excluded: two were under 18 years, one lacked demographic information, and two completed outcome measures in a manner that did not permit calculation of a total score. The final analysed sample was 88 participants, 44 per group.  
   - The manuscript implies that all 88 analysed participants contributed to the repeated-measures analyses, as the ANOVA degrees of freedom were 1,86. Thus, missing post-intervention LAT data among analysed participants does not appear substantial.  
   - Nevertheless, the exclusions are not reported by randomized group, nor is it clear at what stage the two unusable outcome measures occurred—before or after randomization, and before or after exposure to the intervention. This matters because inability or unwillingness to complete the LAT could plausibly be associated with the outcome.  
   - The exclusion of a participant for missing demographic information is not explained adequately. It is not self-evident why missing demographics should require exclusion from the outcome analysis.  
   - The overall amount of missing/excluded data is small, so a high-risk judgement would be excessive. But the absence of arm-specific accounting and timing of exclusions prevents a low-risk judgement. Hence, **some concerns**.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: high risk**

   **4a. NumberAttributionScore (proportion of listed attributions classified as sleep-related): high risk**  
   - This outcome was measured immediately after participants in the fatigue-information group had been given explicit examples of non-sleep causes of fatigue—such as caffeine rebound, boredom, inactivity, and post-lunch physiological changes. The subsequent task directly asked them to generate factors that account for their tiredness.  
   - Participants were not blinded to the content of the intervention, and the measure was a subjective, self-generated response task highly vulnerable to priming and demand characteristics. A participant could readily infer that listing the just-presented non-sleep explanations was the expected response.  
   - The short interval between intervention and outcome assessment—within the same one-hour session—makes recall of intervention content especially likely. This is not merely a generic limitation; it creates a direct route by which knowledge of allocation can influence the recorded outcome.  
   - Coding of responses as sleep-related versus non-sleep-related was performed initially by a volunteer blinded to hypotheses, and the investigator reviewed coding while blinded to allocation. This reduces bias in categorising written responses. However, blinded coding cannot remedy bias in what participants chose to write.  
   - The outcome is therefore at **high risk of bias in measurement**.

   **4b. FrequencyScore (rank-based aggregate score for sleep-related attributions): high risk**  
   - Participants ranked their own generated fatigue attributions according to how frequently each accounted for their fatigue. This is a subjective judgement, not an independently observable outcome.  
   - Participants in the fatigue-information condition had just been taught that several non-sleep factors can cause fatigue. They were therefore aware of both their assigned intervention and the direction of the hypothesised change when ranking causes.  
   - The rank outcome is additionally dependent on the earlier self-generated list. If intervention recipients list more non-sleep explanations because they have just been supplied with them, the number and position of sleep-related explanations will mechanically change.  
   - Neither participant blinding nor a credible masking of study purpose was present, and no independent behavioural or real-world assessment corroborated the rankings.  
   - This outcome is consequently at **high risk of bias in measurement**.

   **4c. ImportanceScore (proportion of perceived causal importance allocated to sleep-related attributions): high risk**  
   - Participants allocated percentages of causal importance to the factors they had generated. This is an explicitly subjective, participant-reported assessment.  
   - The fatigue-information intervention specifically instructed participants about alternative causal explanations before they completed this rating for the second time. Thus, it directly supplied content that could alter responses through demand, recall, or compliance rather than through a durable change in personally held attributions.  
   - The authors acknowledge this possibility, but do not provide evidence that it was absent. The stability of the control group does not eliminate the problem, because the fatigue-information group alone was exposed to material that mapped directly onto the requested post-intervention responses.  
   - Blinded coding of categories does not protect this component either, because the percentages were supplied by unblinded participants.  
   - This outcome is at **high risk of bias in measurement**.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - No prospective trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or dated prespecified primary outcome is reported. Therefore, it is not possible to verify that the three LAT-derived outcomes, their scoring rules, the group-by-time interaction tests, and the analytic choices were specified before inspection of the data.  
   - The LAT generated at least three related outcome measures: NumberAttributionScore, FrequencyScore, and ImportanceScore. The manuscript reports statistically significant interaction tests for all three, but there is no stated primary outcome and no adjustment for multiplicity.  
   - The scoring of the LAT involved several analytic decisions: classification of open-ended responses; aggregation of multiple attributions; a ratio-based NumberAttributionScore; a rank transformation capped at scores from 1 to 10; and proportional importance scores. These are potentially reasonable choices, but without a protocol it is impossible to know whether alternative coding or analytic approaches were explored.  
   - The reported analyses appear consistent with the narrative hypotheses, and there is no direct evidence that nonsignificant outcomes were suppressed. However, absence of preregistration and the presence of multiple related outcomes mean that selective reporting cannot be excluded.  
   - This domain is therefore judged as **some concerns**.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The overall rating is **high risk of bias** because the primary endpoint—the List of Attributions Task, including its number, frequency, and importance components—was measured immediately after an unblinded intervention that explicitly provided participants with the alternative attributions they were then asked to generate, rank, and value. This creates a substantial and direct risk that the apparent intervention effect reflects priming or demand characteristics rather than a validly measured change in personal fatigue attributions.  
   - Additional concerns about allocation concealment, incomplete accounting for exclusions, intervention fidelity/expectancy effects, and absence of a prespecified analysis plan further lower confidence in the result.
