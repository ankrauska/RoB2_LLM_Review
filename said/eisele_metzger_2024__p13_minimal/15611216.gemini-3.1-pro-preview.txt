Based on the provided manuscript, here is the step-by-step Risk of Bias assessment for the **Agitation Score** outcome, following the requested criteria:

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**
**Justification:** The authors state that they used "random assignment to groups" but specify that they "randomized participants to study groups in time blocks of 1 week" and "randomized time blocks as treatment or control condition weeks." It is unclear how allocation concealment was achieved or if this block design fully protected against selection bias. Furthermore, the randomization process failed to equalize the groups at baseline, as there was a statistically significant difference in the amount of time spent in arousal imbalance between the experimental and control groups at pretest (p < .012), which the authors had to control for analytically.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: high-risk**
**Justification:** The study suffers from significant deviations from the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. The authors explicitly state that "10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention," meaning that participants were excluded from the analysis post-randomization simply because the staff failed to deliver the intended activity changes (e.g., due to being short-staffed or busy). Excluding non-compliant participants from the analysis disrupts the baseline comparability established by randomization and introduces a high risk of bias in favor of the intervention. 

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high-risk**
**Justification:** Out of 102 initial participants, only 78 were included in the final analysis. Twelve died, 2 were excluded for missing data, and crucially, 10 were deliberately dropped because they did not receive the intervention. The missingness for these 10 individuals is directly related to the intervention (inability of staff to implement the treatment schedule). Because their outcomes are missing and their exclusion is explicitly tied to treatment delivery, there is a high risk that the missing data compromises the true effect estimate.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low-risk**
**Justification:** The primary outcome (agitation) was assessed using a visual analog scale by direct observation. The study explicitly states that "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions." Although the nursing staff implementing the altered schedules could not be blinded, the Research Assistants (RAs) recording the agitation outcomes were blinded to group assignment and underwent rigorous training with high interrater reliability. Thus, the risk of bias in outcome measurement is low.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**
**Justification:** There is no indication that a pre-specified protocol or trial registration was published prior to the study's commencement. Although the ANCOVA analysis and handling of covariates (arousal imbalance and MMSE) are explained in the text, without a pre-published analysis plan, it is impossible to verify whether the choice of covariates, data transformations, or specific subgroup comparisons were selected post hoc based on the data. 

**6. Overall rating: high-risk**
**Justification:** The trial is judged to be at high risk of bias overall. This is primarily driven by the post-randomization exclusion of participants who did not receive the assigned intervention (a severe deviation from intention-to-treat principles) and the resulting missing outcome data directly tied to the failure of treatment implementation. Additionally, there are some concerns regarding the baseline imbalances caused by the randomization process and the lack of a pre-registered protocol.
