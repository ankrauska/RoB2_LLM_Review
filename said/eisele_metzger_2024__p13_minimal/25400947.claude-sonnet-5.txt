# Risk of Bias Assessment: Frequency of Delivery After 48 Hours
## Sir Ian Chalmers, Clinical Trials Reviewer

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: HIGH RISK**

Justification: The authors state only that patients were "divided into two groups by simple random sampling" — no description of the random sequence generation method (computer-generated, random number table, etc.) is provided, and there is no mention whatsoever of allocation concealment. Given that one arm requires IV cannulation/infusion and the other oral capsules, concealment prior to allocation would have been essential to prevent selective enrollment or channeling of patients into a preferred arm, yet nothing is reported. Compounding this concern, the baseline tables show suspiciously exact symmetry between groups (identical history of preterm labor: 12/12, 4.0%/4.0%; near-identical parity distributions: 54/54 for parity 2, 6/6 for parity 3), which raises questions about whether "simple random sampling" was truly random or was manipulated/post-hoc balanced. This is a serious concern for this specific endpoint since imbalance in unmeasured prognostic factors could easily confound a treatment-response outcome like delivery timing.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: HIGH RISK**

Justification: The trial is described as "single-blinded," but the interventions are fundamentally unblindable — one group receives intravenous MgSO4 with intensive monitoring (deep tendon reflexes, urine output, respiratory rate) while the other receives oral celecoxib with different monitoring (nausea, rash, headache). Clinicians and likely patients were aware of group assignment. This creates high potential for differential co-intervention, differential threshold for determining "arrest of contractions" or decision to discontinue the drug, and differential clinical management — all of which could directly affect whether delivery occurred before or after 48 hours. No mention of a per-protocol vs intention-to-treat analysis strategy, and the statement "if uterine preterm contractions did not stop... drug has been stopped" suggests deviations occurred but were not systematically analyzed as such.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: LOW RISK**

Justification: All 600 randomized women (300 per group) appear to be accounted for in Table 1 with complete outcome data (delivery before/after 48 hours). No dropouts, exclusions post-randomization, or losses to follow-up are reported for this endpoint.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (Frequency of delivery after 48h): SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: Delivery timing itself is an objective, verifiable clinical fact (relatively resistant to bias in direct measurement). However, this is not a fully "hard" endpoint in this context — the decision of whether/when to discontinue tocolysis, diagnose "failure" of treatment, or escalate management was made by unblinded clinicians aware of treatment allocation, and these decisions could plausibly influence the timing of delivery (e.g., a clinician's differential comfort continuing observation on one drug vs. the other). Without blinded outcome assessors or a prespecified, objective, non-modifiable trigger for delivery, I cannot rate this as low risk.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: SOME CONCERNS**

Justification: No trial registration number, protocol, or prospectively published statistical analysis plan is referenced anywhere in the manuscript. It is therefore impossible to confirm that "delivery after 48 hours" was a pre-specified primary/secondary outcome rather than one of several outcomes analyzed post hoc (alongside effacement, dilatation, gestational age, duration of drug use) with the possibility of selective emphasis on non-significant comparisons to support the paper's conclusion of "equivalence." The absence of any registration or protocol reference is a notable transparency failure for a 600-patient RCT.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **HIGH RISK**

**Justification:** This endpoint suffers from serious, compounding concerns: an inadequately described and possibly compromised randomization/concealment process (Domain 1 — High), an unblindable intervention with plausible differential co-management (Domain 2 — High), and additional unresolved concerns regarding outcome-assessment blinding (Domain 4) and absence of any protocol/registration to verify outcome pre-specification (Domain 5). Per the stated rules, a single "high risk" domain — reinforced here by two — is sufficient to warrant an overall "high risk" judgment. I would not accept this trial's effect estimate for this outcome at face value without independent verification of the randomization procedure and access to a trial protocol.
