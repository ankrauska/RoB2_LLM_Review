**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**
The manuscript reports on the long-term follow-up of the PORTEC-2 trial. While the specific details of the randomization sequence generation and allocation concealment are not explicitly restated in this paper, it references the primary publications of this well-established multicenter trial which utilized adequate central randomization and allocation concealment procedures. There are no imbalances in baseline characteristics that would suggest a failure of randomization.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some-concerns**
Given the nature of the interventions (external beam radiation therapy versus vaginal brachytherapy), blinding of the participants and healthcare providers was not possible. While there is no clear evidence that non-protocol interventions were administered unevenly between the groups, the lack of blinding in a trial assessing quality of life and subjective symptom endpoints leaves open the possibility that patients' expectations or differential co-interventions could have influenced the trajectory of care. 

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high-risk**
There is a substantial amount of missing data in this long-term follow-up. Only 348 of the original 427 randomized patients (81%) provided baseline and at least one follow-up questionnaire. At the 7-year mark, the response rate among those alive and eligible was 77% (205/265), and it fell further to 67% (80/119) at 10 years. Importantly, the authors acknowledge that responders in this analysis were significantly younger and had fewer comorbidities than the overall trial population. Since the missingness is quite high and clearly related to the health status of the patients (and therefore likely related to their true Quality of Life and EORTC QLQ-C30 Global health scores), this poses a high risk of bias.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: high-risk**
The endpoint evaluated is the EORTC QLQ-C30 Global health score, which is a strictly subjective, participant-reported outcome. Because the participants were entirely unblinded to their assigned radiation modality (EBRT vs. VBT), their knowledge of the received treatment and its widely known side effect profiles (such as gastrointestinal toxicity from EBRT) is highly likely to influence their perception and reporting of their global health and quality of life. This lack of blinding inherently introduces a high risk of measurement bias for subjective PROs.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: low-risk**
The analyses of the EORTC QLQ-C30 global health scale and its subscales follow standardized EORTC Quality of Life Group guidelines. The authors reported the results for the predefined scales comprehensively, and there is no indication that the reported data were selected from multiple available outcome measurements or analysis methods to favor a specific conclusion. 

**6. Overall rating: high-risk**
The overall risk of bias for the EORTC QLQ-C30 Global health endpoint is judged to be high. This is primarily driven by the unblinded nature of the trial severely impacting this subjective, patient-reported outcome (Measurement of the outcome), combined with substantial and differential attrition over the extended follow-up period (Missing outcome data).
