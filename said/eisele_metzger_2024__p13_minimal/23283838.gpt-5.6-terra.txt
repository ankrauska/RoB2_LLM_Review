1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - Participants were said to be “randomly assigned” to 250 mg DCS or matching placebo, and allocation was undertaken by a data manager with no participant contact. This separation is reassuring and reduces the likelihood that recruiting or treating staff manipulated assignment.  
   - DCS and placebo were supplied in identical-appearing capsules. Participants and experimenters were apparently unable to guess allocation better than chance after Visit 1, supporting successful masking.  
   - However, the report does not state how the random sequence was generated (for example, computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, blocking, or stratification). “Randomized” alone is insufficient to establish that the sequence was genuinely random.  
   - Nor is the allocation-concealment mechanism described in adequate operational detail. The involvement of an independent data manager is encouraging, but it is not reported whether assignments were held in a secure system, pharmacy-controlled list, consecutively numbered containers, or another mechanism preventing foreknowledge of the next allocation.  
   - Baseline imbalance in age (DCS group older; p = .019) is compatible with chance in this very small trial, but it also gives no added reassurance about the randomization process.  

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**  
   - The study used matching placebo capsules, and both participants and experimenters were blinded. The reported formal assessment of masking found that neither group could identify treatment allocation, which makes differential behaviour, co-intervention, encouragement, or outcome-related interaction due to known assignment less likely.  
   - The intervention appears to have been administered as intended: a single 250-mg DCS or placebo capsule immediately before preparation for the first exposure speech. No crossovers, dosing errors, unblinding events, or post-randomization changes to medication assignment are reported.  
   - The exposure was individualized—speech duration, preparation time, use of notes, topic, and experimenter demeanour could vary—to achieve a target anxiety level. This introduces variability in the behavioural intervention. However, this tailoring was part of the intended assay procedure, was apparently undertaken under a common protocol, and should have been implemented without knowledge of treatment allocation. It is therefore not, on the reported information, evidence of bias from deviations attributable to trial context.  
   - The investigators analysed participants according to assigned DCS/placebo group among those with outcome data. Although exclusion of non-returners is problematic for missing-data bias, there is no indication that analysed participants received an intervention other than that assigned.  

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - Four of 34 randomized participants (12%) did not return for Visit 2 and were excluded from *all* analyses. Thus, the primary endpoint—between-session habituation—was unavailable for these participants.  
   - Attrition was numerically balanced: 2/18 in DCS and 2/16 in placebo. Reported reasons were also superficially balanced: one unreachable and one declining further participation in each group. This reduces, but does not eliminate, concern that missingness differed by allocation.  
   - Crucially, the authors explicitly chose not to conduct an intention-to-treat analysis: “Only those participants completing Visit 2 are analyzed.” No imputation, sensitivity analysis, best/worst-case analysis, or analysis assessing whether results were robust to plausible outcomes among the four missing participants was reported.  
   - In a small study, the observed primary result rests on 12 DCS and 14 placebo completers. The categorical response comparison is based on small numbers, so outcomes in only one or two missing participants could plausibly affect the estimated treatment effect and perhaps the statistical conclusion.  
   - There is no evidence that missingness depended on the unobserved outcome or differed materially by treatment, but the available information is insufficient to conclude that it did not.  

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — between-session habituation: low risk**  
   - The endpoint was defined as a categorical reduction of at least 5 points in average speech-related Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS) ratings from Visit 1 to Visit 2. SUDS is a participant-reported, subjective measure of anxiety/distress and is therefore potentially susceptible to expectation and performance bias if participants know their allocation.  
   - Here, participants received identical capsules and the masking assessment indicated that they could not discern DCS versus placebo. This is particularly important for a subjective outcome such as distress.  
   - Experimenters were also blinded, reducing the risk that their conduct, prompts, interpretations, or recording of ratings differed by group.  
   - Ratings were obtained repeatedly at specified points before, during, and at the end of each speech, and the average across available ratings was used. The same type of speech exposure was repeated at the second visit. These procedures provide a reasonably consistent measurement approach across groups.  
   - Some measurement variability remains because speech parameters were deliberately tailored to each participant and could vary in duration and other features. However, the tailoring was designed to standardize anxiety intensity, was part of the planned intervention, and there is no indication that it was influenced by knowledge of allocation.  
   - On the evidence reported, lack of knowledge of treatment assignment makes differential measurement of this subjective endpoint unlikely.  

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The manuscript identifies between-session habituation, defined as a ≥5-point reduction in average SUDS across visits, as the primary measure. It reports the corresponding categorical comparison (75% DCS versus 36% placebo; p = .030). This is preferable to introducing an entirely unannounced endpoint after analysis.  
   - Nevertheless, no prospectively dated protocol, statistical analysis plan, or trial-registration entry is provided. Consequently, it is not possible to verify that the categorical SUDS endpoint, the 5-point response threshold, the averaging method, the timing of analyses, and the choice to analyse completers only were specified before outcome data were examined.  
   - The study assessed several related outcomes and analytic forms: categorical SUDS response, continuous average SUDS change, repeated SUDS measurements within speeches, and Social Phobia Scale change. The continuous SUDS group-by-time interaction—the more direct continuous analysis of the stated distress outcome—was not statistically significant (p = .14), whereas the categorical responder analysis was significant.  
   - The report also presents a statistically significant group-by-time result for the Social Phobia Scale (p = .023), although this is described as a generalization measure rather than the stated primary between-session-habituation endpoint. In the absence of a protocol or analysis plan, the scope for selecting among related outcomes and analyses is a concern.  
   - The available report therefore does not allow confidence that the reported statistically significant categorical result was selected without knowledge of the data.  

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   - The study has important strengths: matching placebo, apparently successful masking of participants and experimenters, and balanced recorded attrition.  
   - However, there are some concerns about insufficiently described sequence generation/allocation concealment, complete-case analysis despite missing primary outcomes, and inability to verify prespecification of the categorical primary analysis and response threshold.  
   - No domain is definitively high risk on the information available, but the combined concerns reduce confidence in the reported DCS effect on between-session habituation.
