# Risk of Bias Assessment: "Perception of Pain During Orthodontic Treatment with Fixed Appliances"
## Outcome: Initial Pain

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: Some concerns**
Justification: The manuscript states patients were "randomly selected" for insertion of the 0.014 or 0.016 inch wire, but provides no details on the randomization method (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random number tables), no description of allocation concealment, and no information on who performed the randomization or whether the clinician placing the wire was aware of the allocation in advance. Given that wire size is visually and tactilely distinguishable by the treating clinician at the point of insertion, and no concealment mechanism is described, there is a real risk that allocation could have been influenced or predicted. Baseline group sizes (56 vs 53) are reasonably balanced, which is somewhat reassuring, but this alone does not confirm adequate concealment. This is a subjective, self-reported outcome (initial pain), making randomization integrity particularly important.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: Some concerns**
Justification: This was not a blinded trial (blinding is not mentioned, and by nature of the intervention, blinding of participants/clinicians to wire size is difficult though not impossible). No information is given on whether patients were aware of which wire size they received, whether care providers who interacted with patients (potentially influencing symptom reporting) were blinded, or whether any co-interventions (e.g., verbal reassurance, differential handling) varied by group. There is no explicit statement that analysis was by intention-to-treat, though this is less relevant here as no crossovers are described. The lack of blinding combined with a subjective outcome (patient-reported pain perception) raises concern that expectation or differential clinician-patient interaction could have influenced reporting of initial pain.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: Low risk**
Justification: The manuscript explicitly states "The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned at the following appointment." This indicates 100% completion for the outcome data collection instrument, with no dropouts reported for the initial pain outcome specifically. No participants appear to be excluded from the initial pain analysis (Table 1 accounts for all patients including the "no pain" category).

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (Initial Pain): High risk**
Justification: Initial pain was a patient-reported outcome, assessed via a questionnaire (with a categorical time-point choice for "initial pain" and, per the Methods, potentially linked to VAS scoring). This is inherently subjective. Critically:
- There is no evidence that participants were blinded to which wire size (0.014 vs 0.016 inch) they received. Patients could plausibly discern wire thickness or be informed inadvertently by staff.
- No description of who administered/explained the questionnaire, raising the possibility of differential encouragement or explanation between groups.
- The assessors of the "outcome" are the patients themselves, and knowledge of allocation (even if by inference or communication) could influence self-reported pain onset time, a well-documented source of bias in unblinded subjective-outcome trials.
- No independent, objective corroboration of pain onset time was used.
Given the subjective nature of this outcome and the total absence of blinding procedures, this domain is rated high risk.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: Some concerns**
Justification: No trial registration, published protocol, or a priori statistical analysis plan is referenced or available for review. The outcomes reported (time of initial pain, duration, region, self-medication, effect on daily life, gender effects) appear comprehensive and are pre-specified in the aims section, which is reassuring. However:
- Without a registered protocol, it cannot be verified whether all pre-specified outcomes were reported, whether analyses (e.g., choice of non-parametric tests due to non-normality) were prespecified or determined post hoc after inspecting data distribution, or whether subgroup analyses (e.g., by gender, by arch) were pre-planned or exploratory.
- The initial pain outcome itself was measured at discrete time points to the first hour, and it is unclear whether this level of granularity was specified a priori or whether alternative cut points were tried.
Given the absence of any protocol/registry evidence, but no overt sign of selective reporting for this particular outcome, this is rated as "some concerns" rather than "high risk."

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** While randomization procedures and reporting-result selection raise "some concerns," and missing data is low risk, the outcome measurement domain is rated high risk due to the fundamentally subjective nature of "initial pain" being self-reported by unblinded participants in an unblinded trial. Per the stated rules, a "high risk" rating in even one domain (here, outcome measurement) is sufficient to classify the overall risk of bias for this outcome as high risk. This is compounded by the additional "some concerns" in randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and selection of reported result, further lowering confidence in the reported result for initial pain perception.
