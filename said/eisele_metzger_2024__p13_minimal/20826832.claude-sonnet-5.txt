# Risk of Bias Assessment: Compliance with Mammogram Screening

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: Low risk**
Justification: The randomization sequence was generated using a computerized random number generator, and allocation was stratified by age (50-59; 60-70). Critically, the complete baseline database was sent to an independent coordination center (Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center) in the US to perform the actual allocation to intervention arms — this represents robust allocation concealment, as local investigators could not have foreknowledge of or influence over assignment. Baseline characteristics were well balanced across groups (Table 1), consistent with successful randomization. While participant/provider blinding was not possible, this is a feature of the intervention type and not a flaw in the randomization/concealment process itself.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: Some concerns**
Justification: By necessity, participants and interventionists were not blinded to allocation. The authors themselves acknowledge a risk of contamination — control group participants, aware of the study, "could have demanded more information than usual," potentially inflating control-arm screening rates. Although the authors argue this would bias toward the null (conservative), this is a deviation from the intended "standard care only" condition that was not measured or verified. No formal assessment of contamination or protocol fidelity in the control arm was reported. Analysis was appropriately intention-to-treat, which mitigates but does not eliminate concern.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: Low risk**
Justification: The primary outcome (mammography screening) was ascertained via electronic clinical records, which were reviewed for essentially all 500 randomized women regardless of survey completion, and analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis with baseline denominators. Loss to follow-up for the self-reported survey was modest (7.6% overall) and balanced across arms (6.6%, 7.8%, 8.4%), with similar reasons for attrition. No differential or outcome-dependent missingness is apparent.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome:**

- *Electronic record outcome (primary analysis):* **Low risk.** This is an objective outcome ascertained from clinic/radiology electronic databases. Staff at the clinic and radiology unit performing/recording mammograms were explicitly blinded to allocation group, and the data analysts at the coordination center were also blinded. This substantially reduces the risk of differential outcome ascertainment.

- *Self-reported outcome:* **High risk.** Compliance with mammography was also measured by self-report, a participant-reported outcome. Participants were not blinded to their allocation (nor could they be, given the nature of mail/phone/home-visit contacts), creating a strong potential for social-desirability bias and differential recall — intervention-arm participants who received intensive contact might over-report compliance, while control participants might under- or over-report. This self-reported measure is therefore vulnerable to bias driven by lack of blinding.

Overall for this outcome domain, since the primary conclusions rely on the blinded electronic record data, this component is low risk, but the parallel self-report measure introduces high risk of bias for that specific instrument.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: Some concerns**
Justification: No trial registration number or published protocol is referenced in the manuscript, making it impossible to fully verify that the primary outcome, analysis plan, and subgroups were pre-specified rather than chosen post hoc. The stated primary outcome (mammography compliance) and sample size calculation are reported prospectively in the Methods and match the Results, which is reassuring. However, the paper reports multiple outcome sources (self-report and electronic records) and additional belief/attitude outcomes (Table 3) without clear pre-specification of primary versus secondary status, and there is no accessible protocol/registry to confirm the analysis was not adapted after seeing the data.

## 6. Overall rating: **High risk**

Justification: While randomization/concealment and missing data domains are low risk, there are compounding concerns: (a) unverified contamination risk between arms due to lack of blinding (deviations from intended intervention), (b) high risk of bias in the self-reported compliance measure due to lack of participant blinding on a subjective/behavior-report outcome, and (c) inability to confirm outcome/analysis pre-specification due to absence of a trial registry or protocol. Although the electronic-record-based primary outcome is comparatively well protected (blinded assessors, ITT, low attrition), the combination of unaddressed contamination potential and the reliance on a self-reported outcome measure alongside an unregistered protocol substantially lowers overall confidence in the robustness of the reported effect sizes, warranting a "high risk" overall judgment rather than merely "some concerns."
