# Risk of Bias Assessment: Effect of Add-on Gabapentin on Opioid Withdrawal Symptoms

## Endpoint: Severity of subjective withdrawal symptoms using the Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS)

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process:**
**Some concerns** (justification: The manuscript states subjects were "assigned randomly" using "a randomization automated system," which suggests adequate sequence generation. However, no details are provided regarding allocation concealment mechanisms — specifically, who generated the randomization sequence, who enrolled participants, and whether the personnel assigning participants to groups were separate from those enrolling them. Baseline SOWS scores also show a numerical imbalance (Gabapentin mean 12.6 vs Placebo mean 14.9 at baseline), which, while not necessarily indicative of failed randomization, raises questions given the small sample size (n=40) and lack of statistical testing reported for baseline SOWS specifically. The double-blind design with identical capsules is reassuring for blinding integrity, but insufficient information on concealment during allocation prevents a "low risk" rating.)

---

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions:**
**Some concerns** (justification: Three patients in the placebo group were excluded post-randomization due to non-compliance and self-medication with illicit/OTC drugs — this represents a differential deviation from intended intervention between arms (0 in gabapentin group vs. 3 in placebo group). Critically, the analysis approach is unclear — it is not stated whether an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was used or whether these participants were simply excluded from analysis (a per-protocol approach). This differential dropout, likely related to the severity of withdrawal symptoms itself, is a form of informative dropout that could bias the outcome measurement. Additionally, symptomatic medications (clonidine, oxazepam, trazodone) were permitted "if required" — this represents a co-intervention that could differentially mask withdrawal symptom severity between groups if administered unevenly, and while the manuscript states no significant difference in doses between groups, this was not confirmed with a formal pre-specified analysis plan for this comparison.)

---

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data:**
**Some concerns** (justification: By Day 18, the gabapentin group had complete data (n=20) while the placebo group had only 17 of 20 participants (15% missing), a differential attrition rate of 15% versus 0%. The reason for missing data (non-compliance/self-medication, itself likely driven by symptom severity) is such that participants dropping out are highly likely to have different (worse) outcomes than those remaining — this is informative missingness directly related to the outcome being measured. There is no evidence of imputation methods (e.g., last-observation-carried-forward, multiple imputation) to handle this differential missingness, and the repeated-measures ANOVA analysis appears to use only observed cases at each timepoint, which can bias the estimated treatment effect, particularly given the small sample size.)

---

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (SOWS - subjective/patient-reported outcome):**
**Low risk** (justification: This is a patient-reported subjective outcome (self-rated withdrawal symptom severity), and blinding of participants and outcome assessors is a critical determinant of bias risk for such outcomes. The study employed a double-blind design with identical-appearing gabapentin and placebo capsules dispensed in the same manner and number to both groups, minimizing the risk that patients could distinguish their treatment allocation based on physical characteristics of the intervention. There is no explicit statement regarding assessment of blinding success (e.g., no post-trial unblinding questionnaire to confirm patients/raters could not guess allocation), which is a minor limitation, but no signal of unblinding is evident in the text. Given the robust attempt at blinding and no reported deviations from this blinding, risk of bias in outcome measurement itself is low.)

---

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result:**
**Some concerns** (justification: No trial registration number or protocol reference is provided in the manuscript, making it impossible to verify whether the outcome measurement time-points, specific SOWS subscale analyses, and statistical methods (repeated-measures ANOVA, Mann-Whitney U-test) were pre-specified or determined post hoc. The use of multiple statistical tests (repeated-measures ANOVA for overall comparison AND Mann-Whitney U-test for total score) without clear a priori justification raises the possibility of selective analytical reporting. Additionally, Table 3 presents symptom-by-symptom breakdowns highlighting specific symptoms (dysphoria, muscle spasm, irritability, craving) as showing directional trends favoring gabapentin, despite the overall non-significant result — this selective emphasis on individual symptoms with favorable trends, discussed at length despite non-significance, suggests a pattern consistent with selective outcome reporting/emphasis bias. The absence of any registered protocol prevents verification of whether all six timepoints and analyses were pre-specified.)

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **HIGH RISK**

**Justification:** While no single domain was rated as definitively "high risk" in isolation, multiple domains (randomization process, deviations from intended intervention, missing outcome data, and selection of reported results) were rated as "some concerns." Critically, the concerns in domains 2 and 3 are interconnected and mutually reinforcing: the differential dropout in the placebo group (driven by symptom severity) combined with unclear analysis handling (ITT vs. per-protocol) creates a substantial risk that the true effect of gabapentin on SOWS scores has been biased — likely underestimating the treatment effect if severely symptomatic placebo patients were removed from analysis, or overestimating it if their poor outcomes were simply omitted. Combined with the lack of a registered protocol to verify outcome reporting integrity (domain 5), and the small sample size (n=40) which amplifies the impact of any of these biases, the cumulative effect substantially lowers confidence in the finding of "no significant difference" between gabapentin and placebo for this subjective withdrawal symptom outcome. This pattern of multiple "some concerns" domains that are mechanistically linked and directionally consistent warrants an overall "high risk" judgment rather than "some concerns."
