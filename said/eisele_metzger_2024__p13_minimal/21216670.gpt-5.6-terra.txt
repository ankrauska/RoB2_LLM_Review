**Endpoint assessed: Progression in the Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale (FMMS) from baseline to day 90 (3 months).**

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
The random allocation sequence was generated by computer, using centre-balanced blocks of four. This is a genuinely random method of sequence generation. Allocation was reportedly concealed using sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, with allocation undertaken by the coordinating-centre pharmacist according to the randomisation list. The active and placebo capsules were identical in appearance and packaging, and participants, carers, site staff, investigators, and outcome assessors were stated to be masked.

There were modest baseline imbalances: the fluoxetine group was older (mean 66.4 vs 62.9 years), had more previous strokes (17% vs 7%), and had a somewhat higher baseline FMMS score (17.1 vs 13.4). Such differences are quite compatible with chance in a trial of only 118 participants and do not, by themselves, demonstrate a failure of randomisation or concealment. The reported method is adequate, and no persuasive evidence indicates that allocation was predictable or manipulated.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
The trial was designed as a double-blind, placebo-controlled comparison of fluoxetine 20 mg daily versus placebo for 90 days. Reported adherence was high and similar between groups: mean cumulative doses were 88.3 and 87.4 tablets, respectively, and relative dose intensity was approximately 94% in both groups. Thus, there is no evidence of important non-adherence to assigned trial treatment.

However, two issues warrant concern:

1. **Rehabilitation was not standardised.** All participants received physiotherapy, but physiotherapists were instructed to follow each centre’s usual practice rather than a common rehabilitation protocol. Since rehabilitation intensity and content are likely to affect motor recovery materially, this creates scope for co-intervention differences. Adjustment for centre does not ensure equivalence of rehabilitation received by individuals within or across centres. There is, however, no direct evidence that rehabilitation differed systematically by randomised group.

2. **Protocol-permitted open-label fluoxetine for incident depression could contaminate the comparison.** If depression developed, clinicians were instructed to continue study treatment and, if necessary, give open-label fluoxetine. Consequently, participants allocated placebo could receive 20 mg fluoxetine daily, whereas those allocated fluoxetine could receive 40 mg daily. Depression occurred much more often in the placebo group (17/59, 29%) than in the fluoxetine group (4/59, 7%). The number actually given open-label fluoxetine is not reported. This is a potentially important post-randomisation deviation from the intended placebo comparison, related to a post-randomisation clinical event that may itself be associated with motor recovery. The analysis did not clearly estimate an effect under a defined strategy for this treatment switching or contamination.

These deviations probably tend to dilute, rather than exaggerate, a direct drug-versus-placebo contrast, but their extent and consequences for FMMS progression cannot be adequately judged.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
Of 118 randomised participants, 113 (96%) were included in the day-90 “full analysis set”: 57/59 (97%) in the fluoxetine group and 56/59 (95%) in the placebo group. Thus, missing primary-outcome data were limited in quantity and only slightly imbalanced.

The five exclusions comprised two deaths (one per group) and three withdrawals: one in the fluoxetine group because of severe hypoxia, and two in the placebo group because of kidney tumour and pulmonary embolism. Death and serious intercurrent illness after stroke are plausibly associated with poor functional and motor outcomes. The primary analysis was described as including “all patients for whom data were available,” which is a complete-case analysis rather than a true intention-to-treat analysis incorporating all randomised participants.

Although the absolute amount of missing data is small and deaths were balanced, the report provides no imputation, no analysis assigning poor outcomes to deaths, and no sensitivity analysis exploring plausible outcomes for those missing. Therefore, it is not possible to exclude some bias due to missing data, though a large effect of the missing data is unlikely.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—Progression in the Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale: low-risk**  
The FMMS is a clinician-administered performance scale for post-stroke motor impairment, ranging from 0 to 100. It involves assessment of observable motor tasks rather than participant self-report of a subjective symptom. The manuscript states that all motor assessments were performed by physiotherapists at baseline, day 30, and day 90, and that patients, carers, site staff, investigators, and physicians assessing outcomes were masked to treatment allocation.

The placebo was identical in appearance and packaging to fluoxetine, supporting maintenance of blinding. Although fluoxetine-related adverse effects—particularly nausea or diarrhoea—could potentially have suggested allocation for some participants or staff, there is no report that blinding was broken, no evidence that assessors knew treatment assignments, and no indication of differential outcome-assessment procedures between groups.

FMMS assessment necessarily contains some assessor judgement, but it is a structured scale with established inter-rater reliability. In the context of apparently masked assessors and a matched placebo, bias in measurement of FMMS progression is unlikely.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
The trial was registered (NCT00657163), but registration occurred after recruitment had started: recruitment began in March 2005, whereas the study was first posted in April 2008. Retrospective registration limits confidence that all outcome and analysis decisions were specified before knowledge of accumulating trial data.

The registered primary outcome was “Progression in the Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale” at 3 months, which is consistent with the published primary endpoint: change in FMMS between baseline and day 90. This correspondence is reassuring.

Nevertheless, the registry entry does not provide a sufficiently detailed prespecified statistical analysis plan for the primary endpoint. In the publication, several analyses are presented: unadjusted day-90 comparisons, change-score analyses, adjusted linear regression, FMMS total score, and upper- and lower-limb subscores. The adjusted primary analysis controlled for centre and for baseline factors “that showed treatment group imbalance,” specifically age, previous stroke, and baseline FMMS. This covariate-selection approach appears data-dependent and is not shown to have been prespecified. The main conclusion relies on the adjusted estimate of FMMS change, while alternative analytic choices and handling of missing data are not fully reported.

There is no compelling evidence that the prespecified primary outcome itself was substituted or selectively omitted, but retrospective registration and insufficiently prespecified analytic details leave some concern about selective selection among analyses.

**6. Overall rating: some concerns**  
The overall risk of bias for progression in FMMS at day 90 is **some concerns**. Randomisation and blinded outcome measurement appear sound. However, confidence is reduced by: (a) potentially consequential treatment contamination through protocol-permitted open-label fluoxetine for depression, particularly among placebo-assigned participants; (b) complete-case analysis with outcome data missing for deaths and serious illnesses; and (c) retrospective registration and insufficiently prespecified analysis details. These issues do not establish a high risk of bias, but they prevent a low-risk judgement.
