**Endpoint: Change in photoaging** (change in hand-skin microtopography grade from 1992 to 1996). The principal result is for daily versus discretionary sunscreen use; the factorial β-carotene versus placebo comparison is also relevant to this endpoint.

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - Participants were said to have been assigned using a computer-generated random list by an investigator “who had no knowledge of the participants.” This is credible evidence of a random sequence-generation method.  
   - However, the report does not adequately describe **allocation concealment**. It is not stated who held the randomization list, how assignments were implemented, or whether recruiting/enrolling personnel could foresee the next assignment. The investigator’s lack of knowledge of participants is not equivalent to concealment of upcoming assignments.  
   - Baseline characteristics were broadly similar between sunscreen groups and between β-carotene and placebo groups. This provides some reassurance that randomization worked in practice, although it cannot establish adequate concealment. There was a modest numerical imbalance in the factorial β-carotene allocation (447 vs 439 eligible participants; reported baseline allocation imbalance P = 0.06), which is not compelling evidence of a failure of randomization.  
   - **Judgement:** Random sequence generation appears acceptable, but the absence of a clear description of allocation concealment leaves some concern. This applies to both factorial comparisons.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - **Daily versus discretionary sunscreen:** This comparison could not realistically be blinded because no placebo sunscreen was used, reportedly for ethical reasons. Participants knew whether they had been instructed to apply sunscreen daily or to use it at their discretion.  
   - Adherence differed substantially, as expected: 77% of participants allocated to daily sunscreen reported using sunscreen at least 3–4 days per week at follow-up, compared with 33% in the discretionary group. Thus, there was nonadherence in the daily-use group and considerable sunscreen use in the discretionary group. These deviations tend principally to dilute the contrast between assigned strategies in an intention-to-treat analysis, rather than necessarily bias it in favour of daily sunscreen.  
   - Knowledge of allocation could nevertheless have altered other sun-protective practices or sun-exposure behaviour. The authors report similar self-reported outdoor exposure, shade seeking, and hat use between sunscreen groups. This is reassuring, but these were self-reported measures and cannot exclude differential behavioural changes.  
   - The analysis was according to random allocation, appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to a daily-sunscreen strategy. It should not be interpreted as a clean estimate of the biological effect of perfectly adherent daily sunscreen use.  
   - **β-Carotene versus placebo:** Tablets were placebo-controlled, and adherence was similar between groups (68% vs 67% taking at least 80% of tablets). This comparison is less vulnerable to deviations caused by knowledge of allocation. Skin reflectance supported separation in β-carotene exposure.  
   - **Judgement:** Some concerns overall, driven chiefly by the necessarily unblinded sunscreen comparison and the potential for unmeasured behavioural co-interventions. The risk is lower for the β-carotene comparison.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - Missingness was appreciable. Of 886 eligible participants with at least one good-quality replica, only 604 had outcome measurements at both baseline and 1996. A further 213 had baseline only and 69 had follow-up only. Thus, approximately one third contributed only one measurement.  
   - The primary analysis used generalized estimating equations with all available outcome observations rather than restricting analysis to complete cases. This can be reasonable, but validity depends on assumptions about the missing-data mechanism.  
   - The authors investigated predictors of missingness. Missing follow-up microtopography grade was not associated with baseline microtopography grade, and treatment allocation was reportedly not associated with missing grades. These findings lessen concern that missingness directly depended on the observed baseline endpoint or differed by intervention group.  
   - However, participants with only one measurement were more likely to have severe clinical photoaging of the neck and to report 2–5 prior sunburns. These characteristics plausibly relate to future photoaging. The authors adjusted for these variables and reported that complete-case and multiple-imputation analyses produced materially similar findings. This is useful, but the details of those sensitivity analyses, including their numerical results and the plausibility of the imputation model, are limited in the report.  
   - **Judgement:** The available checks and sensitivity analyses are reassuring, but the quantity of missing paired outcome data is substantial and cannot be dismissed. Some concerns remain.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low risk**  
   - **Outcome assessed: change in photoaging measured by skin-surface microtopography grade from silicone replicas of the back of the left hand.**  
   - The endpoint was based on physical silicone impressions rather than participant self-report. This is particularly important because sunscreen allocation was unblinded to participants; subjective perceptions of aging therefore did not determine the endpoint.  
   - Replicas were graded by experienced assessors who were explicitly unaware of treatment allocation. Blinding of outcome assessors is the critical safeguard here and appears to have been implemented.  
   - The grading scale was ordinal (grades 1–6), but the report cites prior validation against dermal elastosis and reports high intra-rater and inter-rater repeatability (weighted κ = 0.81 and 0.86, respectively).  
   - Potential measurement error remains—for example, quality and consistency of replica collection and grading—but there is no credible indication that such error differed between randomized groups. Participants were asked to avoid sunscreen and moisturizer on the day of sampling, reducing short-term cosmetic effects on the replica.  
   - **Judgement:** Low risk of bias in measurement of this outcome.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The article identifies change in photoaging as the primary outcome and describes the main comparison and analysis in advance within the manuscript. The reported main sunscreen result—relative odds 0.76 (95% CI, 0.59 to 0.98)—is consistent between the unadjusted and adjusted models shown in Table 4.  
   - The study is registered (ACTRN12610000086066), but the trial itself was conducted from 1992 to 1996, whereas the registry identifier appears to be from a later registration period. On the material supplied, it is not possible to verify that the photoaging endpoint, its measurement time point, analytic model, covariate adjustment, and handling of missing data were prospectively specified before outcome data were known.  
   - The report includes several analytic choices: GEE ordinal models, adjusted and unadjusted models, complete-case analysis, multiple imputation, subgroup analyses, and exploratory analyses stratified by baseline photoaging grade. The principal result is clearly presented, but the availability of multiple analyses creates some opportunity for selective emphasis.  
   - In particular, β-carotene subgroup findings differed according to baseline photoaging severity and are described as exploratory. They should not be treated as confirmatory evidence of benefit in less severely photoaged participants or harm in more severely photoaged participants.  
   - **Judgement:** Some concerns because prospective specification of the exact outcome-analysis plan cannot be verified from the supplied registration/protocol information, despite reasonably clear reporting of the principal analysis.

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   - The outcome was measured in a reasonably objective manner by blinded assessors, which is a major strength.  
   - Nevertheless, there are unresolved concerns about allocation concealment, unavoidable lack of participant blinding in the sunscreen comparison with possible behavioural co-interventions, substantial missing paired outcome data, and inability to verify prospective specification of the exact analysis.  
   - These issues do not amount to clear high risk of bias for the principal daily-sunscreen result, but they preclude a low-risk judgement.
