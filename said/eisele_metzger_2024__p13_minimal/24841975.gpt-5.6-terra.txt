**Endpoint assessed: Overall survival (OS)**  
Effect of assignment to elacytarabine versus the investigator-selected control regimen.

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk.**  
- Patients were randomly assigned 1:1 through a centralized Interactive Web Response System. This is an appropriate allocation mechanism and is likely to have concealed the forthcoming assignment from recruiting investigators.  
- Randomization was stratified by prior treatment intensity and relapsed versus refractory disease, with blocks of four within strata. Although the use of fixed small blocks can theoretically make assignments more predictable if recruitment staff know the sequence and prior allocations, central web-based allocation substantially protects against this concern.  
- Importantly, investigators selected and recorded the intended control regimen *before* random assignment. This reduces the important risk that knowledge of allocation influenced the comparator selected for an individual patient.  
- Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable. There was an imbalance in age ≥70 years (30% elacytarabine versus 18% control), but such an imbalance can occur by chance, particularly when age ≥70 was not a stated randomization stratum. The reported age-adjusted OS estimate was essentially unchanged (HR 0.98 versus unadjusted HR 0.97).  
- There is no indication that allocation was manipulated or that non-random methods were used.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk.**  
- This was an open-label trial, so participants and treating clinicians knew treatment assignment. Knowledge of assignment could potentially influence supportive care, decisions to give further cycles, or decisions concerning transplantation. However, for the primary intention-to-treat comparison, there is no clear evidence that such deviations created a material imbalance affecting OS.  
- The protocol allowed additional cycles in both groups when the investigator considered that a patient was benefiting. The control treatment was deliberately pragmatic and could be administered according to local guidelines or published references; this is part of the intended intervention strategy rather than necessarily a deviation.  
- Six control-arm patients received treatment different from the control regimen selected before randomization. This is a small number (6/190, 3.2%). The report does not indicate comparable incorrect-treatment numbers in the elacytarabine group, but the magnitude is too small to plausibly alter the null OS result materially.  
- A somewhat larger proportion of patients assigned to control than elacytarabine did not receive any study treatment (16/190 [8.4%] versus 6/191 [3.1%]). Nevertheless, the primary OS analysis was explicitly conducted in the full intention-to-treat population, preserving the comparison created by randomization.  
- Stem-cell transplantation after trial treatment could influence survival, but it occurred in similar numbers in the two randomized groups (20 versus 21). The primary analysis was not restricted to treated patients. An additional analysis censoring at transplantation was reported, but this does not replace the primary ITT estimate.  
- The manuscript does not give sufficiently detailed data on all post-protocol anticancer therapies or supportive-care differences to exclude minor deviations. Still, no evidence suggests deviations serious enough to bias the ITT OS comparison materially.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk.**  
- OS data were available for nearly all randomized participants. At the analysis cutoff, only one participant in the elacytarabine group and four in the control group were reported as lost to follow-up or withdrawn: 5/381 (1.3%) overall.  
- Death was very common in this population (302 events for the planned event-driven analysis), so the small amount of loss to follow-up is unlikely to have meaningfully affected the estimated hazard ratio.  
- Patients alive at the cutoff were administratively censored on the cutoff date, which is appropriate for a time-to-event OS analysis. Those lost to follow-up were censored when last known alive; this is standard practice.  
- Loss to follow-up was slightly more frequent in the control group (four versus one), but the absolute difference is trivial. Given the very short survival and overwhelmingly complete ascertainment of deaths, it is implausible that the missing data could overturn the conclusion of no material difference in OS (HR 0.97; 95% CI, 0.77–1.22).

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—overall survival: low-risk.**  
- Overall survival is an objective outcome: death is not a subjective, participant-reported, or assessor-dependent endpoint.  
- Although the study was open-label, lack of blinding is unlikely to affect whether or when death occurred. Thus, unlike subjective outcomes such as symptoms or quality of life, the absence of blinding does not create an important measurement-bias concern for OS.  
- The report does not describe an independent blinded mortality-adjudication process or the specific sources used to ascertain deaths. That omission is regrettable, but in a trial of relapsed/refractory AML, with very high mortality and only five patients lost to follow-up, differential misclassification of death status or death date is unlikely.  
- The analysis used conventional survival methods, with time from randomization/treatment initiation as specified by the study analysis. There is no indication that outcome measurement differed systematically between randomized groups.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns.**  
- The trial was registered (NCT01147939), and the manuscript identifies OS as the primary endpoint. The reported primary ITT OS comparison—median OS, hazard ratio, confidence interval, Kaplan–Meier curve, and log-rank P value—is clearly presented. This is reassuring.  
- The methods state prospectively that the study was powered for OS and that analysis would occur when approximately 300 events had occurred. The reported analysis was undertaken at 302 events, which is consistent with the event-driven plan.  
- However, the supplied material does not include the trial registry record, full protocol, or statistical analysis plan. Therefore, it is not possible to verify fully whether the precise primary OS analysis, censoring rules, handling of transplantation, covariate adjustments, and any planned subgroup analyses were all specified before unblinded data were available.  
- Multiple OS presentations are reported: unadjusted ITT OS, age-adjusted OS, OS censoring at stem-cell transplantation, and several subgroup/control-regimen analyses. The main unadjusted ITT result is appropriately prominent and is not replaced by a more favourable alternative analysis. Nevertheless, without access to the prespecified analysis plan, selective emphasis among these analyses cannot be excluded completely.  
- Accordingly, there are **some concerns**, rather than low risk, for selective reporting.

**6. Overall rating: some concerns.**  
The randomization, adherence to the intended randomized comparison, completeness of OS follow-up, and objective measurement of mortality are all at low risk of bias. However, there are **some concerns** regarding selection of the reported result because the supplied report does not permit direct verification against the prospectively registered protocol and statistical analysis plan, despite the reassuring consistency between the stated primary endpoint and the main reported ITT analysis.
