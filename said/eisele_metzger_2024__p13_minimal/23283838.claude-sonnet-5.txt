# Risk of Bias Assessment: Between-Session Habituation Outcome

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns.** Randomization was conducted by an independent data manager with no participant interaction, and capsules were identical-appearing, suggesting adequate concealment. Blinding integrity was formally tested and confirmed (participants and experimenters could not discern assignment, ps > .15). However, no formal randomization sequence generation method (e.g., computer-generated, block randomization) is described, and there is a statistically significant baseline imbalance in age (DCS ~49 vs. placebo ~38, p=.019) despite the small sample. While age was examined as a covariate and did not change results, this imbalance in a small sample (N=30 analyzed) raises some concern about whether the randomization process (or chance) created baseline differences that could not be fully ruled out given the small sample size.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Low risk.** This was a single-dose intervention (one capsule ingested immediately prior to exposure), minimizing opportunity for deviation. Both groups received the same standardized (though individually-tailored) exposure protocol, with the tailoring based on pre-established SUDS targets applied symmetrically across groups (a legitimate part of the intended intervention design rather than a deviation). No crossovers or additional interventions are reported. Adherence to single-dose ingestion appears to have been directly observed/controlled by study staff.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Some concerns.** Of 34 randomized, 4 participants (2 DCS, 2 placebo) did not complete Visit 2 and were excluded from all analyses — an 11.8% dropout, balanced across arms. The reasons given (lost contact, withdrawal) appear unrelated to treatment response, and the balanced/small dropout numbers are somewhat reassuring. However, the authors explicitly used a per-protocol (not intention-to-treat) analysis, justified by reasoning that ITT analysis would be uninterpretable given no outcome data exists for dropouts. This is a plausible rationale for this outcome (which requires two sessions to compute), but it still leaves open the possibility that dropout was outcome-related in ways not detectable with only 4 total dropouts, and no sensitivity analyses (e.g., imputation) were presented to address this.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (between-session habituation)
**High risk.** This outcome is fundamentally a participant-reported subjective measure (SUDS ratings, self-reported anxiety during a speech task) used to classify participants categorically as habituators or non-habituators. Although the study confirmed successful blinding via post-hoc guessing (not significantly different from chance), participant-reported subjective distress ratings remain inherently susceptible to expectancy and demand effects, motivation to please experimenters, or subtle unblinding not captured by the blinding-guess methodology (which only tested guessing accuracy, not the influence of subtle physiological/somatic drug cues on rating behavior). Given that (a) the primary outcome is entirely self-reported/subjective, (b) DCS could have subtle unblinding physiological effects (e.g., no reported side effects strengthens blinding somewhat, but this is reassuring rather than conclusive), and (c) the categorical cutoff (≥5-point SUDS drop) is a somewhat arbitrary researcher-selected threshold that could be influenced by rating tendencies — this combination elevates risk for this specific subjective, self-reported, categorically-dichotomized outcome.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Some concerns.** The manuscript explicitly states this outcome (between-session habituation via SUDS) was the pre-specified primary outcome ("Our primary measure of DCS's exposure-facilitating effect was between-session habituation"), which is reassuring. However, there is no trial registration or protocol referenced/available in the manuscript to verify that this outcome, its specific operationalization (the 5-point cutoff for categorical response), and the analysis plan were specified *before* data collection/unblinding. The 5-point threshold for defining "response" appears somewhat arbitrary and is not justified by citation to established clinical significance criteria for SUDS. Without an a priori registered protocol to check against, the possibility that this specific cutoff was selected after examining the data (or that other cutoffs were tried) cannot be excluded.

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** While randomization concealment and blinding testing were reasonably well-handled (domain 1: some concerns) and the single-dose intervention had low risk of deviation (domain 2: low risk), the combination of (a) unverified/unregistered outcome definition and cutoff selection (domain 5: some concerns), (b) reliance on per-protocol analysis with unaddressed missing data (domain 3: some concerns), and critically (c) high risk of measurement bias inherent to a subjective, self-reported, dichotomized primary outcome in a small sample (domain 4: high risk) — together substantially lower confidence in this specific result. The high-risk rating in domain 4 alone is sufficient to drive the overall rating to "high risk," and this is compounded by concerns in multiple other domains.
