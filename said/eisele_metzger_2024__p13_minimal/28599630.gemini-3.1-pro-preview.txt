**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**
The study was a randomized, double-blind trial. Randomization was performed by a researcher who was not involved in data collection, and it was stratified by EPDS score and previous history of depression. Both the participants and the research assistants were blinded to the allocation. While specific details about the method of generating the randomization sequence were not provided, the involvement of an independent researcher and the successful blinding strategy generally conceal the allocation sequence adequately. Baseline characteristics were well balanced, suggesting no failure in the randomization process. 

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**
This was a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial where the intervention (fish oil) and control (placebo) capsules were identical in color, shape, size, and packaging. Participants, care providers, and outcome assessors were blinded to the assigned intervention. Furthermore, compliance was objectively evaluated via capsule counts and validated through serum fatty acid composition as a biological marker, confirming adherence to the intended interventions without significant deviations.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high-risk**
There was an exceptionally high attrition rate in this study. Of the 60 women randomized (32 in the fish oil group and 28 in the placebo group), only 32 women completed the trial (15 in the fish oil group and 17 in the control group). This equates to nearly a 50% loss to follow-up. Although the authors stated they performed an Intention-to-Treat (ITT) analysis including all 60 women, the sheer magnitude of missing data for the final evaluation compromises the reliability of the outcome, making the risk of bias due to missing outcome data high. 

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low-risk**
The outcome, the prevalence of depressive symptoms, was measured using a validated self-report tool (EPDS score ≥ 11). As this is a highly subjective participant-reported outcome, it is extremely susceptible to bias if participants are aware of their treatment assignment. However, because both the participants and the researchers were blinded throughout the duration of the trial, the risk of bias in the measurement of the outcome is low.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high-risk**
The study was retrospectively registered (registered in May 2012, while enrollment began in November 2009). Crucially, the provided trial registration data details an *observational cohort study*, not an interventional randomized controlled trial. There are no arms or interventions listed in the registration, and the outcomes are defined generally for the cohort. Because the nested RCT was completely unregistered in a prospective manner and no pre-specified statistical analysis plan is available for the trial specifically, it is impossible to verify if the reported outcomes and analytical methods (such as the specific cut-offs and time-points evaluated) were selected from multiple planned analyses. This warrants a high-risk judgement.

**6. Overall rating: high-risk**
The overall risk of bias is high. Although the randomization, blinding, and outcome measurement were adequately handled, the trial suffers from an enormous amount of missing outcome data (nearly 50% attrition) and lacks prospective trial registration for the randomized interventional component. These factors substantially lower confidence in the reported results.
