Based on a thorough review of the manuscript, here is the risk of bias assessment for the endpoint **weight**:

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**
*Justification:* The manuscript states that "randomization was carried out confidentially by the clinic administration, and arranged so that 25 test persons would be treated with the active drug and 24 with a placebo." While this implies an attempt at allocation concealment by using a third party (clinic administration), there is no description of the method used to generate the random allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers) or the exact mechanism of concealment (e.g., opaque sealed envelopes). Without these details, we cannot be entirely certain that the randomization process was fully protected against subversion. 

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**
*Justification:* The trial is described as double-blind, and "Both subjects and clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment." The medication was supplied in "numbered boxes" and matched a placebo. Furthermore, concomitant medications and olanzapine doses were kept constant during the study. There is no evidence to suggest that deviations from the intended interventions occurred that would have compromised the double-blind design.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**
*Justification:* There is a significant and highly asymmetrical dropout rate between the two arms. The authors report that "Six subjects who failed to appear for more than one evaluation (all from the placebo group) dropped out of the study, and their data were not further analyzed." This means 25% (6 out of 24) of the placebo group dropped out, compared to 0% (0 out of 25) in the topiramate group. The authors simply excluded these patients from the analysis rather than employing an Intention-to-Treat (ITT) approach or acceptable imputation methods. This differential attrition heavily compromises the comparability of the groups and introduces a high risk of bias, as the reasons for dropping out in the placebo group may be related to the outcome (e.g., lack of efficacy/continued weight gain leading to frustration).

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low risk**
*Justification:* The outcome evaluated here is body weight, which is an objective, standardized measurement. The subjects were "seen, weighed" every two weeks by staff. Given that weight is an objective physiological measure and that both the patients and the outcome assessors were blinded to the intervention assignment, it is highly unlikely that the measurement of this specific outcome was influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**
*Justification:* The authors report on the outcomes (weight, SF-36, EWL-60-S, Bf-S) exactly as described in the methods section. However, there is no mention of a pre-published trial protocol or registration in a clinical trials database (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) prior to patient enrollment. Without a pre-specified statistical analysis plan, it is impossible to definitively rule out selective analysis reporting, such as whether intermediate time points were selectively emphasized or if the chosen statistical tests were decided post hoc.

**6. Overall rating: high risk**
*Justification:* The study is judged to be at **high risk** of bias overall. While the measurement of the objective outcome (weight) and the blinding procedures appear sound, the severe differential attrition (missing data solely from 25% of the placebo group) without appropriate statistical handling (e.g., ITT analysis) substantially lowers confidence in the validity of the results. Additionally, there are some concerns regarding the lack of details in the randomization sequence generation and the absence of a pre-registered protocol.
