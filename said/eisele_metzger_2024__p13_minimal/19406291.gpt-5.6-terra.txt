**Endpoint assessed: Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-D) score** (clinician-rated depression severity; reported repeatedly through 10 weeks and at end of treatment).

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The investigators state that randomization used computer-generated random-number sequences, with block randomization (blocks of eight). This is an appropriate method for generating an unpredictable allocation sequence.  
   - The pharmacy reportedly undertook random assignment and dispensing, and placebo and sertraline tablets had identical markings. This provides some reassurance that investigators and participants were blinded.  
   - However, the report says that sequences were placed in “individual presealed envelopes,” without specifying that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, and opened only after irrevocable enrolment. “Presealed” is not, by itself, adequate evidence of secure allocation concealment.  
   - Baseline HAM-D means were reasonably similar (sertraline 27.5; placebo 25.2), although this small trial was imbalanced in some prognostic characteristics, notably injury severity (mild TBI: 20% versus 50%) and income. Such differences may be chance findings in a small sample, but do not fully reassure one about the allocation process.  
   - Therefore, sequence generation was satisfactory, but allocation concealment is insufficiently documented.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - This was intended to be a double-blind, placebo-controlled comparison. Medication packaging and pill-number increases were matched between groups, which should have helped preserve blinding.  
   - Participants were asked not to initiate new pharmacological or psychosocial treatments, attended scheduled visits, and staff used a standard protocol intended to keep supportive contact comparable. These are reasonable efforts to maintain the assigned intervention contrast.  
   - Dose was adjusted from 25 mg up to 200 mg/day according to HAM-D symptoms, clinical assessment, response, and tolerance. Individual dose adjustment is clinically plausible and applied under the trial protocol, but it makes the intervention a flexible-dose strategy rather than a fixed-dose comparison.  
   - There is no report of medication adherence data from the required diaries, pill counts, actual doses received by group, use of prohibited co-interventions, or an assessment of the success of blinding. Sertraline-related effects could potentially have revealed allocation to participants or clinicians.  
   - The effect estimate is also difficult to separate from the handling of discontinuations: the principal reported analyses mix random-effects models, last-observation-carried-forward analyses, and analyses apparently based on completers.  
   - On balance, blinding and protocol standardisation reduce concern, but insufficient information on adherence, co-interventions, and maintenance of blinding leaves **some concerns**.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
   - Fifty-two participants were randomized according to the registry (27 sertraline, 25 placebo), but only 41 completed the trial (22 and 19, respectively). Thus, 11/52 participants (21%) did not complete follow-up: 5/27 (19%) in the sertraline group and 6/25 (24%) in the placebo group.  
   - The manuscript’s baseline and end-of-treatment outcome table uses only the 41 completers, despite describing an intention-to-treat analysis. The reporting therefore does not clearly provide outcome data for all randomized participants.  
   - Attrition was related to outcome severity: participants who dropped out tended to have higher HAM-D scores at their last assessment than those who remained in treatment (reported *P*=.052). This directly suggests that missingness was associated with depression severity, the endpoint under review.  
   - Reasons for non-completion included worsening anxiety, worsened depression after a family death, an allergic reaction, missed appointments, failure to collect medication, and loss of contact. Several of these reasons may be related to treatment response, tolerability, or depressive severity.  
   - The authors used last observation carried forward for some analyses. LOCF is a potentially biased method when symptoms change over time and when dropout is associated with outcome severity; it does not satisfactorily address the problem here. Random-effects models may use available repeated observations, but their validity requires assumptions about missingness that were neither justified nor explored.  
   - Although the numbers discontinuing were superficially similar between groups, the amount of missing outcome data is substantial for this small trial and is plausibly outcome-related. The reported HAM-D comparison is consequently at **high risk of bias** from missing outcome data.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — HAM-D score: some concerns**  
   - The HAM-D is a clinician-rated, established depression-severity instrument. The study reports that postdoctoral fellows or licensed psychologists independently rated depression severity, separately from the physicians who adjusted medication. This separation is a strength.  
   - The trial was described as double-blind; participants and study staff were said to be blinded to whether sertraline or placebo had been prescribed. If maintained, this would materially reduce detection bias for this clinician-rated outcome.  
   - Nevertheless, the report does not explicitly state that the HAM-D outcome assessors themselves remained blinded throughout, nor does it report any check of blinding. “Study staff” were said to be blinded, but the assessors had repeated contact with participants and may have become aware of side effects, dose changes, or participant beliefs about allocation.  
   - HAM-D includes several items requiring clinical judgement and patient report. Depression ratings can therefore be influenced by knowledge or suspicion of treatment allocation.  
   - The different assessors at baseline and end of treatment may reduce recall of prior scores but could introduce inter-rater variability; no study-specific inter-rater reliability or assessor-training results are reported.  
   - Accordingly, measurement bias is not demonstrated to be present, but the evidence that blinding of outcome assessment was secure is incomplete: **some concerns**.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high risk**  
   - The registered record was first submitted in 2005 but results were not posted until 2015, long after study completion (2008) and publication (2009). Thus, the publicly available registry cannot provide a reliable contemporaneous prespecified analytic record.  
   - The registry lists “Depression at Baseline” and “Depression at End of Treatment” as primary outcomes, but does not provide a clear prespecified primary contrast, detailed statistical model, missing-data strategy, covariate-adjustment plan, or specification of which repeated-measures time point or summary was primary. It also describes a 12-week period, whereas the manuscript reports a 10-week intervention.  
   - The manuscript identifies the total HAM-D as primary and presents several different analyses: end-of-treatment analyses, LOCF analyses, random-effects models across six time points, responder analyses, SCID diagnostic outcomes, and analyses of four alternative HAM-D subscales.  
   - Although the primary total-HAM-D analyses were reported as non-significant, the authors additionally report nominally significant results for two post hoc or selectively emphasised HAM-D subscales (HamG and HamFive, *P*=.04 and .05). No multiplicity adjustment is described. The availability of numerous scales, subscales, outcome definitions, analysis populations, and statistical approaches creates substantial scope for selecting favourable findings.  
   - The discrepancy between the randomized sample of 52 and the 41-person denominators in the reported outcome table further obscures which analysis population was selected for each reported result.  
   - Therefore, there is **high risk of bias** in selection of the reported HAM-D result.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall rating is **high risk of bias**, principally because missing HAM-D outcome data were substantial, plausibly related to depressive severity, and inadequately handled, and because the analysis/reporting framework permitted selective choice among multiple HAM-D analyses and subscales without a clearly prespecified analysis plan. Concerns about allocation concealment, adherence/blinding maintenance, and outcome-assessor blinding further reduce confidence in the reported HAM-D effect estimate.
