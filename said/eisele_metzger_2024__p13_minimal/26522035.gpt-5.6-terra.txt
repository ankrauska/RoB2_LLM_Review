1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
   - A computer-generated random sequence was used, with randomly permuted blocks of sizes 4 and 6. This is an acceptable method for generating an unpredictable allocation sequence.  
   - Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. The sequence was reportedly prepared by a person not involved in the trial, and the envelopes were concealed from researchers and outcome assessors.  
   - Although the report does not explicitly state that envelopes were tamper-proof or opened strictly in sequence, the described procedure is reasonably adequate and there is no evidence of baseline imbalance suggestive of compromised randomization.  
   - The two groups were similar on the reported baseline characteristics.  
   - Clinicians and participants could not be blinded to treatment assignment, but this does not itself indicate a failure of randomization or allocation concealment.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
   - Participants received the interventions to which they were allocated: conventional FFRD or FFRD with mini-implant anchorage.  
   - The intervention procedures were described in reasonable detail, and both groups were followed at similar intervals.  
   - There were no reported treatment discontinuations, crossovers, withdrawals, or failures to receive the allocated intervention.  
   - The appliance phase ended according to a clinical criterion—edge-to-edge incisor relationship—which resulted in variable treatment durations. This could affect participants’ overall experience, but it appears to have been part of the intended treatment strategy rather than a post-randomization deviation from assigned treatment. Mean durations were similar between groups.  
   - Participants and clinicians were necessarily aware of whether mini-implants had been placed. Awareness could influence subjective reports, but that is principally a problem in *measurement* of this patient-reported outcome, rather than evidence of departures from the intended interventions.  
   - There is no indication that co-interventions, adherence, or care differed systematically between groups in a way likely to affect the effect of assignment.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk**  
   - All 32 randomized participants completed the study and were included in the analysis: 16 in each group.  
   - The authors report that all 32 questionnaires were retrieved and that there were no missing questionnaire responses.  
   - There were no losses to follow-up, exclusions after randomization, or outcome-data omissions reported for patient acceptance.  
   - Therefore, missing outcome data are very unlikely to have biased the estimated comparison.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — patient acceptance of the appliance: high-risk**  
   - The endpoint was measured using a patient-completed questionnaire, including ratings of ease of insertion, noticeability, oral hygiene, speech, eating, sleep, appearance, pain, swelling, gum bleeding, satisfaction with results, and willingness to recommend the appliance. These are subjective, participant-reported outcomes.  
   - Participants could not be blinded to treatment allocation. Those receiving mini-implant anchorage would plainly know that they had undergone insertion of mini-implants, under local anaesthesia, with an additional wire segment in the mandibular arch. Thus, participants knew whether they had received the experimental or comparator intervention.  
   - Knowledge of allocation is highly capable of influencing reports of “acceptance,” discomfort, satisfaction, appearance, and functional interference. This is particularly important because the trial’s principal outcome is not an objective physical measurement but the participant’s appraisal of her own treatment experience.  
   - The authors state that outcome assessment and statistical analysis were blinded through coding of questionnaires. This does not adequately address the principal problem: the respondents—the patients generating the outcome data—were unblinded. Blinding of data extractors cannot remove expectation, preference, disappointment, or social-desirability effects in the patients’ answers.  
   - Moreover, questionnaires were administered and collected on the day that the appliance was removed, after participants had achieved the intended treatment stage. Assessments at this point may be influenced by relief at appliance removal and by satisfaction with perceived treatment results, rather than reflecting the experience throughout treatment.  
   - The principal investigator reportedly “thoroughly explained” the questions to patients or parents. Although intended to promote comprehension, this interaction could also influence responses, particularly in a small, single-centre trial conducted by the treating team.  
   - The questionnaire was modified from a previous questionnaire, but the report does not provide convincing evidence that the modified and translated instrument had been formally validated for this population or that its measurement properties were established.  
   - Consequently, measurement of patient acceptance is at **high risk of bias**, because it is subjective and directly susceptible to influence by participants’ unavoidable knowledge of their treatment.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high-risk**  
   - The trial was not prospectively registered, and the protocol was not published before trial commencement. Therefore, there is no independently time-stamped prespecified analysis plan against which the reported analyses can be checked.  
   - Although the manuscript states that there were no outcome changes after trial commencement, this assertion cannot be independently verified in the absence of a prospectively available protocol or trial registration.  
   - “Patient acceptance” was a broad and insufficiently operationalized primary outcome. It was represented by numerous questionnaire items concerning diverse constructs: insertion, noticeability, function, appearance, pain, swelling, gum bleeding, and satisfaction with results. It is unclear whether a single primary questionnaire item, a composite score, a specific subset of items, or a particular direction of effect was prespecified as the definitive analysis of patient acceptance.  
   - The paper reports multiple individual questionnaire comparisons, alongside descriptive percentages, mean scores, ridit analyses, and selected narrative interpretations. With many outcomes and analyses, there was substantial scope to select favourable results or emphasize selected comparisons.  
   - The use of one group as the reference for ridit analysis and the reported handling of the 5-point Likert responses are described, but it is not established that this exact analysis strategy was prespecified before inspection of the data.  
   - The absence of registration/prospective protocol, combined with multiple subjective questionnaire outcomes and no clearly defined single primary estimand for “acceptance,” gives serious concern about selective analysis and reporting.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The overall rating is **high risk of bias**. Randomization, adherence to allocated interventions, and completeness of outcome data were reasonably well handled.  
   - However, the primary endpoint—patient acceptance—was participant-reported, subjective, and measured in participants who necessarily knew whether they had received mini-implant anchorage. This creates a high risk of biased outcome measurement.  
   - In addition, the absence of prospective registration or an accessible pre-specified protocol, together with many questionnaire outcomes and no unambiguous definition of the primary acceptance analysis, creates high risk of bias in selection of the reported result.
