**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**
The study states that students were "consequently randomised with a randomisation table into two different groups (an 'intervention' and a 'control' group)" at each campus. While a randomization table was used, the manuscript does not provide any details on allocation concealment (e.g., sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes), which leaves the possibility that sequence generation could have been foreseen. Furthermore, blinding of participants and personnel to the educational interventions was not possible, which could have influenced expectations. However, baseline characteristics between the trial and control groups within each campus were reported as not significantly different. Due to the lack of information on allocation concealment, there are some concerns for this domain.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**
Given the nature of the educational tutorial strategies (laboratory sessions, intensive tutoring vs routine tutoring), blinding of participants (students) and personnel (tutors) was not possible. However, there is no evidence to suggest that there were substantial deviations from the intended educational interventions that arose because of the trial context. The analysis appears to be based on the groups to which the students were originally assigned (intention-to-treat), with all 144 randomized students continuing through the phases. 

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk**
The flow of participants indicates that all 144 eligible and randomized students (82 in Trento and 62 in Udine) completed the trial through Phase 2. The sample sizes reported for the final outcome assessments perfectly match the initially randomized group sizes (Trento: 41 vs 41; Udine: 39 vs 23). Therefore, there is no missing outcome data, and the risk of bias in this domain is low.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low risk**
The outcome (number of mistakes made in diagnosing problems) was based on students' responses to a standardized, simulated case history ("Valentina's case"). To prevent bias, the manuscript explicitly states that "Two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students and these were compared to the 'standard'." The data were processed blind, and the identities of the schools and specific groups were only revealed after the evaluation. Because the outcome assessors were blinded to group assignment, the risk of measurement bias is low.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**
There is no mention of a pre-published study protocol or a trial registry entry, which is common for older educational pragmatic trials. Without a detailed a priori analysis plan, it is difficult to definitively rule out selective reporting, especially given the multiple phases (Phase 0, 1, and 2) and the combinations of intra- and inter-trial analyses performed. Because we cannot verify the original intentions against a registered protocol, there are some concerns regarding the selection of the reported results.

**6. Overall rating: some concerns**
The overall risk of bias is judged to be of 'some concerns'. This is primarily driven by the lack of clear details regarding allocation concealment during the randomization process and the absence of a pre-specified trial protocol or registration to rule out selective reporting of the complex multi-phase outcomes. Domains relating to missing data, deviations from interventions, and objective, blinded outcome measurement were at low risk.
