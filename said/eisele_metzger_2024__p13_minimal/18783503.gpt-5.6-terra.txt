1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
   - The report states that participants were assigned using an “automated” randomization system on a 1:1 basis, which is consistent with a genuinely random allocation sequence.  
   - The randomization list was reportedly held securely and released only after study completion. This provides credible allocation concealment: neither recruiters nor treating personnel should have been able to predict the next assignment.  
   - Gabapentin and placebo were dispensed in identical-appearing capsules, and placebo recipients took the same number of capsules as gabapentin recipients. These arrangements support maintenance of blinding at allocation.  
   - The two groups each initially contained 20 participants. Although baseline SOWS scores were somewhat higher in placebo recipients (mean 14.9 versus 12.6), this modest imbalance in a trial of only 40 patients does not, by itself, indicate a failure of randomization.  

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The trial was described as double-blind and used matched capsules, reducing the likelihood that participants or care providers knowingly altered treatment because of allocated group.  
   - Both groups underwent methadone-assisted detoxification and could receive symptomatic medication (clonidine, oxazepam, or trazodone) “if required.” Methadone dosage was individualized initially according to prior opium use and then adjusted during the first 3 days according to withdrawal signs and symptoms. Such co-interventions may be appropriate components of the detoxification regimen, but they may also influence the subjective withdrawal outcome.  
   - The authors state that there were no statistically significant differences between groups in mean methadone dose or use of symptomatic medicines, but provide no numerical group-specific data, timing, cumulative doses, or information on whether these treatments were administered before SOWS assessments. Therefore, one cannot adequately judge whether management was truly comparable between groups.  
   - Three placebo-group participants were excluded after non-compliance and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter drugs. These events represent deviations from the assigned treatment strategy. The report does not state whether analysis followed an intention-to-treat principle or whether any outcome data after these deviations were retained.  
   - Thus, blinding makes major differential deviations less likely, but inadequate reporting of adherence, co-interventions, and handling of deviations leaves **some concerns**.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high-risk**  
   - All 20 gabapentin recipients completed detoxification, whereas three of 20 placebo recipients did not: one was excluded on day 14 and two on day 17. Consequently, outcome data at the later assessment stages were missing disproportionately from the placebo group. Table 1 confirms this: placebo-group numbers declined from 20 at baseline to 19 at day 14 and 17 at day 18, while gabapentin-group numbers remained 20 throughout.  
   - Missing data therefore affected 15% of the placebo group and 7.5% of the total randomized sample. In a trial with only 40 participants, this amount and imbalance are potentially consequential.  
   - Crucially, the reasons for missingness were plausibly related to the outcome. The authors themselves suggest that non-compliance and self-medication in placebo recipients “may be due to the greater severity of withdrawal symptoms.” Participants with worse subjective withdrawal symptoms may therefore have been preferentially removed from the placebo analysis.  
   - The article does not report an intention-to-treat analysis, an appropriate method for handling missing repeated measurements, sensitivity analyses, or imputation. The reported analyses appear to use observed cases only, as evidenced by declining denominators.  
   - Since missingness is imbalanced, plausibly outcome-related, and inadequately addressed, this domain is at **high risk of bias**.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low-risk**  
   **Outcome assessed: severity of subjective withdrawal symptoms using the Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS).**  
   - SOWS is a participant-reported scale covering 16 withdrawal symptoms, each rated from 0 to 4. The outcome is inherently subjective and would be vulnerable to expectation effects if participants knew whether they received gabapentin or placebo.  
   - However, the trial is described as double-blind, with identical-appearing gabapentin and placebo capsules and the same capsule number in both groups. These measures should have prevented participants from knowing their allocated intervention.  
   - The outcome measure was administered at scheduled time-points, and no evidence is provided that assessors or participants became unblinded.  
   - Although the report does not document a formal assessment of the success of blinding, there is no affirmative evidence that knowledge of allocation influenced self-reported SOWS ratings. The use of a validated and reliable withdrawal scale further supports appropriate outcome measurement.  
   - Accordingly, for the specified SOWS outcome, the risk of measurement bias is judged **low-risk**.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - No prospectively registered trial record, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or pre-specified primary-outcome definition is provided. It is therefore not possible to verify whether the reported SOWS analysis, time-points, scale scoring method, covariate adjustment, and comparison were specified before examination of the data.  
   - There are material inconsistencies in outcome timing. The methods describe SOWS measurement on days 0, 4, 7, 10, 18, and 21, whereas Table 1 and Figure 1 report baseline and days 3, 7, 10, 14, and 18. The analytical table also refers only to six “follow-up stages,” without resolving this discrepancy. These inconsistencies make it unclear which outcome measurements were pre-specified and analysed.  
   - Multiple analyses are mentioned: repeated-measures ANOVA adjusted for age and methadone dose, Mann–Whitney U-tests for “total symptom score,” individual-symptom comparisons, and graphical presentation. The article does not clearly define which analysis and which time-point or summary measure was the primary result for the total SOWS score.  
   - The paper emphasizes selected individual symptoms that appeared numerically more favourable with gabapentin despite stating that none was statistically significant. This selective narrative emphasis increases concern, although it does not establish definite selective non-reporting.  
   - In the absence of protocol/registration information and given discrepant time-points and unclear pre-specification, this domain has **some concerns**.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The overall rating is **high risk of bias**, principally because of high risk from missing SOWS outcome data. Attrition occurred only in the placebo group, was plausibly related to worse withdrawal symptoms, and appears to have been handled by excluding participants with missing data rather than by an intention-to-treat approach.  
   - Additional concerns arise from incomplete reporting of co-interventions and treatment deviations, and from unclear outcome time-points and analysis selection. These issues materially reduce confidence in the reported effect of add-on gabapentin on subjective opioid-withdrawal severity.
