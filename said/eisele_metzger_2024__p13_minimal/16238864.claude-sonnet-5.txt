# Risk of Bias Assessment: Intentions to Consume RDIFV

## 1. Risk of Bias Arising from the Randomization Process
**Some concerns**

Justification: The randomization sequence was generated using a true random number generator (Haahr, 2004), which is an adequate method for random sequence generation. However, allocation concealment is problematic: booklets were pre-assembled into "a single pile in an order determined by" the random sequence and then physically distributed in a lecture theatre/lab class. There is no description of how this distribution occurred (e.g., whether booklets were handed out by staff blind to allocation, or picked up by students themselves), raising concern that participants could potentially see or select booklets, or that distributors were aware of the sequence. Baseline balance was checked and no significant differences were found between groups on pre-intervention cognitions (including intention itself), which is reassuring. However, the lack of detail on allocation concealment mechanisms prevents a "low risk" rating.

## 2. Risk of Bias Due to Deviations from the Intended Interventions
**Some concerns**

Justification: This was an unblinded study by necessity (participants could see whether they received the leaflet-style intervention or the control task), which is typical for this type of behavioural intervention. Participants were explicitly asked not to discuss booklet content with others for one week, but this relies on self-report compliance with no verification described, and given that the study was conducted with students in shared academic environments (lectures, labs), between-group contamination is plausible. No formal check for contamination was performed. Additionally, no intention-to-treat analysis is explicitly described—analyses appear to be based on those who completed both pre- and post-intervention measures, which for this immediate post-intervention outcome should include the whole randomized sample (as it was measured within the same session), somewhat mitigating concern for this specific outcome (since attrition at this stage should be minimal, occurring within the same testing session). This reduces the severity of concern for this specific outcome (intentions, measured immediately post-intervention in the same sitting) compared to the follow-up behavioural outcomes.

## 3. Risk of Bias Due to Missing Outcome Data
**Low risk**

Justification: The specific outcome under review—post-intervention intentions to consume the RDIFV—was measured immediately following the intervention/control task, within the same booklet, in the same session (under "examination conditions"). All 218 participants who completed the booklet would have completed this measure as part of a continuous administration; the paper reports full N=218 for pre/post-intervention cognitive measures with no indication of missing data at this stage. The substantial attrition (33%) affected the 1-week follow-up behavioural measures, not the immediate post-intervention intention measure. Therefore, for this specific outcome, missing data risk is low.

## 4. Risk of Bias in Measurement of the Outcome (Intentions to Consume RDIFV)
**High risk**

Justification: Intention is a subjective, self-reported psychological construct assessed via Likert-type items (e.g., "I intend to eat the RDIFV every day over the next week"). Participants were not blinded to their group assignment — the intervention group received explicit persuasive messaging directly urging them to "make a firm decision now that you will eat the recommended daily intake" and told them "you can do it, so do it this week." This creates a substantial risk of response bias/demand characteristics: participants in the intervention arm were essentially primed and explicitly told what to believe/intend immediately prior to answering the intention items, whereas controls received a neutral task. This is a classic case where the measurement of the outcome is inextricably confounded with the unblinded persuasive content of the intervention itself — participants may report inflated intentions simply because they know they are expected to (social desirability, demand effects), not because of a genuine attitudinal shift. There is no objective or blinded outcome assessment possible for this construct given the study design. This represents a high risk of bias in outcome measurement for this specific endpoint.

## 5. Risk of Bias in Selection of the Reported Result
**Some concerns**

Justification: There is no trial registration or pre-specified protocol referenced anywhere in the manuscript (no registration number, no citation of a published protocol). The four hypotheses are stated in the introduction, and Hypothesis 2 does explicitly pre-specify "intention" as an outcome, which is reassuring and suggests this particular endpoint was not selectively reported post hoc. However, multiple cognitive outcomes (intention, self-efficacy, attitude, norms, PBC, anticipated regret) were measured and analyzed, several statistical models were run (hierarchical regressions with multiple entry orders, ANCOVAs), and the lack of a pre-registered analysis plan means we cannot rule out flexibility in analytic choices (e.g., choice of covariates, decision to exclude PBC from analyses post hoc "since self-efficacy and PBC were strongly correlated"). This post hoc exclusion of PBC from the model is an example of a data-driven analytic decision that raises some concern about selective analysis/reporting, even though intention itself was reported as specified.

## 6. Overall Rating
**High risk**

Justification: While randomization generation was adequate and missing data were minimal for this specific immediate outcome, the outcome measurement itself (Domain 4) is at high risk of bias because the self-reported intention outcome was measured in an unblinded design immediately following explicit persuasive messaging designed to change exactly that construct — creating substantial potential for demand-characteristic-driven inflation of scores rather than genuine attitude change. This high-risk domain, combined with "some concerns" in randomization/allocation concealment, deviations from intended intervention (potential contamination), and selection of reported results, substantially lowers overall confidence in the finding that the intervention increased intentions to consume the RDIFV.
