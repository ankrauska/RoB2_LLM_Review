**Endpoint assessed:** Time to first occurrence of **all-cause death or nonfatal heart-failure event**.

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - Participants were described as randomly assigned in a 3:2 ratio, stratified by clinical centre and ischemic status, using an algorithm intended to maintain near balance within strata. The registered protocol likewise specifies randomized allocation with stratification. This is compatible with a genuinely random allocation process.  
   - Baseline demographic, clinical, electrocardiographic, echocardiographic, and medication characteristics were closely balanced between groups, providing no empirical suggestion of a major failure of randomization.  
   - However, the report does not describe how the random sequence was generated in sufficient detail, nor—more importantly—whether allocation was concealed from investigators enrolling participants before irrevocable assignment. An “algorithm” for balancing is not itself evidence of adequate allocation concealment.  
   - The trial was explicitly unmasked after assignment; this is unavoidable to a considerable degree in a comparison of CRT–ICD with ICD alone, but it does not resolve the absence of information about concealment at enrolment.  
   - Therefore, there are **some concerns**, rather than a low-risk judgement, because allocation concealment cannot be verified.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The primary analysis was explicitly intention-to-treat, using all 1820 randomized participants according to assigned group. This is the appropriate analytic approach for estimating the effect of assignment to CRT–ICD versus ICD-only.  
   - Device implantation was achieved in 98.4% of participants, and 95.4% received their assigned device. Thus, most participants received the allocated strategy.  
   - Nevertheless, clinically meaningful deviations occurred:
     - In the ICD-only group, 91/731 participants (12.4%) crossed over to CRT–ICD; 30 crossed over before an endpoint, and 61 did so after a heart-failure event.
     - In the CRT–ICD group, 82/1089 participants (7.5%) received ICD-only devices, largely because CRT lead placement was technically unsuccessful.
     - Some devices were removed during follow-up.  
   - These deviations are largely plausible consequences of device feasibility and clinical care, rather than clearly trial-induced departures designed to favour an intervention. The intention-to-treat analysis appropriately preserves the effect of treatment assignment despite them. However, the extent and asymmetry of crossover and failed CRT implantation could dilute or otherwise complicate the observed effect of assignment.  
   - In addition, treating physicians knew allocation, and the report does not provide sufficiently detailed longitudinal information on whether concomitant heart-failure management, surveillance, thresholds for escalation of care, or other co-interventions differed by group. This is particularly material because the principal treatment difference was in heart-failure events.  
   - Overall, the intention-to-treat analysis is a substantial safeguard, but the unmasked clinical context and incomplete information about post-randomization care leave **some concerns**.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - Follow-up averaged 2.4 years, and the primary analysis included all randomized participants on an intention-to-treat basis. Mortality is generally ascertainable even when a participant stops attending study visits.  
   - However, 44/1089 CRT–ICD participants (4.0%) and 55/731 ICD-only participants (7.5%) declined further participation, were withdrawn by a physician, or were lost to follow-up. The proportion was appreciably higher in the ICD-only group.  
   - The report does not separate these categories by treatment group, provide reasons in enough detail, state the amount of person-time lost, or demonstrate that ascertainment of both death and nonfatal heart-failure events continued through external records for those no longer participating.  
   - Because heart-failure events require active clinical ascertainment and because discontinuation/loss was differential, missingness could be related both to prognosis and to the likelihood that a nonfatal heart-failure event was detected.  
   - The amount missing is not so large, on the information available, as to establish that the result is at high risk of bias; nor is there an analysis demonstrating robustness to missing events. The appropriate judgement is therefore **some concerns**.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: high risk**  

   **4a. All-cause death: low risk**  
   - All-cause death is an objective, clinically important outcome, unlikely to be materially influenced by participants’ or clinicians’ knowledge of treatment allocation.  
   - Deaths were adjudicated by an independent mortality committee that was unaware of group assignments.  
   - Although incomplete follow-up could in principle affect death ascertainment, the measurement method itself is objective and masked adjudication was used.  
   - For the **death component**, the risk of bias in outcome measurement is **low risk**.

   **4b. Nonfatal heart-failure event: high risk**  
   - The trial was open-label: the registry explicitly records masking as “none,” and treating physicians were aware of treatment assignment.  
   - A heart-failure event required symptoms and signs consistent with congestive heart failure plus intravenous decongestive therapy without formal admission, or an augmented oral/parenteral decongestive regimen during hospital admission. Thus, the endpoint depended not only on clinical findings but also on clinicians’ discretionary decisions to intensify treatment, administer intravenous therapy, and admit or manage patients in hospital.  
   - Knowledge that a participant had received CRT–ICD rather than ICD-only could plausibly influence symptom interpretation, diagnostic labelling, monitoring intensity, admission thresholds, and treatment escalation. These influences could favour CRT–ICD and reduce recorded heart-failure events in that group.  
   - The heart-failure adjudication committee was blinded and applied prespecified criteria. This is an important safeguard, but it cannot fully remove bias introduced earlier in the causal chain: if an unblinded treating physician does not admit a participant, administer qualifying therapy, or document a suspected event in the same way, there may be no event available for blinded adjudication.  
   - The investigators themselves acknowledge this limitation: knowledge of assignment “contributed in some way to the lower frequency of heart failure in the CRT–ICD group” is possible.  
   - Since the reported composite benefit was driven by a 41% relative reduction in heart-failure events, rather than by a mortality benefit, this measurement problem is central to the endpoint result.  
   - For **nonfatal heart-failure events**, the risk of bias in outcome measurement is **high risk**.

   **4c. Composite endpoint: all-cause death or nonfatal heart-failure event: high risk**  
   - The composite combines an objectively measured death component with a heart-failure component at high risk of ascertainment/measurement bias.  
   - The observed effect was driven by heart-failure events: heart-failure-only HR 0.59 (95% CI 0.47–0.74), whereas all-cause mortality showed no difference (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.69–1.44).  
   - Consequently, the validity of the composite estimate depends predominantly on the component most vulnerable to unblinded clinical ascertainment.  
   - The risk of bias in measurement of the specified composite endpoint is therefore **high risk**.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: low risk**  
   - The trial was prospectively registered (NCT00180271; first posted in September 2005) before completion and well before the primary analysis. The registered primary outcome was mortality from any cause or first heart-failure event, matching the manuscript’s primary endpoint of death from any cause or nonfatal heart-failure event, whichever occurred first.  
   - The planned comparison, CRT–ICD versus ICD-only, and the time-to-first-event, intention-to-treat framework were also consistent between registry and publication.  
   - The manuscript reports the prespecified group-sequential design, interim-monitoring approach, stopping boundary, stratified log-rank test, and stratified Cox model. The trial stopped after crossing a prespecified superiority boundary at the ninth planned interim analysis. Early stopping was therefore not apparently an unplanned choice of a favourable analysis.  
   - The article reports the primary composite result as well as the component results for heart failure and death, allowing readers to see that the apparent composite benefit was confined to heart-failure events.  
   - There is a small numerical discrepancy between the publication’s final primary-endpoint count (187 CRT–ICD and 185 ICD-only) and the subsequently posted registry count (188 and 188), plausibly reflecting database versions, later reporting, or differing data cutoffs. The supplied materials do not explain it fully. However, it does not indicate selective choice among analyses, and the primary outcome and analytic approach were specified in advance.  
   - On the available protocol/registration and publication information, selection of the reported primary result is at **low risk** of bias.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall rating is **high risk of bias** because the result for the composite endpoint was driven by nonfatal heart-failure events, whose occurrence depended substantially on unblinded clinicians’ diagnostic, treatment, and hospitalisation decisions. Blinded endpoint adjudication mitigates but does not eliminate this problem, because unblinded management determines whether a qualifying event is generated and documented. There are also some concerns regarding allocation concealment, differential loss/withdrawal, and deviations from allocated device strategy.
