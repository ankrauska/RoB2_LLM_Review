1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The authors state that 120 students were “randomized into three groups using a computer program,” which is compatible with generation of a genuinely random allocation sequence.  
   - The groups were equal in size (40 per group), and the reported distributions of sex, age, and school age were broadly similar. These observations do not suggest an obvious failure of randomization.  
   - However, the report gives no information about **allocation concealment**: who generated the sequence, who enrolled students, whether allocation was concealed before assignment, or whether group assignment could have been anticipated or manipulated.  
   - Blinding of participants was infeasible: students necessarily knew whether they were viewing CT images, manipulable computer 3D images, or physical 3D-printed models. This is not itself a defect in sequence generation, but it increases the importance of adequate allocation concealment and objective outcome assessment.  
   - The students were volunteers recruited through a hospital-network post. This may limit generalizability, but does not by itself establish bias in the randomized comparison.  
   - **Judgement:** Some concerns, because computer randomization is reported but concealment and the implementation of randomization are not described.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The assigned interventions were clearly distinct: CT images, computer-based 3D reconstructions, or physical 3D-printed models. Participants apparently received their allocated teaching modality, and no crossovers or withdrawals are reported.  
   - A teacher “provided a detailed interpretation of the fracture in each group.” However, the report does not establish that the teacher’s presentation was standardized in content, duration, emphasis, opportunities for questions, or interaction across the three groups. The teacher was necessarily aware of the group’s intervention and may have conveyed different amounts or types of assistance.  
   - The availability and mode of access were also not equivalent. The CT and 3D subgroups had six computers, whereas the 3Dp subgroups had 12 models. A physical model can be handled directly, whereas computer images may have required turn-taking or interaction with a computer. These features may be part of the intended educational package, but they also mean that the comparison is not solely of image format or printing technology.  
   - No information is provided about adherence to a prespecified teaching script, prevention of communication between groups, or whether students had differential time with the materials.  
   - Since the endpoint was assessed immediately after teaching, deviations or co-interventions during the teaching session could materially affect performance.  
   - **Judgement:** Some concerns. There is no evidence of major non-adherence, but inadequate reporting of standardization of teacher input, exposure time, and access to materials leaves uncertainty about whether effects can be attributed specifically to the assigned modality.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk**  
   - The investigators report that all 120 randomized students participated and that “no one dropped out.”  
   - Table 3 provides outcome data for all participants: 40 in the CT group, 40 in the 3D group, and 40 in the 3Dp group. The sex-specific denominators also sum to the randomized group totals.  
   - There is no indication that answers, examination scores, or assignment times were missing for any student.  
   - Although individual-question data are not presented, the reported total-score and time outcomes appear complete for all randomized participants.  
   - **Judgement:** Low risk, because outcome data appear available for every randomized participant and there is no indication of differential missingness.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome**

   **4a. Performance outcome: total number of correct answers to the 10 anatomical questions — some concerns**  
   - The outcome was measured using a written examination with 10 predefined questions and prespecified correct answers. This is more objective than a participant-reported judgement of learning.  
   - The answers were apparently scored as correct or incorrect, reducing the scope for assessor discretion. The report also states that answers were recorded, although it does not specify who marked the papers, whether marking was automated, or whether markers were blinded to group allocation.  
   - The students themselves were unblinded, which is unavoidable for this educational intervention. Knowledge of allocation is less likely to distort factual multiple-choice answers than it would be to distort subjective self-ratings.  
   - Nevertheless, the examination paper “contained many ‘3D images’.” This creates a serious measurement concern for a comparison between 2D CT, computer 3D, and 3D-printed-model teaching: the test format may preferentially resemble, and therefore favour, the 3D/3Dp learning conditions rather than measure equally the underlying anatomical understanding intended by the study.  
   - In addition, the paper does not demonstrate prior validation of the examination, comparable difficulty across questions, or independence of question construction from the intervention developers.  
   - **Judgement:** Some concerns. Scoring is likely objective, but the outcome instrument appears potentially aligned with the 3D-oriented interventions and assessor blinding is not reported.

   **4b. Performance outcome: time required to answer the 10 anatomical questions — low risk**  
   - Time was recorded by an invigilator for each student. Time is a relatively objective outcome, and there is little room for participants’ expectations to influence the recorded value directly.  
   - The report does not state whether the invigilator was blinded to allocation. Blinding would be difficult because group assignment was apparent. However, measurement of start and finish time is unlikely to be materially influenced by an unblinded invigilator, provided the same timing procedure was used.  
   - The study does not describe whether all students began at the same point, whether instructions about when to start and stop were standardized, or whether students could return to materials during the test. Such omissions introduce some uncertainty, but there is no direct indication of differential measurement.  
   - **Judgement:** Low risk, because the outcome is objectively measurable and was apparently recorded for all students, though timing procedures should have been described more fully.

   **4c. Participant-reported outcomes: pleasure, perceived assistance, self-rated learning effect, and confidence — high risk**  
   - These are subjective participant-reported outcomes. Students knew their allocated educational modality, and the 3D-printed model was novel, tangible, and likely perceived as more technologically advanced.  
   - No blinding was possible, and no measures are described to reduce expectation, novelty, social-desirability, or demand effects. Students may have inferred that the investigators expected favourable responses to 3D printing.  
   - The questions were administered immediately after the intervention and appear to have been developed for this study; no evidence of validation is supplied.  
   - The outcome labelled “personal learning effect” is a self-assessment, not an objective measure of learning.  
   - **Judgement:** High risk for all four subjective evaluation outcomes, because unblinded participants rated subjective experiences that are highly susceptible to expectation and novelty effects.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The article does not report a prospectively registered trial protocol, trial-registration number, statistical analysis plan, or a prespecified primary outcome.  
   - Multiple outcomes were assessed: total correct-answer score, time to complete questions, four evaluative questionnaire outcomes, subgroup analyses by sex, and multiple pairwise comparisons. The manuscript emphasizes statistically significant findings and sex-related findings, but it is unclear which analyses were planned before data were examined.  
   - The report does not provide results for each of the 10 anatomical questions, despite stating that answers to each question were recorded. It instead reports an aggregated total score. This may be appropriate, but without a protocol it is impossible to know whether aggregation, individual-item analyses, sex interactions, or particular comparisons were selected after inspecting the data.  
   - The reported 3D-versus-3Dp score comparison is borderline (*p* = 0.0508), while the text emphasizes superiority of both interventions over CT. This is not necessarily selective reporting, but it illustrates the importance of a prespecified primary comparison and analysis.  
   - **Judgement:** Some concerns, because no protocol or registration is available to verify that the reported outcomes, subgroup analyses, and statistical comparisons were selected independently of the results.

6. **Overall rating: some concerns**  
   - The study has complete outcome data and objective aspects of performance measurement, particularly examination time.  
   - However, there are unresolved concerns about allocation concealment, standardization of teaching and access to materials, possible alignment of the examination with 3D-based teaching, and selective reporting in the absence of registration or a protocol.  
   - The subjective satisfaction and confidence outcomes are at high risk of bias, but for the specified **performance** endpoint, the overall judgement is **some concerns** rather than high risk.
