**Outcome assessed:** Smoking abstinence, defined biochemically as a breath carbon monoxide (CO) sample **<3 ppm**. The principal contrast of interest appears to be percentile versus fixed-criterion incentive schedules, although a smaller random-incentive group was also included.

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
- Participants were randomized only after a five-visit abstinence trial and only those unable to provide an abstinent sample during that trial were eligible. This restriction defines the study population but does not itself invalidate randomization.  
- The report states that allocation was accomplished by assigning, from each group of five entrants, two participants to percentile incentives, two to fixed-criterion incentives, and one to random incentives; randomization was stratified by intended medication use and order of study entry. This suggests a restricted/block allocation procedure.  
- However, the manuscript does not explain how the random sequence within these blocks was generated (for example, computer-generated sequence, random-number table, or sealed allocation schedule). The phrase “randomly assigning” is insufficiently specific.  
- Crucially, there is no information on allocation concealment. Because allocations were made after a readily observed five-visit trial and within small blocks with a fixed 2:2:1 ratio, allocations could potentially have become predictable to recruiting or study personnel if the sequence was not adequately concealed.  
- Baseline characteristics were mostly similar, but there was an imbalance in vocational/technical/community-college education between percentile and fixed-criterion groups. This is compatible with chance, but does not reassure sufficiently about the randomization and concealment procedures.  

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low risk**  
- Blinding of participants was not feasible: participants necessarily had to know the incentive contingency to which they had been assigned. This knowledge was an intrinsic and intended component of the behavioural intervention, rather than an avoidable deviation from it.  
- The intervention procedures are described reasonably clearly. Participants were informed of their assigned payment contingency immediately after randomization, and incentive payments were linked to the relevant schedule.  
- The schedules differed deliberately in whether incentives could be earned for reduced CO values versus abstinence alone. Such differences are the intended intervention contrast, not protocol deviations.  
- Participants reported use of cessation medication and self-reported smoking, but these reports were collected after payment and reportedly did not affect payments. No evidence is presented that use of medication, additional cessation treatment, or other co-interventions differed systematically between groups.  
- There is no indication that staff systematically failed to apply the allocated contingencies, crossed participants between groups, or delivered material intervention components inconsistently.  
- Differential attendance was observed, particularly compared with the random-incentive group, but attendance was itself plausibly affected by the assigned incentive arrangements. Between the two principal active groups, mean numbers of submitted samples were similar (42/60 percentile versus 40/60 fixed criterion). This does not provide evidence of differential deviations from the intended interventions.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
- Outcome measurement depended on participants attending scheduled weekday visits and supplying a breath sample. Missed visits therefore produced missing biochemical outcome data.  
- Missingness was substantial: the mean number of submitted samples out of 60 was 42 (SD 22) in the percentile group and 40 (SD 24) in the fixed-criterion group. Thus, on average, approximately 30% to 33% of scheduled outcome measurements were absent, with considerable between-participant variability.  
- The event-record figure reportedly shows substantial missed-visit periods. These missing measurements are particularly problematic in a smoking-cessation study, because non-attendance may be strongly associated with smoking relapse, inability to meet the CO criterion, disengagement, or incentive-related behaviour.  
- The manuscript does not provide a missing-data strategy, sensitivity analyses, or a clear statement that missed visits were consistently counted as non-abstinent for every analysis. It reports outcomes based on submitted samples and, for several analyses, only among participants who ever submitted an abstinent sample.  
- The maintenance analyses are especially vulnerable: they are restricted to the post-randomization subgroup who achieved at least one CO <3 ppm. This subgroup was influenced by treatment assignment (23/36 percentile versus 25/40 fixed criterion achieved at least one abstinent sample). Conditioning on this intermediate post-randomization event can compromise the randomized comparison.  
- For the reported outcome of consecutive abstinent samples, a missed visit also interrupts the sequence and may be handled differently from a measured non-abstinent sample. The analytical handling of this issue is not adequately specified.  
- Given the amount of missing biochemical data, the plausible relation between missingness and smoking status, and the absence of an appropriate analysis addressing this, the result is at high risk of bias from missing outcome data.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low risk**  
- The outcome is an objectively measured biochemical outcome: breath CO <3 ppm, determined using a Vitalograph CO monitor.  
- The measurement procedure is described: participants took a deep breath, held it for 20 seconds, expired over 20 seconds into a disposable mouthpiece, and the peak reading was recorded. The abstinence threshold was prespecified in the methods as <3 ppm.  
- Although neither participants nor staff were blinded to the assigned incentive schedule, the primary measurement is machine-generated and uses a fixed numerical threshold. It is therefore much less susceptible to assessor expectation than a subjective participant-reported outcome such as craving, satisfaction, or self-reported cigarette consumption.  
- Participants had financial incentives tied to the CO reading and might in principle attempt to influence a breath test. Nevertheless, the specified standardized procedure and use of the peak monitor reading provide reasonable protection against differential assessor interpretation.  
- The report does not mention calibration, quality-control procedures, or blinding of measurement staff. These omissions leave some residual uncertainty, but there is no specific indication that measurement or classification of CO <3 ppm differed systematically by assigned group.  
- The self-reported smoking and medication-use forms are not relied upon for this endpoint, and hence do not materially affect this judgement.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
- No trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospectively specified primary outcome is provided in the manuscript. Consequently, it is not possible to determine whether the reported analyses and outcome definitions were selected after inspection of the data.  
- The paper presents numerous related abstinence outcomes: proportion with at least one abstinent sample; time to first abstinent sample among those who achieved one; total abstinent samples among those who achieved one; duration of consecutive abstinent samples; probability of a second consecutive abstinent sample; and graphical event-record outcomes. These analyses can yield materially different impressions of treatment effect.  
- The stated hypothesis concerned abstinence initiation after equating the incentive for the first abstinent sample. Yet the principal conclusion emphasizes maintenance and consecutive abstinent samples, where the fixed-criterion group appeared superior. This shift may be reasonable scientifically, but without a prespecified analysis plan it raises concern about selective emphasis.  
- The analysis plan also states that percentile versus fixed-criterion Mann–Whitney tests would be undertaken even if the overall Kruskal–Wallis test did not show a difference, because that pairwise comparison was considered most important. This makes multiplicity and selective emphasis more problematic.  
- The reported maintenance analyses condition on post-randomization achievement of an abstinent sample, further increasing the scope for analytically selected results.  
- There is not enough direct evidence to conclude that a particular favourable result was definitely selected from unreported analyses; therefore the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk.

**6. Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
The overall risk of bias is **high risk**. The central reason is the high risk of bias due to missing outcome data: a large proportion of scheduled CO measurements were missing, missingness is likely related to smoking status and engagement, and the analytical handling of missed visits is not adequately described. In addition, the maintenance findings rely on post-randomization selection of participants who first achieved abstinence. Concerns about incomplete reporting of randomization/concealment procedures and absence of a protocol or registration further reduce confidence in the reported effect on smoking abstinence.
