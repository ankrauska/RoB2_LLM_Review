# Risk of Bias Assessment: Oral Sodium Bicarbonate Trial (SGA Score Outcome)

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Low risk**

Justification: Randomization was performed via a computer-generated list, with allocation concealment maintained by an independent third party not involved in study conduct. Tablets (bicarbonate vs. placebo/starch) were identical in appearance, packaging, labeling, and had matching mint flavor to preserve taste-blinding. Baseline characteristics (Table 1) were well-balanced between groups with no statistically significant differences in demographics, comorbidities, or baseline SGA score. The randomization code was maintained blind until after all biochemical and nutritional assessments were completed. This is a robust process with adequate concealment and no evidence of baseline imbalance suggesting failure of randomization.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns**

Justification: Although double-blinding was implemented, 4/30 patients in the treatment group and 3/30 in the placebo group discontinued study medication due to side effects (dyspepsia, dizziness, skin rash) — this differential dropout (13% vs 10%) is modest but notable. Critically, the drug's mechanism (raising serum bicarbonate) is directly detectable via routine biochemistry checked at every visit — this could potentially unblind assessors or clinicians managing patients (e.g., prompting differential dietary/clinical advice). The paper describes an ITT analysis, which is appropriate, but does not report a per-protocol sensitivity analysis to determine whether discontinuations meaningfully altered SGA outcome ascertainment. Additionally, dialysis prescription could be changed based on "clinical evidence of underdialysis" during the study, introducing a possible co-intervention that was not tightly controlled for in relation to treatment allocation.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Some concerns**

Justification: While the manuscript states that biochemical and nutritional assessments were continued in patients who discontinued medication, and mortality/hospitalization data collection appears complete, there is no explicit reporting of the number of patients with complete SGA assessments at each time point (0, 12, 24, 36, 52 weeks) versus those lost to follow-up, dropped out, or who died during the study (2 deaths in treatment arm, 5 in placebo arm by end of study). Given that 5 patients died in the placebo group versus 2 in the treatment group, and SGA is a repeated-measures outcome, differential mortality could plausibly bias the repeated-measures ANOVA if not appropriately handled (e.g., via mixed models accounting for missingness) — the methods do not clarify how missing SGA data due to death were treated in the analysis.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — SGA score
**Some concerns**

Justification: This is the most vulnerable domain for this specific outcome. Although the SGA raters ("two trained observers") were explicitly stated to be blinded to treatment allocation and biochemical results, and achieved excellent inter-rater reliability (kappa 0.84), SGA is fundamentally a **subjective, participant-derived outcome** built from patient-reported items (anorexia, self-reported weight change) combined with clinical/observer judgment. Two key vulnerabilities exist:
- **Patient-level unblinding risk**: bicarbonate tablets may cause physiological effects (e.g., taste, gastric effects, dyspepsia reported in 4 patients) that could subtly unblind patients to their allocation, potentially influencing their self-report of anorexia/appetite — a core component of the SGA score.
- **Assessor dependence on patient-reported history**: even with blinded assessors, the assessors rely on patient self-report of weight change and appetite, which are not objectively verifiable and are susceptible to placebo/nocebo effects or differential reporting behavior if patients suspect their allocation.
- The observed treatment effect was concentrated in the anorexia and weight-loss subscales (the most subjective components) rather than the muscle mass/subcutaneous fat subscales (more directly observer-assessed), which is consistent with — though not proof of — a differential subjective reporting bias.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Low risk**

Justification: The Methods section explicitly and prospectively states "The primary outcome measure was the SGA score," with a priori sample-size calculation performed specifically for SGA as the primary endpoint using CANUSA-derived effect size assumptions. The results correspond to this pre-specified analysis plan (repeated-measures ANOVA on SGA with post-hoc comparisons), and all pre-specified secondary outcomes (NPNA, LBM, FEBM, albumin, hospitalization, mortality) are also reported, including non-significant ones (e.g., LBM, FEBM, albumin, mortality), suggesting no selective outcome reporting for SGA. There is no trial registration or protocol referenced/available for independent verification, which limits certainty, but internal consistency between stated primary outcome, sample size justification, and reported result supports low risk for this specific outcome.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **Some concerns**

**Justification**: While randomization and allocation concealment were robust (low risk) and the SGA outcome appears to have been reported without selective reporting bias (low risk), there are legitimate concerns in three domains: potential subtle unblinding effects on this subjective, patient-report-dependent outcome (measurement domain), incomplete clarity on handling of missing data particularly related to differential mortality between arms, and modest differential dropout from treatment with unclear per-protocol sensitivity analysis. None of these individually rises to "high risk," but the accumulation of concerns — particularly the inherent vulnerability of SGA as a partially subjective, patient-report-derived measure combined with unclear handling of missing data from deaths — is sufficient to warrant an overall rating of "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk." This does not reach "High risk" because blinding procedures were rigorously described and adhered to as far as reported, and the primary analysis was intention-to-treat and pre-specified.
