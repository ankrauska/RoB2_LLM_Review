# Risk of Bias Assessment: Beck Depression Inventory (Total Score) Post-Intervention

## Sir Ian Chalmers' Review

---

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process:**

**Some concerns** — The authors state participants were "randomly allocated to the treatment or wait-list group in consecutive order. No stratification was applied." This description is vague and concerning: "consecutive order" allocation raises questions about whether true randomization (e.g., computer-generated sequence) was used, or whether this simply reflects a systematic/alternating allocation method masquerading as randomization. No detail is provided about the sequence generation method, nor about allocation concealment mechanisms. However, baseline characteristics were well-balanced between groups (Table 1), which is reassuring but does not substitute for a properly described randomization process. Given the self-selected, online recruitment method, there is also possibility that participants could have deduced or influenced allocation given the transparent design (participants were told about immediate vs. delayed allocation), though the "consecutive" email-based system likely prevented foreknowledge before consent. On balance, insufficient methodological detail regarding sequence generation and concealment procedure warrants "some concerns."

---

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions:**

**Some concerns** — This was an open-label, unblinded trial by design (participants knew whether they had immediate or delayed access — inherent to wait-list designs). This is unavoidable given the nature of the intervention but does introduce risk of performance bias: knowledge of allocation could affect participant expectations, effort, health-seeking behavior, or use of co-interventions. Critically, the trial was naturalistic and did **not** restrict concurrent treatment: patients receiving psychotherapy or medication were not excluded, and no protocol was in place to hold co-interventions constant across arms. Indeed, the authors themselves speculate that the substantial and unexpected improvement in the wait-list group may be due to "patients' use of other treatments." This represents a genuine deviation from intended intervention conditions, differential between groups, that could bias the estimated effect toward the null (or otherwise distort it) and was not equally distributed/controlled by design. Adherence was reasonable (PP restricted to ≥1 session), but ITT handling of this contamination issue is not addressed via per-protocol sensitivity analysis for co-intervention exposure.

---

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data:**

**Low risk** — Completion rate was high (81.9%) and similar across groups (85.7% wait-list vs. 78.1% Deprexis; χ²=2.06, p>0.1). Reasons for missingness were examined; non-completers differed from completers only marginally on two QoL subscales, not on the primary outcome (BDI) or other key baseline variables. Both PP and ITT analyses were reported, with ITT employing multiple imputation (100 imputed datasets, SAS PROC MI) — a robust, pre-specified approach to handling missingness. Results were consistent across PP and ITT for the primary BDI total score (both significant, similar effect sizes: d=.36). This convergence between PP and ITT results substantially mitigates concern regarding attrition bias.

---

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (BDI total score):**

**High risk** — The BDI is a self-report, participant-completed instrument assessing a highly subjective construct (mood/depressive symptoms). Given the open-label design (no blinding of participants — impossible to blind given the nature of wait-list vs. active online intervention), there is substantial risk that participants' knowledge of group assignment influenced their self-reported symptom ratings. Participants in the Deprexis arm may have been motivated to report improvement (demand characteristics, effort justification, novelty/expectation effects), while wait-list participants, aware they were denied immediate treatment, may have reported differently (resentful demoralization or, conversely, motivation to seek alternative help—which the authors themselves suggest occurred). No independent, blinded clinician-rated outcome (e.g., HDRS) was used to corroborate self-report, a limitation explicitly acknowledged by the authors themselves. While correlation between BDI and HDRS is cited from historical literature, this does not substitute for a blinded outcome assessment in this specific trial. Given that the primary outcome is entirely dependent on unblinded subjective self-report in an unblinded trial, this domain is rated **high risk**.

---

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result:**

**Low risk** — The trial was prospectively registered (NCT01401296) with the BDI-assessed "severity of depressive symptoms" as the explicitly named primary outcome, consistent with what is reported in the manuscript. The timeframe in the registry ("7 days" — likely a data-entry artifact, given the described 8-week post-assessment in text) is a minor inconsistency but does not indicate outcome-switching. WHOQOL-BREF is also listed a priori as a secondary outcome and reported as such. Other secondary measures (DAS, RSE, SBQ-R) were not pre-registered but are transparently reported as secondary/exploratory in the manuscript itself, and all pre-specified analyses (BDI total, subscales, PP, ITT) appear to have been reported with results, not selectively suppressed. There is no evidence of selective reporting for the primary BDI outcome specifically.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** While randomization appears to have achieved baseline balance and missing data were well-handled via appropriate ITT methodology, the combination of (a) some concerns regarding the rigor and transparency of the randomization/allocation process, (b) a substantial, unaddressed risk of differential co-intervention contamination between arms in a naturalistic unblinded design, and critically (c) **high risk of measurement bias** — given that the primary outcome (BDI) is an inherently subjective, self-reported measure collected in a completely unblinded trial with plausible differential reporting/demand effects between arms — together substantially undermine confidence in the estimated treatment effect on the primary outcome. The authors' own candid acknowledgment that the wait-list group's "unexpected" large improvement may reflect uncontrolled co-intervention/contamination further reinforces concern that the observed between-group BDI difference (d=.36) may not purely reflect a specific efficacy signal for Deprexis. This trial should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of efficacy on this outcome.
