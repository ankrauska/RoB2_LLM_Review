1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
   - Participants were assigned in a 1:1 ratio using a minimisation algorithm that incorporated a random element. This is an acceptable random allocation method, provided the random component operated as described.  
   - Allocation was administered through an automated, 24-hour telephone randomisation service at the University of Leeds Clinical Trials Research Unit. This centralised system is credible allocation concealment: recruiting clinicians could not reasonably know or control the forthcoming allocation before enrolment.  
   - The minimisation variables (surgeon, age group, and breast density) were appropriate, and reported baseline characteristics were closely balanced between groups, including the randomisation factors.  
   - The trial was open-label, but lack of blinding after allocation is not, in itself, a defect in the randomisation process.  

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
   - The intended comparison was a pragmatic policy of preoperative MRI plus usual triple assessment versus usual triple assessment without further imaging. Open-label delivery was unavoidable: patients and clinicians necessarily knew whether MRI had been performed.  
   - Non-adherence occurred, but was limited. In the MRI group, 761/816 (93%) received MRI; reasons for non-receipt included technical failures, non-attendance, refusal, contraindications, and timing/logistical problems. In the no-MRI group, 9/807 (1%) received MRI, mainly on patient or clinician request.  
   - Some changes in surgery after MRI—including conversion from planned wide local excision to mastectomy—are not departures from the intervention. They are a principal intended consequence of making MRI information available.  
   - The primary analysis was intention-to-treat and included all 816 and 807 participants in their allocated groups. This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to an MRI-based preoperative-management strategy.  
   - There is no indication of substantial systematic co-intervention, crossover, or exclusion from analysis that would materially bias the intention-to-treat comparison.  

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk**  
   - Primary-outcome data were unavailable for very few participants: three in the MRI group and four in the no-MRI group. In addition, four and six participants, respectively, were described as lost to follow-up. Thus, missing or unavailable primary-endpoint information affected roughly 1% or fewer of randomised participants in each group.  
   - The numbers are balanced between groups and are far too small, relative to the 19% event rate, to plausibly overturn the null result.  
   - The investigators classified participants lost to follow-up as not having had a primary-endpoint event. This is not an ideal assumption, because an unobserved repeat operation could have occurred. Nevertheless, only ten participants were lost to follow-up and the allocation imbalance was trivial (four versus six). Even an implausibly extreme reassignment of these missing cases would have little effect on the estimated treatment effect.  
   - Consequently, missing data are unlikely to have materially biased the reoperation-rate result.  

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (reoperation rate): high-risk**  
   - The endpoint was a composite: repeat wide local excision or mastectomy within six months, plus “pathologically avoidable” mastectomy at the initial operation. Although occurrence of an operation can be verified from clinical records, the decision to undertake further surgery is not wholly objective. It depends on clinical and pathological judgements, particularly interpretation of excision margins, disease extent, and the acceptability of breast-conserving surgery.  
   - The trial was explicitly open-label. Surgeons, radiologists, multidisciplinary teams, and patients knew whether MRI had been undertaken and, in the MRI group, knew the MRI findings. This knowledge could influence thresholds for recommending re-excision, mastectomy, additional biopsy, or acceptance of margins.  
   - There was appreciable variation between centres in the predeclared definition of a “clear” margin: 0.5–5.0 mm for invasive disease and 1.0–10.0 mm for ductal carcinoma in situ. Although each centre was intended to apply its own definition in both trial groups, unblinded knowledge of MRI findings could still affect how borderline findings were acted on.  
   - The “pathologically avoidable mastectomy” component required judgement about whether MRI findings had caused mastectomy and whether pathology showed disease that could have been managed by wide local excision. The DMEC reviewed such cases, but the report does not state that this adjudication was conducted blinded to treatment allocation, MRI findings, or the actual management decision.  
   - The imbalance in this component was substantial—16 pathologically avoidable initial mastectomies in the MRI group versus two in the no-MRI group, the latter having received MRI despite allocation. This is clinically plausible as an effect of MRI-driven management, but it also illustrates that outcome classification and clinical decisions were inseparable from knowledge of intervention status.  
   - Thus, while repeat operations themselves are recorded events, the endpoint relies materially on unblinded clinical decisions and partly on unblinded adjudicative judgement. These circumstances are likely to have influenced outcome measurement.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The trial was registered (ISRCTN 57474502), and the manuscript identifies reoperation rate as the primary clinical endpoint. The reported endpoint broadly corresponds to the stated definition: repeat operation or further mastectomy within six months of randomisation, or a pathologically avoidable initial mastectomy.  
   - The statistical approach is also stated: intention-to-treat logistic regression adjusted for the minimisation factors. Both crude event counts and the adjusted odds ratio are presented. This is reassuring.  
   - However, the protocol and statistical analysis plan are not available in the material supplied for direct comparison with the published analysis. Therefore, it cannot be verified that the exact composite definition, the treatment of participants lost to follow-up as event-free, the adjustment model, and all analytic decisions were fully prespecified.  
   - There is particular uncertainty about the timing and prespecification of the “pathologically avoidable mastectomy” classification. The report says that the definition was “specified by the independent DMEC and agreed by the Trial Steering Committee,” but does not clearly establish that this definition was finalised before relevant outcome data were reviewed.  
   - These limitations do not demonstrate selective reporting, but they leave residual concern that cannot be resolved from the manuscript alone.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - Randomisation, adherence to the assigned management strategy, and completeness of primary-outcome follow-up were generally satisfactory.  
   - However, the primary endpoint was substantially dependent on unblinded clinical decisions about additional surgery and on an incompletely described adjudication of “pathologically avoidable” mastectomy. This creates a high risk of bias in measurement of the reoperation-rate endpoint.  
   - In addition, there are some unresolved concerns about prespecification and selection of the reported result.
