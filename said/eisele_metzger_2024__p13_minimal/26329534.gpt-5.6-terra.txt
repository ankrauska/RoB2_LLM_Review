1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The report states that pharmacists “were randomized” to postal-mail, email, and hybrid delivery groups and that the sample was stratified by delivery method, questionnaire length, demographic-item placement, and recorded gender, apparently yielding 24 cells of 300 intended recipients each.  
   - However, the method used to generate the allocation sequence is not described. There is no information on whether a computer-generated random sequence, random-number table, or any other genuinely random procedure was used.  
   - Allocation concealment is also not described. Although blinding of participants to delivery mode would not be feasible, the absence of information about who assigned sampled pharmacists to delivery methods and whether assignment could have been predicted or manipulated leaves uncertainty.  
   - More importantly, the delivery groups were drawn using different commercial databases: MMS supplied the postal and hybrid samples, whereas Statlistics supplied the email sample. This creates concern that delivery-method comparisons may partly reflect differences in sampling frames, coverage, or database characteristics, rather than randomized assignment alone. The observed differences in respondent characteristics across delivery modes—for example, the proportion of Black respondents was 3.2% for postal mail, 26.4% for email, and 19.2% for hybrid—are compatible with materially different underlying sampling frames, although they could also partly reflect differential response.  
   - Therefore, randomization is claimed but insufficiently documented, and comparability of the groups is uncertain.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
   - The interventions were delivery strategies, and the procedures are reasonably clearly specified: postal mail involved an initial questionnaire, a second questionnaire at two weeks for nonresponders, and a later reminder postcard; email involved an invitation and survey link, a repeat email, and a final reminder; hybrid involved two mailed postcards directing recipients to an online questionnaire.  
   - These differences in reminders and mode of contact appear to be deliberate components of the intended delivery-method interventions, rather than unintended departures from them.  
   - There is no indication that participants crossed over between assigned delivery methods, that postal recipients were offered email completion, or that email/hybrid recipients were sent postal questionnaires.  
   - Non-delivery of some postal and hybrid mailings is reported, but this is a failure of contact/delivery rather than evidence that those who received the intervention were managed contrary to their assigned strategy. It is more relevant to the handling of outcome data and the estimand than to adherence to the intended intervention.  
   - No co-interventions or differential treatment after allocation are reported that would plausibly alter response rates independently of the assigned delivery procedure.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - For the stated endpoint, response rate, non-return of a questionnaire is itself an observed outcome—namely, nonresponse—rather than conventional missing outcome data. The numbers returned and the numbers of complete questionnaires are reported for each delivery method.  
   - Nevertheless, 233 of 7,200 intended recipients were excluded from the response-rate denominator because questionnaires were undeliverable: 139 in the postal group and 94 in the hybrid group, compared with none in the email group, because email addresses were said to be guaranteed operational. The response-rate calculation therefore conditions on successful delivery, a post-allocation event that differed substantially by delivery mode.  
   - The authors describe undeliverability as resulting from incorrect addresses, deceased persons, and similar reasons, but do not provide a detailed breakdown by reason or demonstrate that all exclusions were unquestionably ineligible before allocation. If some excluded individuals were eligible pharmacists who merely could not be contacted through the assigned mode, excluding them can favour the postal and hybrid strategies in an analysis of response among the initially sampled population.  
   - In addition, 78 returned questionnaires with one or more missing survey items were excluded from the endpoint because the authors defined a response as a questionnaire with no missing data. This definition is explicit and consistently applied, but it combines survey participation with item completeness. The discussion reports that incomplete-response exclusions differed somewhat by delivery method (9.9% postal versus 9.4% email); the corresponding hybrid figure and complete accounting by randomized subgroup are not clearly presented.  
   - Thus, outcome status is largely known, but the exclusion of undelivered questionnaires and incomplete returns from the denominator/response definition introduces uncertainty about whether the reported estimates reflect the intended comparison of delivery methods.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (response rate): low-risk**  
   - The endpoint is objective: whether a completed questionnaire with no missing items was received. It does not depend on participants’ subjective assessment, recall, or interpretation of a health outcome.  
   - Blinding of pharmacists to delivery method is inherently impossible and is not required for objective ascertainment of questionnaire return. Knowledge of the assigned delivery mode may affect the true behavioural outcome—whether a pharmacist responds—but that is the mechanism under investigation, not measurement bias.  
   - Receipt of postal questionnaires and electronic survey submissions should be directly recorded. The manuscript specifies that electronic responses were stored in a master database and that mailed questionnaires were counted when returned.  
   - Although the manuscript does not describe independent verification of data capture or reconciliation procedures, there is no specific indication that response ascertainment differed in a way likely to misclassify completion systematically between groups.  
   - The unusual requirement of “no missing data for any question” affects the endpoint definition, but not, on the available information, the accuracy with which that defined endpoint was measured.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The manuscript states the principal research questions in advance within the article: whether response rate and response time varied by delivery method, questionnaire length, item placement, and gender, and which delivery method was most cost-effective. Response rate is therefore clearly identified as a planned outcome in the report.  
   - The methods also specify the response-rate definition and state that chi-square tests were used to compare rates across the classifications.  
   - However, no trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or dated prespecified analysis document is cited. It is therefore not possible to verify that the exact outcome definition—particularly exclusion of questionnaires with any missing item—or the reported analysis and subgroup/factorial comparisons were specified before the data were examined.  
   - The study includes multiple intervention dimensions and outcomes, creating scope for selective emphasis on statistically significant findings. Delivery method was the only classification reported as statistically significant, while the full prespecified analytic framework, interaction analyses, and any alternative response-rate definitions are not available for verification.  
   - Accordingly, the reported result is plausible and aligned with the stated aims, but selective reporting cannot be ruled out.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - There are some concerns in several important domains: inadequate reporting of random-sequence generation and allocation procedures, likely non-comparability arising from different sampling databases for email versus postal/hybrid groups, differential exclusion of undelivered questionnaires from response-rate denominators, and absence of a protocol or registration permitting assessment of selective reporting.  
   - Although outcome measurement itself is at low risk of bias and there is no clear evidence of unintended deviations from the delivery interventions, the combination of concerns about group comparability, denominator handling, and result selection substantially lowers confidence that the observed differences in response rate are attributable solely to the delivery methods.
