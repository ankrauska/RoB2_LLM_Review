# Risk of Bias Assessment: Number of Mistakes in Diagnosing Problems (Phases 1 and 2)

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns**

Justification: The manuscript states students were "consequently randomised with a randomisation table" in each campus. However, several critical details are missing or concerning:
- No description of allocation concealment procedures — it is unclear whether the person enrolling students could foresee the assignment.
- Baseline imbalances are evident in Table 2 (previous work experience duration, voluntary work experience, and clinical training experience programmed differ significantly between Trento and Udine, p<0.01 for several variables), raising suspicion about whether randomization was effective, particularly for the inter-trial comparison.
- The Udine "intervention" vs "control" split (weekly tutorials n=39 vs routine tutoring n=23) shows an unusual, unequal allocation without explanation of the randomization ratio used.
- No mention of who generated the randomization table or who assigned participants — key for ruling out selection bias in the randomization process itself.

Given the imbalances in Table 2 and lack of methodological detail on concealment, this raises legitimate concern, though it does not reach "high risk" as randomization was reportedly used and groups were largely similar demographically (sex, education).

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**High risk**

Justification: This is a pragmatic, unblinded trial where deviations are substantial and consequential:
- The Trento Faculty experienced "cut-backs" mid-study, reducing tutor numbers and altering the intensity of the intended intervention ("Student exposure to intensive tutorials was reduced and routine tutorials by ward staff... were introduced"). This is a major deviation from the intended intervention that directly affects the primary outcome (diagnostic accuracy) and was not part of the original trial design.
- This deviation was not symmetric across groups and was not analyzed via a proper intention-to-treat framework with sensitivity analysis to account for the contamination.
- No blinding of participants or those delivering interventions (unsurprising for this type of trial) — this by itself is inherent to pragmatic trials, but combined with the unplanned mid-study change in intervention delivery, it substantially undermines confidence that the assigned intervention was actually the exposure being measured.
- No mention of how many students in the "control" group might have been contaminated by exposure to the changed structure.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Some concerns**

Justification: Of 179 enrolled students, only 144 (80.4%) were eligible/included, with exclusions based on eligibility criteria (language, prior work experience, attendance) applied at baseline — these are described as pre-specified eligibility criteria rather than post-randomization dropout, which is reassuring. However:
- The manuscript does not clearly report attrition *between phases* (Phase 0 → Phase 1 → Phase 2) for the outcome of interest. Given three sequential testing points, it is unclear whether all 144 students completed all three case evaluations, and whether the denominators reported in Table 3/4 (which vary: 82, 62, 41, 41, 23, 39) reflect any dropout during the study or simply the randomized allocation.
- No CONSORT-style accounting of missing outcome data (i.e., how many students who were randomized failed to complete the Phase 1 or Phase 2 case evaluation) is provided, and no discussion of whether missingness was related to outcome (e.g., poorer-performing students dropping out).

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome
**Some concerns**

Justification: This outcome (number of diagnostic errors) is assessed by researchers against a "standard" list of problems, not self-reported by participants, which reduces (but does not eliminate) concern about participant-reported bias. However:
- The outcome assessors were reported to be blinded ("Two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students... The data were processed blind; only after this had been done were the identities of the Schools... revealed"). This is a strength.
- However, it is unclear whether assessors were blinded to *individual group allocation* (intervention vs. control within each campus), only that they were blinded to campus identity. If assessors could infer group assignment from context (e.g., handwriting styles, case timing, or campus-specific test administration procedures), differential assessment bias could occur.
- The outcome measurement tool itself (agreement with a "standard" list generated by 15 nurses using Cohen's K with a cutoff of 0.80) appears methodologically reasonable, but no inter-rater reliability statistic between the two blinded assessors is reported for the actual scoring of student responses (only for standard-setting).
- Students were not blinded to their own group assignment (impossible in this design), and since the case scenario was reused across phases with modified data, a "trial effect"/practice effect is acknowledged by the authors as a limitation — students could improve simply from repeated exposure to the same case structure, which may differentially affect groups with more contact time (e.g., intensive tutoring groups had more opportunities to discuss similar clinical reasoning approaches).

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Some concerns**

Justification: No trial registration or published protocol is referenced or available for comparison against the methods and results reported.
- The paper reports multiple outcomes (primary: diagnostic errors; secondary: self-perception questionnaire; secondary: in-process data-supporting-hypothesis analysis) but the "in-process analysis evaluating data supporting diagnostic hypotheses" mentioned in Section 3.5 is never reported in the Results — suggesting selective non-reporting of a pre-specified secondary outcome.
- Multiple statistical comparisons (intra-trial, inter-trial, univariate, multivariate) are presented, with results selectively emphasized in the abstract/conclusion (the significant inter-trial OR) while the non-significant intra-trial comparisons (the actually randomized comparisons: OR 1.84, CI 0.62–5.53 for Trento; OR 2.98, CI 0.86–10.74 for Udine) are downplayed in the conclusions, despite these being the only truly randomized comparisons. The headline result (OR 3.75) driving the conclusions is from the **inter-trial** (non-randomized, cross-campus) comparison, which is a comparison between two different populations/curricula, not a randomized comparison — this constitutes a significant risk of selective emphasis on a favorable but confounded result.

## 6. Overall Rating
**High risk of bias**

Justification: While no single domain alone reaches "high risk" definitively, the combination of (a) a major protocol deviation (unplanned mid-trial reduction in intervention intensity at Trento) rated as high risk, (b) baseline imbalances raising questions about randomization integrity, (c) unclear missing data accounting across phases, (d) uncertain blinding robustness during actual outcome scoring, and (e) the critical concern that the headline result relied upon in the abstract/conclusions (OR 3.75) is derived from a **non-randomized, inter-campus comparison** rather than the true randomized comparisons (which were non-significant) — collectively and substantially lower confidence in the reported result for this outcome.
