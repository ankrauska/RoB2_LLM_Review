**Outcome assessed:** number of mistakes in diagnostic hypotheses identified by students in Phases 1 and 2.

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: high-risk**  
   - The manuscript states that, within each campus, students were “randomised with a randomisation table” and that the Phase-2 groups were randomly selected using such a table. This is compatible with a genuinely random sequence, but the method for generating the table is not described sufficiently to verify it.  
   - Crucially, there is no account of **allocation concealment**. It is not stated who held the randomisation list, whether assignments were concealed before enrolment or group allocation, or whether tutors, investigators, or students could foresee assignments. This is particularly important in an educational trial, where knowledge of allocation could affect group constitution or delivery.  
   - More seriously, the Phase-1 comparison that underpins an important reported result was **not randomized**: all Trento students received the nursing-process course plus laboratory session, whereas all Udine students received the course without this formal strategy. Thus, laboratory exposure was completely confounded with faculty/campus. The faculties differed in prior tutorial traditions, implementation context, clinical-training organisation, student characteristics (including voluntary and work experience), and other environmental features.  
   - The authors themselves acknowledge major baseline contextual differences between faculties. Adjustment for a limited set of measured variables cannot resolve confounding by faculty-level characteristics, educational culture, tutors, ward environments, or unmeasured student characteristics.  
   - Therefore, although the within-campus Phase-2 allocation may have used a random table, the reported outcome and conclusions substantially rely on non-randomized inter-campus comparisons. For the endpoint as reported across Phases 1 and 2, this is high risk of bias.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - Students and tutors could not realistically be blinded to tutorial strategy. Knowledge of allocation could alter effort, engagement, expectations, tutor behaviour, and the intensity of support received. Such effects are especially plausible where the intervention explicitly consists of reflective discussion and tutor questioning.  
   - The intervention was complex and multifaceted: tutorial frequency, pedagogical expertise, clinical expertise, group structure, learning setting, and tutor/student ratio all differed. These components may properly be considered part of the intervention package, but they make it difficult to know what was actually delivered and whether the compared groups received the intended contrasts consistently.  
   - Routine tutoring was explicitly **not standardised**. This creates substantial scope for variable delivery within and between tutors and wards. No adherence data are reported—for example, attendance at laboratory sessions, attendance at daily/weekly debriefings, actual duration of tutoring, fidelity to questioning methods, or contamination between groups.  
   - The manuscript says that teaching practices did not change during the study. However, it also describes Trento cut-backs in tutor provision and the introduction of routine tutoring, and states that Udine started weekly tutorials during the study. These changes reinforce concern that implementation and context may have differed from the intended intervention contrast.  
   - There is no clear evidence that deviations were systematically different because of trial participation, but the absence of fidelity/adherence information and the unstandardised control condition prevent a low-risk judgement.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - The report indicates that 144 eligible students entered the study and presents Phase-1 and Phase-2 group totals that sum to 144. It also describes the included group as “stable” during the first six months. This is somewhat reassuring.  
   - Nevertheless, the article does not provide a proper participant-flow accounting for the primary endpoint at each phase: numbers assessed, numbers with analysable diagnostic-hypothesis responses, exclusions after randomization, withdrawals, absences from testing, and reasons for any missing data are not reported clearly. The supplied flowchart description does not remedy this deficiency.  
   - It is therefore impossible to verify whether every randomized student contributed outcome data in Phase 2, whether all students tested in Phase 1 were included, or whether missingness differed by tutorial group.  
   - Because apparent loss to follow-up seems limited but is insufficiently documented, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than low risk.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—number of mistakes in diagnostic hypotheses: low-risk**  
   - The endpoint was based on written responses to a common simulated case, with a fixed 20-minute assessment period. This provides a reasonably standardised measurement procedure across groups at each assessment time.  
   - The manuscript reports that two researchers conducted a **blind evaluation** of students’ identified problems. It also states that data were processed blind and that faculty identities were revealed only after processing. Blinded outcome assessment is important because scoring whether a proposed diagnostic hypothesis is “wrong” can involve judgement.  
   - The standard against which answers were judged was developed independently by a panel of 15 professional nurses, with agreement assessed using Cohen’s kappa and a stated threshold of at least 0.80. This supports the use of a prespecified reference standard rather than outcome assessors deciding correctness after seeing responses.  
   - Students were aware of their tutorial experience, but this outcome is not a participant-reported subjective assessment of benefit, pain, enjoyment, or satisfaction. It is an externally scored performance outcome. Awareness might affect performance, which is relevant to deviations from intervention, but does not in itself imply biased outcome measurement.  
   - Reservations remain: the exact scoring rules, inter-rater agreement between the two study assessors, and handling of disagreements are not reported; moreover, repeated use of a modified version of the same case could introduce learning or recall effects. However, these limitations seem likely to affect groups similarly within a phase and do not overturn the protections offered by blinded scoring and a common standard.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - No trial registration, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospectively dated prespecified outcome-analysis plan is provided. The paper identifies the number of mistaken diagnostic hypotheses as the main outcome, but this alone does not establish that the reported analyses were specified in advance.  
   - The continuous/count outcome was converted into binary outcomes using phase-specific median cut-offs: fewer than 3 versus at least 3 errors in Phase 1, and fewer than 2 versus at least 2 errors in Phase 2. There is no evidence that these cut-offs were prespecified. Dichotomising an outcome at observed medians loses information and creates opportunities to select favourable analytical approaches.  
   - Multiple analyses were available and reported: intra-trial comparisons, inter-trial/campus comparisons, univariate odds ratios, and selectively adjusted multivariable models. The authors’ conclusions place considerable emphasis on favourable inter-campus comparisons, despite those comparisons being non-randomized and heavily confounded.  
   - The report also describes this publication as a “secondary analysis of the original research,” increasing the need for access to the original protocol and analysis plan. None is available here.  
   - There is insufficient evidence to conclude that a particular favourable analysis was definitely selected after examining results; hence some concerns rather than high risk for this domain.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall judgement is **high risk of bias**. The principal reason is the high risk of bias arising from the randomization process for the reported Phase-1 and inter-campus comparisons: exposure to laboratory/tutorial strategies was confounded with faculty, campus, educational culture, tutor expertise, and clinical-learning context rather than randomly allocated. This is compounded by concerns about intervention fidelity, incomplete reporting of missing outcome data, and absence of a prespecified analysis plan. Although blinded outcome scoring is a relative strength, it cannot rescue causal claims based on non-randomized between-faculty comparisons.
