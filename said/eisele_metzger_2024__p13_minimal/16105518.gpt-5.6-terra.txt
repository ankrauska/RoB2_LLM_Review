1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns** (The report states that, after the pre-test, participants “were randomly assigned” to the three practice-schedule groups. However, it provides no information on the random-sequence generation method—e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, or equivalent—and no information on allocation concealment. In an educational experiment conducted in a group setting, unconcealed or predictably generated allocation could permit enrolment or allocation decisions to be influenced. Baseline checklist scores and other baseline characteristics by group are not reported, so baseline balance cannot provide reassurance. Randomization is asserted, but not adequately documented.)

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns** (The interventions were necessarily apparent to participants and faculty: whole-task functional-order practice, random part practice, and blocked part practice are plainly distinguishable. Lack of participant blinding is not, by itself, a bias problem for an objectively assessed performance outcome, but it could affect effort, expectations, or behaviour. The authors state that each participant practised each skill three times for the same duration, which is reassuring regarding nominal practice dose. They also state that an orthopaedic faculty member provided feedback “systematically” during acquisition, but do not explain whether feedback was standardized, whether the faculty member knew group allocation—which seems likely—or whether feedback content and intensity differed between groups. There is also no direct report of adherence to assigned schedules, crossover, or contamination between participants. These omissions leave some concern that differences in implementation, rather than schedule alone, influenced the checklist-score changes.)

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk** (Twenty-eight students were enrolled and randomized, and the report presents results for all three groups at both immediate and delayed post-test assessments. No withdrawals, exclusions after randomization, missed assessments, or missing checklist data are reported. The reported standard errors for each group are consistent with outcome data having been analysed across the groups. Although group-specific denominators are not explicitly provided, there is no indication that missing checklist outcomes occurred, nor any suggestion that outcome availability differed according to intervention or performance.)

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — change in the operation-specific checklist scores: some concerns** (The outcome was assessed at pre-test, immediate post-test, and delayed post-test using an operation-specific checklist of detailed procedural actions. This is more structured and less vulnerable to subjective judgement than a global rating, and the checklist was developed by a panel of three orthopaedic surgeons, which is reassuring for content relevance. Two experienced orthopaedic surgeons performed the assessments. However, the report does not state that assessors were blinded to practice-group allocation, test occasion, or the study hypothesis. Since the intervention concerned the order in which component skills were practised, assessors observing an entire plating task may have been able to infer participants’ allocation from task flow, setup, or performance characteristics. Knowledge or inference of allocation could influence scoring of checklist items that require judgement about whether actions were correctly performed. No inter-rater reliability, independent duplicate scoring procedure, adjudication process, or evidence that assessors were masked is reported. Thus, measurement bias is possible, although the structured nature of the checklist limits the concern.)

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns** (No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospective registration is reported. The manuscript describes several outcome measures—operation-specific checklist, final-product analysis, global ratings, drilling time during tests, and drilling time during acquisition—assessed at more than one post-intervention time point. The methods describe mixed ANOVAs for the checklist difference scores and the results report the stated group and test-phase findings, which provides some reassurance that the presented analysis broadly follows the reported methods. Nevertheless, without a pre-specified protocol or analysis plan, it is not possible to determine whether the selected checklist analysis, the use of change-from-baseline scores, the handling of the two post-tests, the choice of model, or the reported contrasts were selected after examining the data. The reported significant group effect and selected pairwise comparison therefore remain vulnerable to selective-analysis/reporting bias.)

6. **Overall rating: some concerns** (There are some concerns in the randomization, intervention-deviation, outcome-measurement, and selective-reporting domains. Most importantly, allocation concealment and random-sequence generation are undocumented; outcome assessors were not reported to be blinded; and there is no protocol or registration against which to verify the chosen analysis. However, there is no evidence of substantial missing outcome data, no direct evidence that randomization was non-random, no demonstrated deviation from the allocated practice schedules, and no clear indication that unblinded assessors actually altered checklist scoring. On the available report, these issues justify an overall judgement of **some concerns**, rather than high risk.)
