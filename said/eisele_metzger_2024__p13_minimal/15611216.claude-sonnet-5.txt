# Risk of Bias Assessment: Agitation Score (Table 4)

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns**

Randomization was conducted by weekly time-blocks, with blocks then randomly assigned to treatment or control condition. This method is vulnerable to prediction of upcoming allocation by staff/researchers if block-length and sequence were known in advance, and no formal allocation concealment mechanism (e.g., sealed envelopes, central randomization) is described. Critically, despite randomization, the treatment group had significantly greater arousal imbalance at pretest than controls (t = 2.57, p < .01) — precisely the variable most mechanistically linked to the outcome and the variable subsequently used as a covariate to "correct" for this imbalance. A significant baseline imbalance on a prognostically important variable following randomization raises a flag about whether randomization was successfully implemented, even though the authors statistically adjusted for it post hoc.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**High risk**

The design was not truly blinded at the level of intervention delivery — nursing staff who implemented the schedule changes necessarily knew which participants were in the BACE group, creating potential for differential care, attention, or ancillary support to the intervention group (performance bias). Additionally, 10 participants were excluded post-randomization because staff reported the intervention was "not given" due to being short-staffed or busy — this is a per-protocol style exclusion based on adherence, occurring after randomization, and introduces potential bias since reasons for non-delivery may correlate with participant or facility characteristics related to outcome. The analysis was not a true intention-to-treat analysis.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Some concerns**

Of 102 randomized participants, 24 (23.5%) were removed prior to analysis: 12 died, 10 removed for non-delivery of intervention, and 2 excluded for erroneous/missing data. This is a substantial proportion of missingness. The manuscript does not report whether attrition (particularly deaths) was balanced between arms or whether reasons for missingness were related to the outcome (agitation, illness severity). No sensitivity analysis (e.g., worst-case imputation) is presented to demonstrate that missingness did not bias the result.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — Agitation Score (Table 4)
**Some concerns**

Agitation was assessed via direct behavioral observation by trained research assistants (RAs) using a visual analog scale, rather than by participant self-report — this is a strength, as it reduces the risk inherent to subjective self-report in an unblinded design. The authors state RAs were "blinded to treatment and control conditions," and interrater reliability was good (.95–.984). However, true blinding of observers is questionable in practice: RAs recorded participant activities in real time within the facility, and the BACE intervention involved visibly altering the daily activity schedule (adding/removing rest periods or stimulating activities). It seems plausible that an attentive observer could infer group assignment from the nature and timing of activities being conducted, undermining effective blinding. No formal check of the success of blinding (e.g., asking RAs to guess allocation) is reported. This creates a realistic risk of detection bias despite the stated blinding procedure.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Some concerns**

No trial registration, published protocol, or a priori statistical analysis plan is referenced anywhere in the manuscript, making it impossible to verify whether the primary outcome, covariates, and analytic model (ANCOVA with arousal imbalance and MMSE as covariates) were pre-specified or chosen post hoc after observing the baseline imbalance. The number of covariates and subsequent addition of a second covariate (therapeutic activities) suggest an iterative/exploratory approach to modelling rather than a pre-specified plan. Numerous additional post hoc analyses (stratified t-tests, Table 5 subgroup analysis by arousal state, three additional interaction models, dose-response regression) were conducted and reported, consistent with a study that explored multiple analytic pathways — raising concern that the reported ANCOVA result (Table 4) may be one of several models tested, selectively emphasized in the abstract. Additionally, an internal inconsistency exists between the reported p-value in the abstract/Discussion (p = .043/.046) and Table 4 note (p = .43, likely a typographical error), further suggesting a lack of rigor in results reporting and inability to trace a locked pre-specified analysis.

---

## 6. Overall rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** Although outcome measurement using blinded behavioral observation is a relative strength, this trial exhibits multiple compounding concerns: a baseline randomization imbalance on a variable mechanistically tied to the outcome, an unblinded intervention-delivery process susceptible to performance bias, post-randomization exclusion of non-adherent participants (deviating from intention-to-treat principles), substantial (23.5%) attrition without demonstration of balance between arms, absence of a pre-registered protocol to verify selective outcome/analysis reporting, and evidence of exploratory/multiple post hoc analyses. The combination of "some concerns" across several domains together with a "high risk" rating for deviations from intended intervention substantially lowers confidence in the reported Group × Time effect on agitation.
