1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns** (The study is described as randomized, using blocks of four, and participants were allocated to four groups: sham, 1 Hz, random 1–5 Hz, and 100 Hz stimulation. Block randomization is compatible with a genuinely random sequence, but the report does not state how the sequence was generated—for example, computer generation or random-number tables. More importantly, it does not describe allocation concealment: who held the allocation schedule, whether device programming was performed independently, or whether recruiters could foresee the next assignment. This matters particularly with small fixed blocks of four, in which assignments can become predictable if concealment is inadequate. Baseline demographic and behavioural characteristics were reported as not statistically different, but this does not establish successful randomization, especially with approximately 9–11 participants per group. Participants and outcome assessors were reportedly blinded, and participant guesses did not differ from chance; this supports blinding after allocation but does not resolve the absence of information on concealment before allocation.)

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk** (The intervention procedures appear to have been applied consistently: active groups received 2 mA tPCS for 20 minutes, differing only in stimulation frequency, and sham involved 30 seconds of stimulation followed by automatic cessation. Participants and the investigator assessing outcomes were said to be blinded. The sham procedure and the formal blinding check provide some reassurance that participants were not generally able to identify their assigned treatment beyond chance. There is no indication of cross-over, non-adherence, co-interventions, early cessation after treatment began, or differential management between groups. Two people withdrew before receiving stimulation and therefore do not represent post-randomization deviations from the assigned intervention. Although staff delivering/programming stimulation are not explicitly said to have been blinded, there is no reported evidence that knowledge of allocation caused differential departures from the intended protocols.)

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns** (Of 40 recruited individuals, 38 completed the stimulation session; two declined participation before stimulation and thus provided no post-randomization outcome data. The denominators reported after randomization total 38. One participant in the random-frequency group was then excluded from analysis because of excessive EEG artefact. Thus, for the interhemispheric-connectivity endpoint, outcome data appear to have been unavailable for 1 of 11 participants (about 9%) in the random-frequency group, leaving an especially small analysed group. Exclusion for EEG artefact may be legitimate, but the report does not state whether artefact was determined using prespecified, objective criteria; whether the decision was made while blinded; whether the excluded participant’s baseline or post-stimulation recording was affected; or whether exclusions differed according to outcome or group. No sensitivity analysis including alternative handling of missing EEG data is reported. The amount of missing data is modest, but in this very small trial even one exclusion could materially affect the estimate and nominal significance level. There is insufficient information to judge the risk as low, but insufficient evidence that missingness was outcome-dependent enough to warrant a high-risk judgement.)

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low-risk**  
   **Outcome measurements assessed separately: interhemispheric EEG coherence/functional connectivity in theta, alpha, low-alpha, high-alpha, low-beta, and high-beta bands; each assessed under eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions, using the specified interhemispheric electrode-pair measurements.** (The endpoint was derived from recorded 64-channel EEG data before and after stimulation, using magnitude-squared coherence calculated by an explicitly described signal-processing approach. These are instrument-derived physiological measurements, not participant-reported subjective outcomes; consequently, participants’ beliefs about treatment are unlikely directly to influence the numerical outcome. The investigator assessing outcomes was reportedly blinded to intervention assignment, and the successful participant blinding assessment adds reassurance against differential behaviour during recording. EEG processing included manual artefact detection and rejection, which can introduce judgement; however, the report states that outcome assessment was blinded, and there is no evidence that artefact handling was differential by group. The report does not provide detailed artefact-rejection thresholds, the duration of analysable recordings, or reproducibility procedures, which limits confidence in technical precision, but these omissions do not by themselves demonstrate differential outcome measurement between intervention groups. Therefore, for measurement bias specifically, the risk is judged low for each listed coherence measure.)

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high-risk** (No trial registration entry, protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prespecified primary outcome is provided. The manuscript itself describes the analyses as exploratory. Interhemispheric coherence was examined across several frequency bands and sub-bands—theta, alpha, low-alpha, high-alpha, low-beta, and high-beta—under two eye conditions and at two electrode-pair locations, alongside EEG-power, behavioural, and adverse-event outcomes. This creates a substantial multiplicity of possible analyses and results. The authors report omnibus group effects for alpha, low-alpha, and theta, then emphasize the favourable random-frequency versus sham comparison for theta (p = 0.0284), as well as further exploratory comparisons of random frequency against 1 Hz and 100 Hz. Low-alpha is also highlighted despite the random-frequency versus sham comparison being borderline (p = 0.05) with a confidence interval that includes the null. Conversely, multiple non-significant bands, sub-bands, interactions, and comparisons are less prominent. The reported post-hoc comparison plan covers only active-versus-sham comparisons, while the apparently favourable random-versus-other-active comparisons are explicitly described as “further exploratory.” In the absence of a prespecified analysis plan and adjustment or a convincing rationale for the many tested measures and contrasts, selection of the reported statistically favourable result is a serious concern.)

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias** (The study has some concerns regarding allocation concealment and missing EEG data, but the principal issue is high risk of bias in selection of the reported result. The endpoint arises from numerous exploratory EEG coherence analyses in a small study, without an available protocol or registration defining the primary coherence measure, frequency band, eye condition, electrode-pair analysis, or comparison of interest. The favourable theta result therefore has a material risk of being a chance-selected finding.)
