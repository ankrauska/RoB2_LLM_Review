# Risk of Bias Assessment: SBGM Frequency and HbA1c Endpoint

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns.** The manuscript states patients were "randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion" after stratification by income category, but no details are provided regarding the actual method of random sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated, random number table) or the mechanism of allocation concealment prior to assignment. Given that the intervention itself (free vs. self-purchased strips) cannot plausibly be concealed from participants once allocated, the phrase "patient-blinded" allocation is dubious — it is unclear whether concealment of the allocation sequence was maintained before assignment. Baseline characteristics (Table 1) appear well balanced, which is reassuring, but the absence of methodological detail on sequence generation/concealment leaves genuine uncertainty.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**High risk.** By design, participants could not be blinded to their allocation (one group received free strips monthly, the other had to purchase them) — this is an inherent, unavoidable feature of this intervention, not merely a flaw, but it creates strong potential for differential motivation, Hawthorne effects, and unequal co-intervention. Critically, insulin dose adjustments were made unequally by patients/providers based on unblinded glucose readings, and the control group was permitted to purchase additional strips "as needed," introducing an uncontrolled, variable co-intervention that could dilute or confound the true effect of strip cost. There is no clear description of protocol adherence monitoring or standardization of care beyond study visits. The analysis attempted intention-to-treat with last-observation-carried-forward, which is a suboptimal method to handle the substantial and differential deviations/dropouts described below.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**High risk.** Dropout was substantial and highly asymmetric between groups: 19% in the NC group vs. 48% in the C (control) group at 12 months — the study itself flags this as "a bigger dropout rate than we anticipated." The authors' own discussion speculates that dropout in the C group was linked to cost-related frustration and poorer glycaemic control, i.e., missingness is plausibly related to the true value of the outcome (informative censoring). This differential, outcome-related attrition substantially undermines confidence in the reported between-group HbA1c and SBGM frequency differences, despite the LOCF/ITT analysis attempted.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome
- **SBGM frequency:** **Low risk.** This outcome was measured objectively via automated glucometer memory download rather than patient self-report, minimizing risk of differential/subjective reporting bias despite the unblinded nature of the intervention.
- **HbA1c:** **Low risk.** This is measured via a standardized laboratory assay (DCA 2000 Analyzer), an objective biochemical measure not susceptible to patient or assessor subjective interpretation. The research nurse did not provide feedback on results to patients, and analysis was withheld until study completion, further limiting outcome-assessment bias for this parameter, even though the nurse/assessor was not clearly described as blinded to group allocation.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Low risk.** SBGM frequency and HbA1c were explicitly prespecified in the Methods as primary endpoints, and sample size calculations were performed a priori for both. No trial registry or protocol is available for cross-checking (predates common registration practice, conducted 1998–2000), but the reported outcomes match the stated endpoints and pre-specified sample size/power calculations, with no evidence of selective outcome switching or suppression of contradictory analyses.

## 6. Overall rating
**High risk of bias.**

**Justification:** While the outcome measurements themselves (glucometer-downloaded SBGM frequency and laboratory-measured HbA1c) are objective and at low risk of measurement bias, and outcome reporting appears consistent with prespecified endpoints, the combination of (a) unclear randomization/concealment methodology, (b) an intervention that inherently precludes blinding and permitted unequal co-interventions (self-purchase of strips, unblinded insulin adjustments), and (c) a large, differential, and plausibly outcome-related dropout rate (48% vs. 19%) collectively and substantially undermine confidence in the reported treatment effect on SBGM frequency and HbA1c. This combination of concerns across multiple domains meets the threshold for an overall "high risk" rating.
