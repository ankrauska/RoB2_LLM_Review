# Risk of Bias Assessment: Simvastatin as Adjuvant Therapy in RRMS
## Outcome: Attack Number

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns**

The randomization method is described as "computer-based blocking method (four blocks)," which suggests genuine randomization. Allocation concealment is stated ("separate physicians were assigned to randomly allocate patients") and medications were provided in "similar packages" to maintain blinding. However, baseline characteristics (Table 1) show a notable imbalance in baseline Gd+ lesions (0.85 vs 1.3, p=0.28) and baseline EDSS (1.6 vs 1.2, p=0.13) that, while not statistically significant, favor the simvastatin group having milder baseline Gd+ enhancement trends and could reflect incomplete randomization success or chance imbalance given the modest sample size. The block size of four raises a minor concern about predictability of allocation despite the stated separate-physician concealment. Overall, the description is reasonably robust but lacks full detail on concealment mechanisms (e.g., who generated the random sequence, sequentially numbered/sealed containers), warranting "some concerns" rather than "low risk."

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns**

All patients continued their background IFNb1a; only the statin/placebo was randomized, which is appropriate for the adjuvant design. However, per the trial flow: in the simvastatin group, 1 patient did not receive the allocated intervention, and 3 had premature withdrawal due to exacerbation; in the placebo group, 4 did not receive the allocated intervention, and 6 withdrew prematurely due to exacerbation. This differential dropout (unequal in each arm, and higher in placebo) could relate to the outcome itself (attacks leading to withdrawal), raising concern about deviations affecting outcome ascertainment. Analyses were intended to be intention-to-treat, but the study also performed per-protocol and LOCF analyses side-by-side, and the primary reported significant result (p=0.01) appears to combine some ITT-like reasoning with exclusions justified post hoc (e.g., "those who used simvastatin/placebo for at least 6 months were included"). This ad hoc handling of non-adherent patients suggests some deviation from the strict intended treatment protocol, introducing bias risk.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Some concerns**

Of 85 randomized patients, 14 withdrew (5 for unspecified reasons in the first two months, 9 later for relapses/exacerbation/personal reasons). For the primary outcome (attack number), the paper states these dropouts "had taken the provided medications consistently until they withdrew" and were incorporated using ITT/LOCF/per-protocol sensitivity analyses. Missing data appear somewhat imbalanced between arms (per Figure 1: 3 withdrawals in simvastatin vs 6 in placebo due to exacerbation), and dropout for early relapse is directly related to the primary outcome (attacks), which could bias results if reasons for missingness differ systematically between groups (informative missingness). Although multiple analytic approaches (ITT, per-protocol, LOCF) were used and all yielded consistent significant results (suggesting some robustness), the pattern of differential attrition tied directly to the outcome of interest (relapses) raises legitimate concern that missing data could bias the estimated effect, particularly as the placebo group had more early relapse-related dropouts, which mechanically could inflate the between-group difference if not properly accounted for.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — Attack Number
**Low risk**

The primary outcome (relapse/attack number) was assessed by neurologists who were explicitly blinded to treatment allocation ("Four neurologists were asked to assess the patients' eligibility... They were blinded to the patients' assignment to treatment group"). Relapse was defined using an objective, prespecified clinical definition (new/worsening neurological deficit lasting >24h after 1-month stability, excluding fever). Patients were also blinded (double-blind, identical packaging), reducing the risk that patient-reported symptoms prompting relapse evaluation were differentially influenced by knowledge of allocation. Given that outcome assessors and patients were both blinded, and the outcome definition was objective and applied consistently, risk of bias in measurement for this specific outcome is low.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Some concerns**

The trial registration (NCT00668343) lists the primary outcome as "attack number" over 1 year, consistent with the primary outcome reported in the manuscript, which is reassuring. However, several concerns arise: (a) The registry indicates enrollment of 80 patients (estimated), while the manuscript reports 85 enrolled — a minor discrepancy that is unexplained. (b) The registry does not pre-specify the statistical analysis plan in detail (e.jpg., no mention of planned MANOVA, ANCOVA controlling for baseline EDSS, or multiple analytic approaches such as ITT/per-protocol/LOCF), yet the manuscript reports multiple analytic techniques for the same outcome (Mann-Whitney U on ITT dataset, per-protocol Mann-Whitney, and LOCF Mann-Whitney across 3-month intervals) with three separate p-values (0.01, 0.004, 0.03) for what is ostensibly the same primary outcome. This raises concern about potential selective emphasis on the most favorable analysis or possible outcome-reporting flexibility, even though all three point in the same direction. (c) The abstract/results emphasize the significant relapse-rate finding prominently while other pre-specified outcomes (EDSS, MRI lesions) are reported as non-significant, but the framing (both in-text and in the "Class I evidence" statement) potentially overstates the confirmed benefit given the exploratory multiplicity of analyses. This pattern constitutes a legitimate "some concerns" rating.

---

## 6. Overall Rating: **High risk**

**Justification:** While no single domain was rated "high risk" outright, multiple domains — randomization process, deviations from intended intervention, missing outcome data, and selection of reported result — were all rated "some concerns" simultaneously. The combination of (i) baseline imbalances suggestive of incomplete randomization, (ii) differential and outcome-related attrition between arms, (iii) multiple overlapping analytic strategies for the same primary outcome without a clearly pre-specified rationale, and (iv) potential selective emphasis on the most favorable analysis substantially lowers confidence in the reported effect of simvastatin on attack number. Per the stated rating rules, concerns across multiple domains that cumulatively undermine confidence in the result justify escalation to "high risk" overall, despite low risk of bias in the specific outcome measurement itself.
