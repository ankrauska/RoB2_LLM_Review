**Endpoint assessed:** Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) total score at 8-week post-intervention assessment.

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The trial is described as randomized, with 105 participants allocated to each group. Baseline BDI total scores were reasonably similar (wait-list mean 30.02; Deprexis mean 28.81; *p* > .4), as were the reported demographic and clinical baseline variables. This is compatible with successful random allocation.  
   - However, the allocation procedure is inadequately described. Participants were “randomly allocated … in consecutive order,” but the manuscript does not state how the random sequence was generated (for example, computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, or other genuinely random method). “Consecutive order” could imply a predictable allocation process unless coupled with a concealed random sequence.  
   - Allocation concealment is also not described. It is unclear whether the person or system enrolling participants could foresee the next assignment. The post-baseline assignment may have been automated, but that cannot be assumed from the report.  
   - The registration records single masking of an “outcomes assessor,” but the BDI was self-completed online by participants; this does not resolve concerns about concealment at randomization.  
   - Thus, although there is no observed baseline imbalance suggesting a serious failure of randomization, the absence of a clear random-sequence-generation and allocation-concealment description leaves **some concerns**.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - Participants necessarily knew whether they had immediate access to Deprexis or had been placed on a wait-list. This was an open-label comparison of an active, interactive psychological intervention with delayed access.  
   - Knowledge of allocation could plausibly alter behaviour beyond use of Deprexis: intervention participants may have had increased expectations or motivation, while wait-list participants may have sought other treatment, disengaged, or altered questionnaire responses. The authors themselves speculate that substantial improvement in wait-list participants, particularly those with severe depression, may have reflected receipt of effective additional treatment.  
   - Concurrent psychotherapy and psychotropic medication were permitted. This is not intrinsically inappropriate in a pragmatic trial, but treatment changes and use of additional care during the 8-week period were not reported by randomized group. Consequently, it is not possible to judge whether important co-interventions differed between groups after allocation.  
   - The planned intervention was broadly delivered: intervention participants received access vouchers and, among completers, reportedly completed a mean of 6.32 sessions. The per-protocol analysis required only one log-in, which is a very weak definition of adherence, but the principal intention-to-treat analysis retained all randomized participants with baseline data through imputation.  
   - There is no direct evidence of major differential deviations caused by the trial context, but the absence of blinding, the wait-list design, and unmeasured post-randomization co-interventions create **some concerns**.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - BDI post-intervention data were available for 170 of 210 randomized participants (81.0%): 90/105 (85.7%) in the wait-list group and 80/105 (76.2%; reported in Table 1 as 78.1%, apparently due to rounding or a reporting inconsistency) in the Deprexis group. Thus, missing outcome data were more frequent in the intervention group—approximately 24% versus 14%.  
   - The overall attrition level is not trivial, and the between-group difference in attrition could matter for a subjective depression outcome. Reasons for non-completion are not reported. Therefore, it is unknown whether missingness was related to the unobserved 8-week BDI score—for example, lack of benefit, worsening symptoms, dissatisfaction, or illness severity.  
   - The authors state that non-completers did not differ from completers on measured baseline demographic, medical, or psychopathological variables, apart from two quality-of-life subscales. This provides some reassurance, but baseline similarity cannot establish that missing BDI outcomes were unrelated to post-randomization outcomes.  
   - They conducted multiple imputation for the ITT analysis, generating 100 datasets stratified by treatment group. However, the imputation model, included predictors, assumed missing-data mechanism, and diagnostic checks are not sufficiently described. Multiple imputation does not itself eliminate bias if missingness depends on unobserved outcomes.  
   - The ITT result (*p* = .03; *d* = .36) was similar to the complete-case result (*p* = .02; *d* = .36), which is reassuring, but does not adequately resolve the uncertainty caused by differential, unexplained attrition. Therefore, **some concerns** remain.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — Beck Depression Inventory total score: high risk**  
   - The BDI total score is a participant-reported measure of depressive symptoms. Although it is a well-established and psychometrically supported questionnaire, it is inherently subjective and susceptible to expectations, demand characteristics, and knowledge of treatment allocation.  
   - Participants were not blinded. They were explicitly informed whether they would receive immediate Deprexis access or would wait eight weeks for access. The intervention was designed to be psychologically active and involved repeated engagement with depression-related material; the control was a wait-list.  
   - The trial registration’s reference to single masking of an outcome assessor offers no meaningful protection for this endpoint because the BDI was completed directly by participants in an online survey, not scored through a blinded clinician assessment.  
   - The authors acknowledge that the primary outcome rested “exclusively on self-report” and that no independent clinician ratings were obtained. Good reported test–retest reliability in the wait-list group does not address differential measurement bias between unblinded groups. Reliability is not blinding.  
   - The subjective-benefit responses among intervention completers also demonstrate that participants formed views about whether Deprexis had helped them; such beliefs can influence a self-rated BDI score.  
   - Because an unblinded, subjective self-report outcome was used in an immediate-treatment versus wait-list comparison, the risk that outcome measurement was influenced by knowledge of allocation is **high risk**.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The trial was registered prospectively (first posted July 2011) and specified depressive-symptom severity assessed with the BDI as the primary outcome. The manuscript correspondingly identifies BDI as the primary outcome and reports the BDI total-score result at the intended 8-week reassessment. This is favourable.  
   - Nevertheless, the registry entry is not sufficiently specific to verify that the reported analysis was fully prespecified. It identifies “severity of depressive symptoms” assessed with the BDI, but does not specify the BDI total score rather than subscales, the exact primary comparison model, covariate adjustment, handling of missing data, or whether the ITT or per-protocol analysis was primary.  
   - The manuscript reports several analytic approaches and results: complete-case/per-protocol ANCOVA, multiply imputed ITT ANCOVA, BDI subscales, response defined as at least 50% reduction, diagnostic-confirmation subgroup analyses, and several moderator analyses. The reported BDI total-score result is consistent across complete-case and imputed analyses, which reduces concern; however, without a detailed prespecified statistical analysis plan, it cannot be established that the chosen analysis and presentation were selected without knowledge of the results.  
   - The registry lists a “7 Days” primary-outcome timeframe, whereas the article reports an 8-week endpoint. This may reflect the BDI recall period rather than the follow-up timing, but the registration is ambiguous rather than precise.  
   - Accordingly, there are **some concerns**, rather than evidence of definite selective reporting.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall risk of bias for the BDI total score at post-intervention is **high risk**. The decisive problem is **high risk of bias in measurement of the outcome**: participants knew their allocation and reported a subjective depression outcome in an immediate-intervention versus wait-list comparison. This concern is compounded by insufficiently documented randomization/concealment, potential allocation-related behavioural and co-intervention differences, and differential unexplained missing outcome data.
