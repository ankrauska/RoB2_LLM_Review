Based on the provided manuscript, here is the assessment of the risk of bias for the outcome of visual acuity:

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**
The manuscript reports on a 5-year extension of a "Multicenter randomized clinical trial". Random assignment was performed, and the authors state that "some of the strengths of this study include a study design involving randomization of the two treatment groups at baseline to try to minimize confounding and bias". Since the trial evaluated different laser treatment timings (prompt vs. deferred) combined with ranibizumab, strict blinding of patients to the laser intervention might not have been fully possible, but the groups were properly randomized at baseline, and visual acuity examiners were masked (see Domain 4). There is no indication of subversion of the randomization process or baseline imbalances that could not be accounted for among the randomized population.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**
The study was conducted with a clear protocol where treatments (injections and laser) were given based on protocol-defined criteria related to visual acuity and OCT. Both groups received ranibizumab according to the protocol (every 4 weeks until no longer improving, etc.). Laser was prompt or deferred according to assignment, and laser was initiated in the deferred group if criteria were met after 24 weeks. There is no evidence of systematic deviations from the intended interventions that would compromise the comparative findings between prompt and deferred laser strategies. 

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**
The study reports on a 5-year extension. The 5-year completion rate was 76% in the ranibizumab + prompt laser group and 74% in the ranibizumab + deferred laser group based on the original participants randomized (excluding deaths). While high among those who consented to the extension (97% and 92%), approximately 25% of the originally randomized eyes are missing 5-year data. The authors acknowledge this limitation: "the 5-year data is absent on about a quarter (excluding deaths) of all randomized eyes mostly due to participants who chose not to continue in the extension phase of the study." They note differences between participants who did and did not have a 5-year visit (Table 1), which they attempted to adjust for in longitudinal models, but the missingness rate is substantial enough to raise some concerns about attrition bias over the 5 years.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (visual acuity): low-risk**
The outcome, best-corrected visual acuity, is an objective, standardized measure. The authors state that "masking of the visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians" was a strength of the study. Since the examiners assessing visual acuity were masked to the treatment assignment, the risk of bias in the measurement of the outcome is low.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: low-risk**
The results reported correspond to the predefined outcomes of the trial (best-corrected visual acuity and OCT measures). The protocol is available on the DRCR.net website. The longitudinal analyses and various time points are clearly reported without evidence of selective outcome reporting or selective analysis reporting. Results are presented comprehensively for the planned 5-year timepoints and endpoints.

**6. Overall rating: some concerns**
The overall risk of bias is judged to have 'some concerns'. While randomization, intervention adherence, outcome measurement (with masked examiners), and reporting are robust (low risk), there are some concerns regarding missing outcome data due to a ~25% attrition rate over the 5-year follow-up period, with known differences between those who completed and those who dropped out.
