**Outcome assessed:** Motor Assessment Scale item 6 (Upper Arm Function; MAS6), measured at baseline (week 0), post-training (week 4), and follow-up (week 12).

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
- The study reports use of a computer-generated random-number sequence with 1:1 allocation to active tRNS plus reaching training or sham tRNS plus reaching training. This is an appropriate method of sequence generation.  
- Allocation concealment was described: an off-site investigator prepared the sequence, and consecutively numbered opaque envelopes containing allocation were collected by the unblinded tRNS intervener only after baseline assessment.  
- Participants, trainers, and outcome assessors were said to be blinded to allocation. The allocation code was not shared with the main investigator team until training and follow-up assessment had been completed.  
- The very small sample makes chance baseline imbalance highly likely, but this is an imprecision issue rather than evidence that randomization was compromised.  
- Although the envelope procedure is less secure than central allocation, there is no reported evidence that envelopes were transparent, unsealed, opened prematurely, or otherwise manipulated.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
- Both trial groups were intended to receive the same reaching-training programme, including outcome-triggered peripheral electrical stimulation as required; the only intended difference was active tRNS (2 mA) versus sham tRNS (0 mA).  
- All four reported participants completed all 12 training sessions, and the number of repetitions was recorded. Thus, there is no evidence of non-adherence, missed sessions, crossover, or discontinuation among the reported participants.  
- Trainers and participants were reportedly blinded, while a separate tRNS intervener was necessarily unblinded. Separation of the tRNS log from training and assessment records was sensible and reduces the likelihood that allocation influenced training delivery or assessment.  
- Nevertheless, sham stimulation involved **0 mA**, whereas active stimulation was delivered at 2 mA with no fade-in/fade-out. This may have produced different sensations and could have undermined participant blinding. Participants were told that they might or might not feel stimulation regardless of allocation, but no formal assessment of the success of blinding was reported.  
- If participants discerned their allocation, expectations or motivation could have influenced effort during a performance-based arm-function assessment such as MAS6. There is no direct evidence that this happened, but the absence of a blinding check leaves some concern.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high-risk**  
- In the manuscript, all four reported participants completed all 12 treatment sessions and apparently underwent the scheduled MAS6 assessments at baseline, post-training, and follow-up. On the face of the manuscript alone, there are no missing MAS6 measurements among these four cases.  
- However, the prospectively registered trial record specifies a final sample size of **8**, whereas the manuscript reports only **4** recruited participants and presents results for only P01–P04. The report provides no CONSORT-style participant flow, no explanation for this discrepancy, and no account of whether four further enrolled/randomized participants existed, withdrew, were excluded, or had unavailable outcomes.  
- This discrepancy is material. If eight participants were indeed recruited/randomized, outcome data or entire participant records for half of the registered final sample are absent from the publication. In such a tiny trial, exclusion or non-reporting of even one participant could substantially alter apparent findings; omission of four would be especially serious.  
- The available information does not permit confidence that missing participants or MAS6 data were unrelated to outcome. The risk that missing data could depend on the true outcome, and materially bias the result, is therefore high.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (MAS6): low-risk**  
- MAS6 is a clinician-assessed measure of upper-arm function, not a participant-reported subjective outcome such as pain, enjoyment, or quality of life. It is therefore less directly vulnerable than self-reported outcomes to participants’ expectations about active versus sham stimulation.  
- The manuscript states that personnel involved in assessment were blinded to group allocation throughout the study. Allocation was reportedly not revealed to the investigator team until after training and follow-up assessments were completed.  
- The outcome assessors were thus appropriately shielded from the intervention assignment, and the study gives no indication that assessors became unblinded.  
- MAS6 is nevertheless a performance-based ordinal clinical scale, so participant effort and assessor judgement can influence scores. The questionable credibility of completely inert sham stimulation creates a theoretical route by which participant expectations could affect performance. However, this does not amount to evidence that measurement itself was biased, and the assessors were blinded.  
- No separate assessor-training, duplicate-rating, or video-rating procedures are described, but these omissions do not by themselves establish a serious measurement bias for this outcome.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high-risk**  
- The trial was prospectively registered before recruitment. The registry defined the primary clinical outcome as a **composite score of MAS upper-limb items 6–8**, measured at baseline, week 4, and week 12.  
- In contrast, the manuscript identifies **MAS6 alone** as the primary clinical outcome and redesignates MAS items 7 and 8 as secondary outcomes. The registered composite primary outcome is not reported as such. This is an unexplained departure from the pre-specified outcome definition.  
- The registry also specified an intention-to-treat analysis and a non-parametric mixed-effects model using a two-group (active/sham) by three-timepoint design, followed by between-group comparisons. The manuscript instead presents descriptive, participant-by-participant changes and does not report the planned group comparison or the registered analytical approach.  
- While the sample size was plainly inadequate for meaningful inferential analysis, that does not justify silently replacing the registered primary composite outcome with a component outcome, nor failing to explain the change in analysis.  
- The unexplained inconsistency between the registered final sample size of eight and the four reported participants compounds concern that the outcomes and analyses shown may have been selected from a wider set of data or planned analyses.

**6. Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
The overall risk of bias for MAS6 is **high-risk**. Random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinded assessment, and reported session completion are broadly reassuring. However, the unexplained discrepancy between the registered final sample size of eight and the four participants reported creates a high risk of bias from missing outcome data. In addition, the manuscript departs materially from the prospectively registered primary outcome (MAS6–8 composite) and planned analysis without explanation. These problems substantially lower confidence in the reported MAS6 findings.
