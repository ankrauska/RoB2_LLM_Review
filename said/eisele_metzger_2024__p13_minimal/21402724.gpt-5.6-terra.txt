**Outcome assessed:** reduction of psychiatric symptom severity, measured using the **Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI)**. The manuscript reports both the BSI Global Severity Index (GSI) and BSI Positive Symptom Total (PST); the trial registry specifically identifies PST as the primary psychiatric-symptom outcome.

1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: low-risk**  
   - Participants were said to be allocated using a random sequence programmed into the computer-assisted interview software. This is a credible random allocation mechanism.  
   - Allocation concealment appears adequate: randomization occurred only after the baseline interview, and the software prevented interviewers and participants from knowing assignment before it occurred.  
   - The reported baseline characteristics, including baseline BSI scores and service use, were broadly balanced between groups. The intervention-condition coefficient for both BSI outcomes was also non-significant, consistent with no material baseline imbalance in the analysed sample.  
   - Although details such as blocking, stratification, sequence generation algorithm, and safeguards against subversion are not reported, the description of computer-generated assignment with concealment is sufficiently reassuring.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The analysis was described as intention-to-treat: all 519 participants with baseline data were retained in the mixed-effects analyses, including 43/276 (16%) allocated to WRAP who did not receive the intervention. This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to WRAP.  
   - However, adherence to WRAP was incomplete: intervention participants attended a mean of only 5.05 of 8 sessions, and 16% received no sessions. This is not itself a bias if analysed by allocation, but it makes interpretation as the effect of receiving a full WRAP programme difficult.  
   - Participants necessarily knew whether they had been offered and were attending WRAP. In a wait-list design, intervention recipients also received substantially more contact, peer interaction, expectation of benefit, and attention than controls. These are potentially important components of the effect of assignment, but prevent attribution specifically to the WRAP curriculum.  
   - Contamination or differential co-intervention is possible. About 42% and 45% of controls reported participation in peer-led support groups over the two follow-up intervals. The authors state that models adjusted for this exposure and results “did not differ substantially,” which is somewhat reassuring, but the actual sensitivity-analysis estimates are not fully reported. Comparable post-randomization service use by allocation group is not comprehensively reported.  
   - Therefore, the ITT approach is appropriate, but incomplete information about post-randomization co-interventions and the inherently unblinded wait-list comparison leaves **some concerns**.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   - Follow-up for the BSI was reasonably high: at the first post-intervention assessment, BSI data were available for 224/251 (89.2%) intervention and 234/268 (87.3%) control participants; at the later assessment, they were available for 220/251 (87.6%) and 228/268 (85.1%), respectively.  
   - Missingness was not appreciably different between groups according to the reported comparisons. Overall loss was somewhat greater in the WRAP arm (25 participants) than in control (11 participants), but reported follow-up completion percentages did not show a statistically significant difference.  
   - The authors give plausible general reasons for loss to follow-up—death/ill health, moving, and withdrawal—and note that many intervention-arm losses had not attended WRAP. This may reduce concern that poor outcomes after substantial intervention exposure were selectively missing, but it does not establish that missing BSI values were unrelated to the unobserved outcome.  
   - Mixed-effects random-regression models included participants with incomplete repeated measurements, but this method relies on a missing-at-random assumption conditional on included variables. The report does not present analyses of whether BSI severity, baseline characteristics, or early changes predicted missingness, nor sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-not-at-random scenarios.  
   - Thus, the amount and balance of missing data are not sufficiently alarming for a high-risk judgement, but the assumptions needed to handle the missing BSI outcomes are not adequately examined.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome (BSI psychiatric symptom severity): high-risk**  
   - The BSI is a participant-reported measure of how bothered the respondent was by psychiatric symptoms during the preceding week. Psychiatric symptom severity as assessed by self-report is inherently subjective and highly susceptible to expectations, demand characteristics, social desirability, and perceived allegiance to peer facilitators.  
   - Participants were not blinded. They knew whether they were receiving the peer-led WRAP programme or were waiting to receive it later. The trial used a wait-list/usual-care control rather than an attention-matched or credible alternative intervention.  
   - Interviewers were nominally blinded, and assignment was reportedly disclosed in only 4% of follow-up interviews. This reduces the likelihood of interviewer-induced bias, but it does not address the main threat: biased self-report by unblinded participants.  
   - The BSI was not corroborated by blinded clinician ratings, medical records, or other independent outcome assessment. The authors themselves acknowledge that reliance on uncorroborated participant self-report is a study limitation.  
   - This is particularly problematic because the intervention explicitly promotes wellness planning, hope, self-management, and positive reinterpretation of symptoms—constructs that may influence how participants perceive or report symptoms even without a commensurate change in underlying clinical severity.  
   - Accordingly, for the specified BSI endpoint, outcome measurement is at **high risk of bias**.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: high-risk**  
   - The trial was registered only in December 2009, after the actual primary completion date (September 2009) and completion date (October 2009). Hence, the registry was retrospective and cannot reliably demonstrate that outcomes and analyses were specified before outcome data were available.  
   - The article states that outcomes, hypotheses, and analyses were prespecified in the funding proposal. However, that proposal and a dated statistical analysis plan are not provided, so this assertion cannot be independently checked.  
   - There is an important discrepancy between registry and publication emphasis. The registry specifies the BSI **Positive Symptom Score/Total** as the primary psychiatric symptom outcome. The manuscript describes psychiatric-symptom reduction more broadly as the primary outcome and reports both the PST and the BSI GSI, with both intervention-by-time tests statistically significant. The GSI does not appear as the registered primary BSI measure.  
   - The study assessed multiple outcomes, subscales, and two post-baseline time points, and it used a longitudinal interaction model. The report does not provide a prospectively specified hierarchy of BSI measures, a clear single primary time point, multiplicity handling, or a prespecified rule governing which BSI result would be emphasised.  
   - The paper does report both BSI measures rather than selectively omitting the non-significant BSI outcome; nevertheless, retrospective registration, the unverified claim of prespecification, and ambiguity over the primary BSI metric and analysis create a substantial risk that the reported favourable result was selected from multiple eligible analyses.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   The overall judgement is **high risk of bias**, principally because the BSI outcome is a subjective participant-reported psychiatric-symptom measure obtained from participants who were aware of allocation in an unblinded wait-list trial, without independent corroboration. There is also a high risk of selective reporting/analysis selection because registration occurred after trial completion and does not unambiguously prespecify the published BSI outcome hierarchy and analysis. Missing data and deviations from intended intervention introduce additional concerns, although the randomization and allocation concealment procedures appear sound.
