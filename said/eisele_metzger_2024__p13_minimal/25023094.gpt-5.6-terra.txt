1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns** (The report describes sex-stratified randomization and allocation concealment using sealed envelopes, and baseline VAS values were broadly comparable between groups: median 7.0 [IQR 1.9] in control versus 7.4 [IQR 2.3] in active ultrasound; p=0.421. These are reassuring features. However, the account is insufficiently precise for a low-risk judgement. “Simple random selection” is inconsistent or at least unclear alongside “sex-stratified randomization”; no method of sequence generation is given (e.g., computer-generated random numbers), and the envelope procedures are inadequately described. In particular, there is no confirmation that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof and opened only after irrevocable enrolment. Thus, genuinely random generation and secure concealment cannot be verified.)

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns** (Patients reportedly were blinded and received either active or sham ultrasound during the first 10 sessions. Both groups were intended to receive otherwise identical care—microwaves, massage and exercises—and both subsequently received 15 sessions without ultrasound. This design reduces the risk that systematic differences in prescribed co-interventions explain the VAS result. However, the treating physiotherapist could not be blinded, as explicitly acknowledged by the authors. This is important because massage and exercise have scope for variation in delivery, encouragement and therapeutic interaction, and the therapist’s knowledge of allocation could have influenced these elements or participants’ expectations. No data are provided on adherence, treatment fidelity, session attendance by group, or whether the sham successfully maintained patient blinding. There is no clear evidence of actual important departures, so this is not necessarily high risk, but the safeguards against them are insufficiently documented.)

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk** (The flowchart and Methods state that 54 eligible consenting participants were randomized, 27 per group, and that “no patient was lost or dropped out.” VAS improvement results are presented for both groups at 10 sessions and at discharge after 25 sessions, consistent with complete follow-up. Therefore, missing VAS data are not apparent and cannot plausibly have biased the reported VAS comparison. The statement that analysis was by intention to treat is compatible with this, although it adds little when no outcome data are missing.)

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome—pain visual analogue scale (VAS): low risk** (VAS pain is a participant-reported, subjective outcome and would be highly vulnerable to bias if participants knew, or could reliably infer, their allocated intervention. Here, the investigators state that patients were blinded, and the control intervention used sham ultrasound with the unit switched off. VAS assessments were undertaken by a physiotherapist assessor described as blinded, before treatment, after 5 and 10 sessions, and at discharge. The outcome method itself is appropriate and was applied at the same scheduled time points in both groups. Although blinding success was not tested, and an unblinded treating physiotherapist might inadvertently influence expectations, the manuscript does not provide evidence that participants or the assessor were unblinded. On the information available, measurement of the VAS is unlikely to have been differentially biased between groups. This judgement applies specifically to the VAS; it does not automatically extend to the separately reported algometry outcomes.)

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns** (No prospective trial registration, protocol, or prespecified statistical analysis plan is reported. The Methods identify several pain outcomes—VAS and four algometry measurements—at several time points, and also multiple mobility outcomes. The paper reports no statistically significant VAS difference after the 10-session ultrasound phase (one-sided p=0.373), but emphasizes the statistically significant VAS difference at discharge after a further 15 sessions during which neither group received ultrasound (one-sided p=0.021). Without a prespecified primary time point and primary pain measure, it is impossible to know whether the discharge VAS analysis was selected because it was favourable. Further concern arises from use of one-sided significance tests, which can make positive findings easier to obtain and are not clearly justified in advance. The VAS results themselves appear tabulated at the stated principal time points, so there is not sufficient evidence for a high-risk judgement; nevertheless, selective choice among outcomes, time points, and analyses cannot be excluded.)

6. **Overall rating: some concerns** (No domain is judged high risk, and missing VAS data and VAS measurement are judged low risk. However, there are unresolved concerns about the randomization/concealment procedures, possible therapist-related deviations from intended treatment in an otherwise blinded trial, and—most importantly—the lack of a protocol or registration in the context of multiple outcomes and follow-up time points. These concerns reduce confidence in the reported delayed VAS benefit of active ultrasound.)
