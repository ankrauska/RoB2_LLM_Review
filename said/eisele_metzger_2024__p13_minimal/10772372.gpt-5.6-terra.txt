1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - The report states that participants were randomized using “a computer-generated list of random numbers.” This is an acceptable method for generating an unpredictable random sequence.  
   - However, the manuscript does not describe **allocation concealment**: there is no information about who held the randomization list, whether assignments were placed in sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or whether enrolment occurred before the allocation was revealed. Thus, it is not possible to exclude foreknowledge of upcoming assignments and consequent selection into treatment groups.  
   - The trial was necessarily open-label with respect to telephone contact, but lack of participant blinding does not itself invalidate sequence generation.  
   - Baseline characteristics were reported to be similar, but with only 15 participants per group this offers little reassurance about successful randomization or concealment.  
   - **Judgement: some concerns.**

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: low-risk**  
   - The intended intervention was weekly pharmacist telephone follow-up for 12 weeks, compared with no such follow-up. The intervention was explicitly behavioural: reminders, discussion of adverse effects and barriers, and reinforcement of the importance of adherence were integral components rather than unintended co-interventions.  
   - All participants were prescribed the same lipid-lowering regimen and received initial medication and dietary counselling.  
   - There is no indication that control participants received the planned pharmacist calls, that intervention participants failed to receive calls, or that drug treatment differed systematically between groups at baseline.  
   - The authors report that no participants were lost to follow-up. Although the analysis is not explicitly labelled intention-to-treat, all 30 randomized participants appear to have been retained in the reported group comparisons.  
   - The open-label design could influence behaviour, but that behavioural effect is precisely what telephone counselling was intended to produce, rather than a deviation from the intervention.  
   - **Judgement: low-risk.**

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low-risk**  
   - The manuscript explicitly states that none of the 30 enrolled participants was lost to follow-up.  
   - Compliance was assessed at 6 and 12 weeks by pill/packet counts and at 1 and 2 years using pharmacy refill information. The article does not report missing outcome measurements at these scheduled assessments, nor exclusions from the analyses.  
   - There is no evidence that missing compliance data differed between treatment groups or was related to actual compliance behaviour.  
   - The sample is very small, but imprecision is not the same as bias from missing data.  
   - **Judgement: low-risk.**

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: assessed separately for each compliance measurement**

   **a. Compliance at 6 weeks: pill count for lovastatin and packet count for colestipol — some concerns**  
   - The outcome was assessed by returned pill and packet counts. This is more objective than a purely participant-reported adherence questionnaire.  
   - Nevertheless, pill/packet counts are an imperfect proxy for medication consumption: participants can fail to bring unused medication, discard tablets or packets before the visit, or otherwise make adherence appear better than it was.  
   - Participants necessarily knew whether they were receiving weekly pharmacist telephone counselling. The report does not state whether the person performing the counts was blinded to allocation. Indeed, the same pharmacist conducted intervention calls, and it is unclear whether that pharmacist also participated in outcome assessment.  
   - The authors state that counts were “not performed within the patient’s view,” which may limit direct participant influence during counting, but does not establish blinded assessment or eliminate manipulation before the visit.  
   - Since no significant between-group difference was found at 6 weeks, any measurement bias may not have materially favoured the intervention result at this time point; however, blinding and measurement safeguards remain insufficiently described.  
   - **Judgement: some concerns.**

   **b. Compliance at 12 weeks: pill count for lovastatin and packet count for colestipol — some concerns**  
   - The same considerations apply as at 6 weeks.  
   - Participants were aware of their allocated intervention, and assessor blinding was not reported.  
   - Pill/packet counts do not demonstrate ingestion and can be influenced by participant handling of unused medication.  
   - The lack of a statistically significant difference at 12 weeks reduces concern that differential measurement produced a positive short-term intervention effect, but it does not resolve uncertainty about the validity of measurement procedures.  
   - **Judgement: some concerns.**

   **c. Compliance at 1 year: pharmacy refill records — low-risk**  
   - Long-term compliance was based on pharmacy refill records, using the prespecified operational rule that filling at least 80% of prescriptions indicated compliance.  
   - Refill data are not direct evidence that medication was consumed, but they are relatively objective records and are much less vulnerable to expectancy effects, participant self-report bias, or an unblinded outcome assessor than pill counts.  
   - The outcome definition is clearly described and appears to have been applied similarly in both groups.  
   - The manuscript does not state whether extraction of refill records was blinded to group assignment. However, because refill records are externally documented and the criterion is largely mechanical, any influence of assessor awareness is unlikely to be important.  
   - **Judgement: low-risk.**

   **d. Compliance at 2 years: pharmacy refill records — low-risk**  
   - The same objective refill-record method and ≥80% threshold were used at 2 years.  
   - The principal favourable compliance finding reported by the authors concerns these longer-term refill-based outcomes. These are less susceptible than subjective or participant-reported outcomes to lack of blinding.  
   - Refill behaviour is not identical to medication ingestion, but this is a limitation of the compliance construct rather than clear differential measurement bias between randomized groups.  
   - **Judgement: low-risk.**

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - No trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, or prospective trial registration is provided. Therefore, it is not possible to determine whether the reported compliance outcomes, time points, thresholds, drug-specific analyses, and statistical comparisons were specified before outcome data were available.  
   - The study measured compliance separately for two drugs, at four time points, and using two different measurement approaches. It also reports both continuous/percentage compliance information and dichotomous “compliant” versus “noncompliant” outcomes using an 80% threshold. These multiple possible analyses create opportunities for selective emphasis.  
   - The article reports the absence of short-term differences at 6 and 12 weeks and then highlights statistically significant differences at 1 and 2 years. This may be a legitimate temporal pattern, but without a prespecified analysis plan one cannot exclude selective choice of favourable time points, adherence definitions, or comparisons.  
   - There is no direct evidence that results were omitted or altered, so high risk cannot be firmly concluded; nonetheless, the absence of prespecification is important in such a small study with numerous related analyses.  
   - **Judgement: some concerns.**

6. **Overall rating: high-risk of bias**  
   - The overall rating is **high-risk** because there are concerns in multiple important domains: allocation concealment is not documented; short-term pill/packet-count compliance measurement may have been affected by lack of blinding and the limitations of returned-medication counts; and there is no protocol, registration, or prespecified analysis plan to address selective reporting among numerous adherence outcomes and time points.  
   - Although missing data appear not to be a problem and the long-term refill-record outcomes are relatively objective, the combination of uncertainty about randomization concealment and selective reporting—particularly in a trial of only 30 participants—substantially lowers confidence in the reported effect of telephone counselling on compliance.
