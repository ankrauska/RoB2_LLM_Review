**Outcome assessed: effects of supervision on patient care, measured by the team checklist score during the supervised simulation scenario.**

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
The report states that residents “were randomized to one of three levels of supervision,” and group sizes were broadly similar (direct 18, immediately available 16, distant 19). Baseline characteristics appear reasonably comparable, although the distant-supervision group had somewhat more previous simulation experience (median 3 sessions versus 1.5 in the other groups) and a higher baseline confidence score than the immediately-available group. These differences may be chance imbalances in a small study.

However, the crucial details are absent:

1. The method used to generate the allocation sequence is not reported. “Randomized” alone does not establish that allocation was truly random rather than predictable or quasi-random.
2. There is no account of allocation concealment. In particular, it is not stated who generated the sequence, who enrolled residents, or whether the supervising fellows, investigators, or residents could foresee the forthcoming allocation before enrolment.
3. Neither participants nor supervisors could be blinded to the allocated supervision condition, because the intervention was defined by the supervisor’s physical proximity. Lack of participant/supervisor blinding is unavoidable here, but makes robust allocation concealment especially important.

Thus, random allocation is claimed, but the evidence is insufficient to rule out selection bias.

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
The intervention was defined as direct, immediately available, or distant supervision. Direct supervision involved the fellow being in the room from the beginning; immediately available supervision allowed telephone access and bedside attendance within 1 minute; distant supervision allowed telephone access only.

There is evidence that the interventions were implemented differently across groups in the expected direction. However, implementation within the “immediately available” group varied substantially:

1. Two of 16 residents did not call the supervisor.
2. Four obtained advice by telephone only.
3. Ten were assisted at the bedside, including four within 5 minutes of scenario onset.

This variability may be part of what “immediately available” supervision was intended to represent, rather than a protocol violation. Nevertheless, it means that the actual exposure was partly determined by residents’ help-seeking behaviour and clinical circumstances, rather than solely by random allocation.

Moreover:

4. Fellows were necessarily aware of the supervision condition and were instructed to balance care and teaching as they would “in real life.” Their clinical actions could therefore be influenced by their beliefs about supervision, resident competence, or the study’s purpose.
5. The team checklist score credits tasks done by *any* team member—resident, fellow, or nurse. Consequently, a superior score under direct supervision may chiefly reflect the fellow being present and performing tasks, which is consistent with the intervention but does not isolate an effect on resident-delivered care.
6. The analysis appears to compare groups according to allocated supervision level, and no exclusions or crossover-related analyses are described. This is reassuring, but the report does not explicitly confirm an intention-to-treat analysis.

For the effect of allocating residents to these pragmatic models of supervision, the deviations are not clearly sufficiently serious to justify a high-risk judgement. However, the variable realization of “immediately available” supervision and inevitable behaviour changes by unblinded fellows leave some concerns.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: low risk**  
There were 53 randomized residents: 18 direct, 16 immediately available, and 19 distant supervision. These numbers are retained in the table reporting the team checklist score, and outcome data are presented for all three groups.

Step-by-step:

1. The randomized group sizes sum to 53, the reported sample size.
2. The team-checklist analysis reports results for all 18, 16, and 19 participants, respectively.
3. There is no indication of withdrawals, missing videotapes, missing checklist ratings, or exclusions from this outcome analysis.
4. Since outcome data appear complete for all randomized residents, there is no evident scope for bias from missing outcome data.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: some concerns**  
The outcome was the proportion of prespecified clinical checklist tasks completed by any team member during the supervised scenario. This is more objective than a global, impressionistic rating, because it is based on whether particular clinical actions were performed. Nevertheless, important concerns remain.

1. The checklist was scenario-specific and was not validated. The authors themselves acknowledge that the checklist development process supported face and content validity but that construct validity was not assessed.
2. The intervention could not be concealed from outcome assessors. Anyone reviewing the videotapes could plainly see whether the supervisor was in the room, arrived later, or was accessible only by telephone.
3. The report describes two trained, independent raters and third-party adjudication of checklist discrepancies. This is a strength, as is videotaping of performances. However, it is not explicitly stated that these procedures applied to the **team checklist score during supervised scenarios**, rather than only to the assessments of independent performance in the subsequent unsupervised scenarios.
4. No inter-rater reliability statistic is reported for the team checklist score. The reported ICC of 0.75 applies to the Ottawa Global Rating Scale, not necessarily to the team checklist.
5. Although checklist items may often be observable, decisions about whether an action was sufficiently performed, timely, or clinically adequate may require judgement. Knowledge of the allocated supervision condition could influence borderline scoring decisions.
6. The outcome is especially susceptible to detection bias in interpretation because the presence of a fellow may make actions more visible, may alter communications, and may influence whether assessors credit team actions as completed.

The outcome is not a participant-reported subjective measure, and it is based on observed actions rather than pain, satisfaction, or a purely subjective judgement. Therefore, the concerns do not clearly reach high risk. But absence of blinded assessment, uncertain applicability of duplicate rating procedures, and use of an unvalidated checklist justify **some concerns**.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
The Methods section identifies team checklist score, time to stabilization of abnormal vital signs, and definitive management as outcomes for patient care during supervised scenarios. The analysis plan specifies a one-way ANOVA for team checklist score across the three supervision groups. The reported result is consistent with that stated analysis: team checklist scores are given for all groups, with an overall p value and pairwise comparisons described in the table footnote.

Nonetheless:

1. No trial registration, dated protocol, or statistical analysis plan is provided. It is therefore impossible to determine whether the team checklist score and its analytic approach were specified before outcome data were examined.
2. The sample-size calculation was based on a different outcome—the Ottawa Global Rating Scale during the subsequent unsupervised scenario—not on the team checklist score, which is the outcome considered here.
3. Several outcomes, analyses, group contrasts, and exploratory multivariable models were undertaken. This creates opportunities for selective emphasis of statistically significant findings.
4. The principal positive patient-care finding is the team checklist score; the other supervised-scenario patient-care outcomes—duration of abnormal vital signs and definitive management—were not statistically significantly different. The manuscript emphasizes the positive checklist-score result in its conclusions.
5. The outcome is nevertheless reported transparently, with group-level data and the prespecified overall comparison, rather than being omitted or replaced by a different measure.

The available report is compatible with the stated analysis having been planned, but without registration or a protocol, selective reporting cannot be confidently excluded.

**6. Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
There are **some concerns** in several domains: inadequate reporting of sequence generation and allocation concealment; variable implementation of the allocated supervision models; unblinded assessment using an unvalidated team checklist with uncertain rating reliability for this specific outcome; and no accessible protocol or registration to verify outcome and analysis selection.

Although missing data are not a concern, the accumulation of concerns in the randomization, outcome-measurement, and selective-reporting domains substantially lowers confidence in the reported superiority of direct and immediately available supervision over distant supervision for team checklist scores. Under the specified decision rule, the overall judgement is therefore **high risk of bias**.
