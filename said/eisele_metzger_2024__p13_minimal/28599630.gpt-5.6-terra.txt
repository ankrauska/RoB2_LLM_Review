1. **Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**  
   - Participants were said to have been “randomly assigned,” with randomization undertaken by a researcher not involved in data collection and stratified by baseline EPDS score and history of depression. This separation is reassuring.  
   - However, the report does not state how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, permuted blocks), nor how the stratification was operationalised. Thus, it cannot be established that allocation was genuinely unpredictable.  
   - Allocation concealment is also insufficiently described. The authors state that participants, research assistants and technicians were blinded, but do not explain how the randomization list was secured, who prepared or labelled treatment packages, or whether the person enrolling participants could access the forthcoming allocation. Blinding after allocation is not, by itself, evidence of adequate allocation concealment.  
   - Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable, although skin colour was imbalanced (non-white: 59.4% fish-oil versus 82.1% placebo), which is compatible with chance in such a small trial but does not resolve the uncertainty about sequence generation and concealment.

2. **Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**  
   - The intended comparison was reasonably well designed: fish-oil and soybean-oil capsules were reportedly identical in colour, shape, size and packaging, and were deodorized. Participants and study personnel were stated to be blinded. These measures should have reduced differential co-interventions and differential behavioural changes attributable to knowledge of allocation.  
   - There is no evidence that participants systematically received additional omega-3 supplementation or that care differed deliberately between groups. Participants were asked not to alter diet or take other supplements.  
   - Nevertheless, adherence was incomplete: among those receiving treatment, mean capsule consumption was approximately 74% in both groups. Moreover, 12 women discontinued the intervention after trial commencement, and a further three had important post-randomization events affecting treatment exposure (lost capsules, pre-eclampsia, stillbirth).  
   - Eleven randomized women never received the allocated intervention, including one who declined participation after randomization. This is a substantial departure from the intended intervention in a trial of only 60 women.  
   - The authors claim to undertake intention-to-treat analyses, but do not clearly explain how women who never started treatment, discontinued treatment, or had no outcome assessment were incorporated into the binary EPDS ≥11 analyses. Consequently, it is not possible to verify that the effect of assignment, rather than an incompletely implemented intervention among completers, has been estimated without bias.

3. **Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high risk**  
   - Missing outcome data are extensive. Of 60 randomized women, only 32 completed the trial to the postpartum visit (15 fish-oil and 17 placebo participants)—approximately 47% of the randomized sample did not complete follow-up.  
   - Losses occurred both before treatment initiation and during follow-up. These included transfers of prenatal care, missed visits, discontinuation of intervention, pregnancy losses/stillbirths, pre-eclampsia and loss of capsules. Some reasons may be unrelated to depressive symptoms, but the report provides insufficient information to establish that missing postpartum EPDS data were unrelated to both outcome and intervention assignment.  
   - The primary outcome is prevalence of EPDS ≥11 at postpartum, yet postpartum outcome data appear to have been observed only in the 32 completers. Table 3 nevertheless labels the analysis “intention-to-treat” and retains denominators of 32 and 28, without reporting the number with an observed EPDS assessment at each time point or the method used to handle missing data.  
   - No multiple imputation, inverse-probability weighting, best/worst-case sensitivity analysis, or other analysis addressing missing binary outcome data is described. Simply describing an analysis as ITT does not make it so when nearly half the relevant endpoint data are unavailable.  
   - Given the magnitude of missingness, the small sample, and absence of an appropriate missing-data strategy or sensitivity analyses, the reported prevalence comparison could be materially biased.

4. **Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — prevalence of depressive symptoms (EPDS score ≥11): low risk**  
   - The endpoint was determined using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), a standardized participant-completed instrument that had been validated in Portuguese/Brazilian mothers. The threshold of EPDS ≥11 was specified as the cutoff for moderate-to-severe postpartum depressive symptoms.  
   - This is a subjective, participant-reported outcome. Such outcomes are particularly vulnerable to bias if participants know which intervention they received or if trial personnel influence questionnaire completion.  
   - In this trial, fish-oil and placebo capsules were described as identical and deodorized, and participants, research assistants and technicians were reported to be blinded to allocation. Thus, knowledge of intervention status was unlikely to differentially influence EPDS responses or administration of the questionnaire.  
   - The report does not present a formal assessment of successful blinding. Fish oil can sometimes have recognizable effects, but the capsules were deodorized and adverse effects were similarly reported in both groups; there is no direct evidence that unblinding occurred differentially.  
   - Although EPDS is a screening measure rather than a diagnostic interview, this concerns the clinical interpretation and validity of the construct, rather than differential outcome measurement between randomized groups. For the specified EPDS ≥11 endpoint, measurement bias is therefore judged low risk.

5. **Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**  
   - The trial was registered retrospectively, on 24 May 2012, after recruitment had begun in November 2009 and apparently near or after completion of follow-up in July 2012. Retrospective registration cannot reliably demonstrate that outcomes, time points and analyses were specified before knowledge of trial results.  
   - More importantly, the registration record supplied is for an observational prospective cohort of 299 women, not for the nested randomized fish-oil trial of 60 women. It lists “major depressive episode” as the primary outcome, not prevalence of EPDS score ≥11, and contains no randomized arms, intervention details, EPDS threshold, postpartum time point, or statistical analysis plan for the RCT.  
   - The manuscript identifies prevalence of EPDS ≥11 as the primary outcome, but examines this at several time points, using unadjusted comparisons and longitudinal random-intercept logistic models, and also presents ITT, per-protocol and history-of-depression subgroup analyses. There is no prospectively dated RCT protocol or statistical analysis plan allowing verification that these analyses and subgroup assessments were pre-specified.  
   - The main EPDS ≥11 findings are reported at the available time points, which limits concern that the primary binary outcome was wholly suppressed. Nevertheless, the retrospective and mismatched registration leaves substantial uncertainty about selective choice among outcome definitions, time points, populations and analytic approaches.

6. **Overall rating: high risk of bias**  
   - The overall judgement is **high risk of bias**, principally because outcome data relevant to the postpartum prevalence of EPDS ≥11 are missing for nearly half of randomized participants, with no adequately described method to address this missingness and no sensitivity analyses.  
   - Additional concerns arise from incomplete reporting of random-sequence generation/allocation concealment, incomplete treatment exposure after randomization, and the absence of prospectively registered RCT outcomes and analyses.
