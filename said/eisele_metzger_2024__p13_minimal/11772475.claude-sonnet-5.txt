# Risk of Bias Assessment: Treatment Retention Outcome

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Some concerns.** Patients were stratified on two variables (self-reported drug/alcohol use in past 30 days; negative urine screens for ≥2 years) prior to randomization, but the actual method of random sequence generation is not described (no mention of computer-generated sequences, random number tables, etc.). There is no description of allocation concealment procedures — it is unclear whether the person enrolling patients could foresee assignment. Given the study design (open-label, three arms including routine care vs. two MMM variants), blinding of participants/investigators to allocation was impossible by nature of the intervention, but this is more relevant to domain 4. For retention specifically, baseline characteristics between groups are not formally compared/tested (only pooled descriptive stats for n=73 are given), raising some concern about whether randomization achieved balance. The differential dropout immediately after randomization (4/25 in routine vs 1/26 in office-based, 0 in clinic-based) before "study start" is notable and undisclosed in terms of reasons tied to arm assignment, suggesting possible post-randomization exclusion bias.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns.** This is an open-label trial by necessity (patients and staff necessarily aware of visit frequency/location), so deviations are plausible and cannot be fully controlled via blinding. The stepped-care protocol allowed patients to move between intensity levels within their assigned condition, and office-based patients returned to the clinic during intensified care — this is a per-protocol design feature, not a deviation, but it does mean the "MMM" experience was diluted for a subset (29% of patients, 21/73) who required intensified care. Analysis was stated to be intent-to-treat for retention (n=73), which is a strength. However, 5 patients withdrew "upon assignment to study condition" before receiving any intervention and were excluded from the n=73 analytic sample — this is a deviation from strict ITT principles (they should ideally be included in retention analysis as randomized). This exclusion, occurring asymmetrically (4 from routine, 1 from office-based, 0 from clinic-based), could bias the retention comparison.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Low risk.** Retention data (the outcome itself) is inherently completely captured — whether a patient remained in or left treatment is documented for all 73 randomized (analyzed) patients. Reasons for the 7 dropouts are transparently reported in detail. Survival analysis (Wilcoxon-Gehan) was used appropriately for time-to-event retention data, accommodating differential follow-up. This is a robust approach with no important missingness on this specific outcome for the analyzed sample.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: Treatment Retention
**Low risk.** Retention (remaining in/completing 6-months of randomized treatment) is an objective, administratively recorded outcome (presence/absence in treatment), not a subjective patient-reported measure like pain or satisfaction. Measurement does not depend on patient blinding or self-report interpretation — it is recorded by clinic staff based on attendance/discharge status. Outcome assessors were not independent/blinded, but given the objective nature of the outcome (in treatment vs. not), this is unlikely to introduce meaningful bias. This differs from the highly subjective CSQ satisfaction outcome, which would carry much higher risk given lack of blinding.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**Some concerns.** No trial registration or protocol is referenced/available in the manuscript to verify pre-specification of retention as a primary or secondary outcome, or to confirm that the analysis method (Wilcoxon-Gehan survival analysis) was pre-specified rather than chosen post hoc among possible methods. The Methods section does describe intent to compare retention via survival analysis, and results are reported in a manner consistent with that plan, with no evidence of selective reporting for this specific outcome (result is reported as null, not selectively suppressed). However, absence of a registered protocol prevents full verification, and the paper is explicitly described as an interim analysis of "the first 6 months of an ongoing 12-month trial" — raising typical concerns about interim/timing selection of the reported analysis window.

## 6. Overall rating
**Some concerns**

**Justification:** While the retention outcome itself is objectively measured (low risk for domain 4) and completely accounted for (low risk for domain 3), there are legitimate concerns regarding the randomization/allocation process (unclear allocation concealment, asymmetric pre-intervention dropout across arms), potential ITT violations from excluding 5 post-randomization withdrawals, and the inability to verify pre-specification of outcome/analysis via a registered protocol. These concerns, particularly the unclear randomization procedures and analytic exclusions, are sufficient to lower confidence below "low risk" but do not rise to the level of "high risk" for this specific objective outcome, as the direction and magnitude of any resulting bias is unlikely to be substantial given the relatively balanced numbers and transparent reporting of dropout reasons.
