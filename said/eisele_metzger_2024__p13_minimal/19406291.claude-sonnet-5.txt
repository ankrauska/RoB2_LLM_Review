# Risk of Bias Assessment: HAM-D (Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression) Score

## 1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process
**Low risk.** Randomization used a block design (blocks of 8) with computer-generated random number sequences maintained in individually presealed envelopes, and allocation was implemented by the pharmacy — independent of study staff and physicians who interacted with participants. Both study staff and participants were blinded to allocation. Baseline HAM-D scores were similar between groups (27.5±7.1 vs 25.2±8.0, per Table 2), suggesting the randomization achieved reasonable balance on the outcome of interest. While income was significantly unbalanced between arms, this is plausible under true randomization with a modest sample size (n=52) and is not evidence of a compromised randomization/allocation-concealment process.

## 2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions
**Some concerns.** The trial was double-blind with identical pill markings, and adherence was monitored via diaries. However, sertraline carries a distinct side-effect profile relative to placebo (e.g., GI effects, one participant withdrew specifically due to side effects), creating a realistic potential for unblinding of participants and/or treating physicians during the dose-titration visits (physicians adjusted dosage based partly on side-effect tolerance). No formal check of blinding success (e.g., asking participants/raters to guess allocation) is reported. This differential experience between arms could have influenced participant expectancy and engagement with the intervention, constituting a deviation from the intended "blinded" comparison that was not assessed.

## 3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data
**Some concerns.** 11/52 randomized participants (21%) did not complete the trial (5 sertraline, 6 placebo — fairly balanced numerically). Critically, the authors report that dropouts had significantly higher (worse) HAM-D scores than completers at their last assessment (F=4.0, P=.052), indicating that missingness was related to the outcome itself (informative/differential dropout by outcome value, not just by arm). Although the authors used intent-to-treat analysis with last-observation-carried-forward (LOCF), LOCF is now recognized as an imputation method prone to bias, particularly when dropout is related to worsening symptoms, as appears to be the case here. This creates a plausible risk that the true between-group HAM-D difference could be under- or over-estimated.

## 4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome — HAM-D
**Some concerns.** HAM-D is a semi-structured, clinician-administered interview but relies substantially on participant self-report of symptoms during the interview, making it a quasi-subjective measure. Raters were reported to be "independent from the physician" and blinded, and different staff conducted baseline versus post-treatment assessments, which is a design strength. However: (a) no formal assessment of masking integrity was performed/reported, and (b) the pharmacological side-effect profile of sertraline differs from placebo, creating a realistic channel for inadvertent unblinding of raters or participants (e.g., a participant volunteering side effects during the semi-structured interview). Given the outcome is not purely objective and blinding success was not verified, residual detection bias cannot be excluded.

## 5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result
**High risk.** The registered primary outcome (per ClinicalTrials.gov) was the HAM-D total score at baseline and end of treatment, consistent with the published primary analysis (total HAM-D, ANCOVA/random-effects, non-significant group effect). However, the manuscript subsequently reports post hoc analyses of four alternative HAM-D "unidimensional" subscales (Beck Melancholia, Maier & Philipp, Gibbons, and "5 most sensitive"/HamFive) that were not pre-specified in the registered protocol or the power analysis. Two of these four (HamG and HamFive) reached statistical significance favoring sertraline and are highlighted in the discussion as evidence the drug may be more effective than the primary (null) analysis suggests. This selective emphasis on a subset of exploratory subscale results that happened to reach significance — while the pre-registered primary total HAM-D outcome was null — represents a substantial risk of outcome-reporting/analysis selection bias.

## 6. Overall rating
**High risk of bias.**

**Justification:** While randomization and allocation concealment appear robust (low risk), there are concerns across multiple other domains — potential unblinding due to differential side-effect profiles (deviations and measurement domains), informative missing data related to the outcome itself, and clear evidence of selective reporting/emphasis of non-prespecified subscale analyses that yielded significant findings after the primary prespecified HAM-D analysis was null. This last issue in particular — post hoc "data-dredging" across four alternative HAM-D scoring schemes with selective highlighting of favorable results — substantially undermines confidence in the reported and interpreted treatment effect on the HAM-D outcome, warranting an overall "high risk" rating rather than merely "some concerns."
