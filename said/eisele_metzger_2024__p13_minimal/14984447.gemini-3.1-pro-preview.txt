Based on the provided manuscript and criteria, here is my evaluation of the risk of bias for the endpoint of SBGM frequency and HbA1c.

**1. Risk of bias arising from the randomization process: some concerns**
The manuscript states that patients were "randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion to two groups of 31 patients each." However, there is no description of the method used to generate the random allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, coin toss) or whether allocation concealment was implemented (e.g., using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes). While baseline characteristics appear relatively balanced, the lack of information regarding allocation concealment raises some concerns that the randomization process could have been subverted. 

**2. Risk of bias due to deviations from the intended interventions: some concerns**
Due to the nature of the intervention (providing free test strips versus requiring patients to purchase them), it was impossible to blind the patients or the care providers to the group assignment. Although the study attempted to keep patients "blind" to the primary objective (to see if cost affects SBGM frequency), they were fully aware of whether they were paying for their strips. This lack of blinding to the intervention could lead to behavioral changes (e.g., patients in the control group intentionally dropping out or changing their testing habits out of frustration). Therefore, there are some concerns regarding deviations from the intended intervention.

**3. Risk of bias due to missing outcome data: high-risk**
There is a severe imbalance and a very high rate of missing data due to dropouts. The authors state, "At the end of the study, there were 25 patients remaining in the NC group and 16 patients in the C group, i.e. a dropout rate of 19% in the NC group and 48% in the C group." A 48% dropout rate in the control group compared to 19% in the intervention group is extremely problematic. The missingness is highly likely to be related to the true outcome, as patients who found the strips too expensive or inconvenient (and thus likely had worse HbA1c or lower SBGM frequency) were the most likely to drop out. The use of Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) for the intention-to-treat analysis does not adequately address this massive, differential attrition.

**4. Risk of bias in measurement of the outcome: low-risk**
Both outcomes are highly objective. SBGM frequency was determined by directly downloading the glucometer's memory, which is objective and not subject to patient self-report bias. HbA1c was measured objectively using a standard clinical analyzer (DCA 2000 Analyzer). Assessors evaluating these specific outcomes (machine readouts and lab values) cannot easily bias the measurement, making the risk of bias in the measurement of the outcome low.

**5. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result: some concerns**
There is no mention of a pre-published protocol or trial registration (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) available to compare the pre-specified analysis plan with the reported results. While the reported outcomes (SBGM frequency and HbA1c) directly match the stated aims and methods, the lack of a registered protocol means we cannot be completely certain that other measured variables or time points were not selectively omitted. 

**6. Overall rating: high-risk**
The trial is judged to be at high risk of bias overall. This is primarily driven by the "high risk" rating in the domain of missing outcome data, where a massive and highly differential dropout rate (48% vs 19%) fundamentally undermines the reliability of the study's conclusions. The lack of clear allocation concealment and the unblinded nature of the intervention further compound these issues.
