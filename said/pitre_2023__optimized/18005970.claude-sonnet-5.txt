# RoB 2.0 Assessment

**Outcome assessed:** Number of mistakes made in diagnosing problems (diagnostic hypotheses) identified by students in Phases 1 and 2
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise because, although randomization with a randomization table was used, allocation concealment is not described, and (more importantly) there are substantial baseline imbalances between the Trento and Udine cohorts that raise concerns about the comparability of groups, particularly for the inter-trial comparison that generates the key OR of 3.75.

**b. Rationale:** The paper states: "In each campus, the students enrolled in the research project were consequently randomised with a randomisation table into two different groups." This confirms a random sequence generation method (Y to signalling question 1.1), but there is no description of allocation concealment procedures (No information to 1.2). Critically, Table 2 shows marked baseline differences between the two campuses: previous work experience duration (1.3 vs 2.3 years, p<0.05), voluntary work experience (17.1% vs 45.2%, p<0.01), and type/duration of clinical training programmed (p<0.01 for several variables). The authors themselves acknowledge: "The two groups were similar in principal demographic characteristics but not in the amount of previous work experience or in experience with voluntary work... The types of clinical training programmed and done during the study in the two campuses were also different." Because the primary result being assessed (OR 3.75) is an *inter-trial* (between-campus) comparison rather than a purely within-campus randomized comparison, the "randomization" only applies to the intervention vs. control split *within* each campus, not between campuses. This introduces potential confounding for the between-campus comparison that is central to the trial's main conclusion. However, within each campus, randomization appears to have been done appropriately with no evidence of problems in the sequence generation. This leads to "Some concerns" rather than "High risk," since the within-site randomization process itself appears sound, but the overall interpretation of the primary comparison partly relies on non-randomized (between-site) contrasts.

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Participants and tutors could not be blinded to intervention (type of tutoring/laboratory), and there is no explicit evidence that non-protocol deviations arose from the trial context; however, an appropriate intention-to-treat-type analysis appears to have been used with all randomized participants accounted for in the data presented for Phases 1 and 2.

**b. Rationale:** By nature of this educational intervention, participants and personnel (tutors) could not be blinded to intervention assignment (laboratory/intensive tutoring vs. routine/weekly tutoring) — this is inherent to educational trials of this kind. The paper does not report any switching between arms or additional non-protocol interventions being provided differentially: "During the study, none of the teaching practices changed in either of the degree courses." This suggests deviations arising from trial context were minimal or not documented. There is no indication that carers/tutors deviated from protocol in ways likely to bias the outcome — tutors met to standardize methods ("All the tutors involved in the study met to ensure that the methods of tutoring and the list of debriefing questions were the same for each group," per Table 1). The analysis appears to include all randomized participants (144 students, with the flow described in Figure 1), consistent with an ITT-type approach; no explicit exclusions post-randomization for eligible participants are described. Given the unavoidable lack of blinding of participants/personnel in an educational context, but no evidence of trial-context-driven deviations affecting the outcome and an appropriate analysis of all participants, this domain is judged "Some concerns" (per algorithm: awareness of assignment = Yes, but no evidence of important unbalanced deviations = "Some concerns" default).

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Outcome data appear to be available for the majority of participants (144/179 eligible), but a meaningful proportion of enrolled/eligible students were excluded pre-analysis, and it is unclear whether missingness in Phase 2 outcome data (e.g., due to withdrawal from clinical placements) is related to the true value of the outcome.

**b. Rationale:** The report states, "From the 179 students enrolled in the first academic year 2003–2004 of the Nursing Science degree courses in both Trento and Udine; 144 subjects were eligible for the study (80.4%)." This 19.6% non-inclusion is explained primarily by eligibility criteria (e.g., language competency: "Many of the students were not included because of their difficulty with the language") rather than being a post-randomization dropout — this affects eligibility rather than missingness in outcome per se, so may not represent a major risk. However, the flow of participants through Phases 0, 1, and 2 is not fully transparent regarding whether all 144 randomized students completed all three phases with data recorded, and whether any students were lost between phases (e.g., due to failure to attend the case-study sessions in Phase 1 or 2). The tables report data for the fully expected numbers in each subgroup (e.g., Phase 2: 41+41+23+39 = 144), suggesting no additional loss occurred after eligibility was established, which would support a "Low risk" judgement. However, absence of an explicit statement confirming 100% outcome data completion for Phases 1 and 2 (e.g., no CONSORT-style accounting of missing case-study responses) introduces uncertainty. Given the data appear complete for the analyzed sample but this is not explicitly confirmed, this is judged as "Some concerns."

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome (number of diagnostic errors) required judgement-based assessment by researchers comparing student responses against a "standard," and although blind evaluation was used to reduce bias, the outcome assessors could not be blinded to which case-study phase (and implicitly could infer intervention group in some analyses), and the same simulated case was reused across phases, raising both measurement validity and potential differential assessment concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The method of measurement involved trained comparison against a standard: "Two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students and these were compared to the 'standard'... A definition of the standard was agreed upon by professionals, using the K of Cohen and taking a cut-off as K>0.80." This supports blinding of outcome assessors regarding the *content* of student responses, and the paper states, "The data were processed blind; only after this had been done were the identities of the Schools (Trento and Udine) revealed," suggesting successful blinding of assessors to group allocation during data processing. This is a strength. However, the outcome (counting reasoning "errors") inherently involves judgement, and the reuse of the same clinical scenario ("Valentina's case") across all three phases is acknowledged by the authors as a limitation: "Using the same clinical scenario three times... allowed students to get results on their own and could have helped students remember what they had learned previously. This might have threatened the internal validity of the study." This is a non-differential measurement concern (affecting all groups), but combined with the subjective judgement involved in classifying "wrong" hypotheses, and no explicit confirmation that assessors were blinded to the *phase* or timing of each case, some risk remains. Given assessors were blinded to group/school identity during scoring, but the measurement method itself has an acknowledged validity threat (repeated case), this is judged "Some concerns" rather than "Low."

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** No trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is available for review, so it cannot be confirmed whether the reported result (number of diagnostic errors, dichotomized at the median for OR calculation) was selected, on the basis of results, from multiple possible outcome definitions or analyses.

**b. Rationale:** The trial registration document (doc_id: 4dab5cab-2b44-42f8-8559-2dd9073b18b5) referenced in the prompt was not available in the material provided to review for pre-specified outcomes/analyses; the published article itself does not reference a trial registry number or a published protocol/SAP. The outcome was dichotomized using "median values of error in Phases 1 and 2... regarded as reference 'cut-off' points" — an analytic choice made using the study's own observed data (post hoc median splits), which raises the possibility that cut-points were chosen based on the resulting distribution rather than pre-specified. The article also reports both continuous ("average number of errors") and dichotomized (median-split OR) outcomes, multiple statistical models (univariate and multivariate), and multiple comparisons (intra-trial and inter-trial), without clear indication that all these were pre-specified in this manner. Without documentation of a prospectively registered protocol or SAP finalized before unblinded data were available, it is not possible to rule out selective reporting of the specific analysis (e.g., choice of median cut-off, choice to emphasize the inter-trial OR of 3.75 as the headline result). This leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" (No information on analysis plan, per algorithm default).

## Overall Risk of Bias: **High risk of bias**

Given "Some concerns" are present in **multiple domains** (randomization, deviations from intended intervention, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selective reporting), and considering that the concerns in the randomization domain are compounded by known, substantial baseline imbalances between the two comparison groups underlying the key reported effect estimate (inter-trial OR 3.75), the cumulative effect of "Some concerns" across nearly all domains substantially lowers confidence in the result. Per RoB 2 guidance: *"'Some concerns' in multiple domains may lead the review authors to decide on an overall judgement of 'High' risk of bias for that outcome."* This is applied here because the confounding baseline differences between the two educational settings (which underpin the primary reported effect), combined with unclear pre-specification of the analysis and judgement-based outcome assessment, substantially undermine confidence in the causal interpretation of the reported result.
