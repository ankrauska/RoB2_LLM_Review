**Outcome assessed:** HAM-D (Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression) score at end of treatment (approximately 10 weeks), comparing sertraline with placebo. The assessment concerns the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** The randomization sequence appears genuinely random and allocation was implemented by the pharmacy, independently of study staff. Although baseline characteristics are reported only for the 41 completers rather than all 52 randomized participants, there is no compelling evidence that observed differences are incompatible with chance or reflect a failure of randomization.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The article states that treatment allocation was “balanced by using a block design (blocks of 8) with **computer-generated random number sequences** in individual presealed envelopes.” This is an adequate random sequence-generation method. It also states: “Once medically cleared, all participants were randomized into the medication or placebo arm of the study **by the pharmacy**,” supporting concealment from recruiting and study personnel.  
   
   The groups had 27 and 25 participants randomized, respectively, according to the registry, reasonably consistent with the intended allocation. Baseline data among completers show some numerical differences (e.g., mild TBI: 20% in sertraline versus 50% in placebo), but the report states that there were “no significant differences” between groups on injury severity, demographic characteristics, or baseline depression. These differences alone do not establish a problem with randomization, particularly in this small trial. The absence of baseline data for all randomized participants is a reporting limitation, but does not provide evidence of a failed randomization process.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Participants and study personnel were blinded, matching placebo procedures were used, and the analysis retained participants in their assigned groups. There is no evidence of deviations arising from the trial context that would bias the effect of assignment to sertraline versus placebo.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** This was described as a “double-blind randomized controlled trial.” The pharmacy “implemented masking and random assignment,” and medication/placebo were supplied with “**identical markings**.” The article further states that “**both study staff and participants were blinded** to the type of intervention received.”  
   
   Dose adjustments were made using the same scheduled physician visits and procedures in both arms. The article also attempted to standardize nonspecific trial contact: “research staff followed a standard protocol, and the duration of each contact was kept constant.” No important unequal co-interventions or protocol-implementation failures attributable to knowledge of assignment were reported.  
   
   For the effect of assignment, the authors state that “**an intent-to-treat analysis compared the outcomes of all study participants, including participants who received minimal treatment**.” Participants appear to have been analysed according to randomized assignment; use of last-observation-carried-forward and incomplete outcome observations is considered under missing outcome data rather than in this domain.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**  
   e. **Judgement:** Outcome data at the treatment endpoint were unavailable for a substantial proportion of randomized participants (11/52; 21%). Missingness was plausibly related to depression severity and clinical status, and no robust sensitivity analysis was reported to show that the HAM-D result was unaffected.  
   
   f. **Rationale:** Of 52 randomized participants, only 41 completed the clinical trial. The registry reports 27 randomized to sertraline and 25 to placebo, with 22 and 19 completing, respectively; thus, 5/27 (19%) and 6/25 (24%) did not complete. The article similarly reports: “Of the 11 who did not complete the study, 3 were removed for clinical reasons,” including increased anxiety, increased depression following a family death, and an allergic reaction. The remaining participants were noncompliant with protocol requirements, including missed follow-up appointments and not collecting medication.  
   
   Importantly, the authors report that “**the dropouts [tended] to have higher HAM-D scores**” at their final assessment than those who remained in treatment (F=4.0, P=.052). This is evidence that missingness may have depended on the underlying outcome value—depression severity—and therefore may bias the estimated between-group HAM-D effect.  
   
   Although dropout proportions were broadly similar between groups, the amount of missing endpoint data is substantial for a small study, several withdrawals were clinically related, and the handling of missing data was not sufficient to exclude bias. The authors used last observation carried forward for certain participants and random-effects models that tolerate unequal numbers of measurements, but did not present sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-not-at-random assumptions. Therefore, the result is at high risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** HAM-D is an appropriate, clinician-rated instrument for depression severity, and outcome assessment was conducted under masking procedures. There is no indication that HAM-D measurement differed systematically between trial arms.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The 17-item HAM-D was the stated primary outcome and is described as a “widely used clinician-rated measurement of depression severity.” It was assessed at baseline, every two weeks, and at the end of treatment using comparable procedures in both groups.  
   
   Outcome ratings were performed by “a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist” independently from the study physician. Since “both study staff and participants were blinded” and pharmacy procedures maintained masking with identical medication markings, the assessors were probably unaware of intervention assignment. The article also states that final assessments were administered by a different staff member from the baseline assessor, further reducing the possibility that expectations based on earlier ratings influenced endpoint assessment.  
   
   Although the HAM-D includes clinician judgement, successful blinding of outcome assessors makes differential measurement bias unlikely.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** The HAM-D outcome and end-of-treatment assessment were prospectively identified in the trial registry, and the publication reports the main total HAM-D findings. However, there was no sufficiently detailed, dated pre-specified statistical analysis plan, while several possible analyses, time points, and HAM-D-derived outcomes were available. This leaves some concern about selection of the reported result.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The registry identifies HAM-D depression at baseline and “**Depression at End of Treatment**” as primary outcomes, with the endpoint specified as “end of treatment, average of 10 weeks.” Thus, the outcome domain and principal endpoint were registered before study completion. The publication is consistent with this in identifying HAM-D as “the primary outcome measure.”  
   
   However, the registry does not specify the statistical analysis approach, whether final values or change scores would be primary, how repeated biweekly measurements would be analysed, how missing data would be addressed, or which HAM-D-derived result would be prioritized. The publication reports several approaches: intent-to-treat analyses, last-observation-carried-forward analyses, random-effects repeated-measures models, baseline-to-endpoint repeated-measures analyses, a responder analysis, and analyses of four alternative HAM-D subscales. The authors note that two of the subscale analyses were statistically significant whereas the total HAM-D group effect was not.  
   
   There is no clear evidence that the specific total HAM-D result was selected because of its direction or statistical significance; therefore, high risk is not warranted. Nevertheless, in the absence of a detailed pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available, the possibility of selective choice among multiple eligible outcome analyses cannot be excluded.
