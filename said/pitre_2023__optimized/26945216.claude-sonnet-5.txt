# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Pain Intensity Outcome
**Trial: TENS vs IFT vs Standard Care for Myofascial Pain Syndrome (Upper Trapezius)**

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: "computerized block randomization" was used — "Y" to random sequence (1.1).
- Allocation concealment: "allocation group concealed in envelops" — no detail on whether envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, or opened sequentially. This is insufficient detail to confirm adequate concealment, so response to 1.2 is **No information/Probably no**.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows no significant imbalance in age, sex, or baseline pain intensity (PI1: F=2.96, P=0.06) or baseline ROM measures (all P>0.05). However, there was a statistically significant difference in **affected side** (right vs left) between groups (χ²=5.952, P=0.015), which is a somewhat unusual imbalance to occur by chance with proper randomization, though it is not established as a key prognostic factor for pain intensity outcome and does not reach the very low P-value threshold (e.g., <0.001) that would suggest a randomization failure.
- Given lack of detail on allocation concealment procedures, combined with an unexplained (though likely chance) imbalance in a baseline characteristic, the domain is judged as **Some concerns** rather than Low risk, per the algorithm (allocation concealment unclear/not clearly adequate → some concerns).

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- **2.1/2.2 – Awareness of assigned intervention:** This was a "single-blind" trial — "The participants were blind to the allocation to treatment groups after randomization." However, participants would clearly be aware of whether they were receiving TENS (a device producing "a strong but nonpainful TENS sensation") versus IFT versus no electrical modality (control) — these are physically distinguishable interventions with different sensory experiences. Thus, in practice, participants were **very likely aware** of which arm they were in despite being nominally "blinded." Critically, **"The principal investigator acted as both outcome assessor, taking all study measurements, and as therapist administering all study treatment."** This means personnel (and the outcome assessor) were **definitely aware** of intervention assignment (2.2 = Yes).
- **2.3 – Deviations arising from trial context:** Because the same unblinded individual delivered all treatments and personally administered/marked myofascial trigger points, differences in enthusiasm, verbal encouragement, technique consistency, or expectation effects could differ systematically by group (trial context effects). There is no evidence of standardized scripting to prevent differential encouragement or performance across the three arms.
- **2.4/2.5 – Likely to affect outcome, unbalanced between groups:** Pain intensity is a subjective, patient-reported outcome highly susceptible to expectation and performance bias. Given the physiotherapist/assessor's lack of blinding and no placebo device used for the control group ("not using a placebo for the patients of the control group could be considered as limitations of the present study" — explicitly acknowledged by the authors), it is likely that unblinded delivery influenced patient-reported pain differentially across groups (e.g., via reassurance, attention, or differing verbal/nonverbal cues for the "active treatment" arms compared to control).
- **2.6 – Appropriate analysis:** All 105 randomized participants completed the study and were analyzed ("All completed the study and were entered into the analysis"), consistent with ITT principles for the assignment-based analysis.
- Given the strong likelihood that lack of blinding of personnel/assessor, combined with lack of a placebo/sham control, led to deviations affecting the subjective pain outcome differentially between groups, this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- "There were 105 individuals who expressed interest in participating in the study. **All completed the study and were entered into the analysis** (Fig. 3)."
- The CONSORT flow chart confirms n=105 randomized, n=35 in each arm, and "post-treatment measurements (n=105)" — indicating complete outcome data for all randomized participants at all time points (baseline, immediately after first treatment, before eighth treatment, and 1 week after eighth treatment).
- With no missing outcome data at all, risk of bias due to missing outcome data is **Low**.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias

**b. Rationale:**
- Pain intensity was measured using a 100-mm visual analog scale (VAS), a **participant-reported outcome**. For participant-reported outcomes, the outcome assessor is effectively the participant, and knowledge of intervention assignment can plausibly influence reporting.
- Crucially: **"The principal investigator acted as both outcome assessor, taking all study measurements, and as therapist administering all study treatment."** This means the person recording pain intensity scores was the same person who delivered the (unblinded) treatment and knew group assignment — introducing potential for the assessor to consciously or unconsciously influence how VAS scores were elicited or recorded (e.g., through cueing, body language, or verbal interaction during measurement).
- Additionally, participants themselves were aware of which modality (or no modality) they received (distinguishable sensory experiences of TENS vs IFT vs none), and no sham/placebo device was used for the control group, so participants' self-reported pain ratings could plausibly be influenced by expectation, perceived novelty of "active" electrotherapy, or demand characteristics.
- Given both the participant (self-report) and the sole assessor/therapist were unblinded, and pain intensity is a subjective outcome highly susceptible to such influence, and the authors themselves acknowledge this as a limitation ("not using a placebo for the patients of the control group could be considered as limitations of the present study"), this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial was prospectively registered ("the study was registered as a clinical trial with the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry"), which is reassuring, but the article does not provide the registry number or a detailed comparison between the pre-specified statistical analysis plan and the reported analyses within the text provided.
- Pain intensity was measured at four pre-specified time points (baseline, immediately after first treatment, before eighth treatment, 1 week after eighth treatment), and results for **all four time points are fully reported** in Table 2, with consistent methodology (VAS 0-100mm) throughout — this is reassuring evidence against selective outcome measurement or selective time-point reporting.
- However, without access to the registry entry or a published protocol/statistical analysis plan to confirm that the analysis approach (one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc comparisons) was pre-specified before unblinded data were available, it is **not possible to fully verify** the absence of selective analytical reporting (e.g., whether alternative statistical approaches were tried, or whether the choice to present change-from-baseline scores in this manner was pre-planned).
- Given the lack of accessible registration details/protocol for full verification despite no overt evidence of selective reporting, this is judged **Some concerns** rather than Low risk.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias for Pain Intensity Outcome: **High risk of bias**

*(Driven by High risk in both "Deviations from intended interventions" and "Measurement of the outcome" domains, compounded by "Some concerns" in randomization and selective reporting domains.)*
