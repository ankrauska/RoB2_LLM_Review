# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Length of Hospital Stay

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: "Procedure type was assigned by a computer-generated randomization list" — this satisfies the criterion for a random component (Answer: **Yes** to signalling question 1.1).
- Allocation concealment: "concealment of randomization was done by telephone inquiry" — this is a reasonable method of concealment, consistent with central allocation (Answer: **Yes/Probably yes** to signalling question 1.2).
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows some differences between groups that raise concern. Notably, weight (65 vs 74.5 kg), Quetelet Index (25.2 vs 26.8), and sonographically estimated uterine weight (283 vs 369 g) all trend higher in the AH group, though none are reported as statistically significant. However, of particular concern is the significant imbalance in surgeon experience: "Twenty patients (71.4%) [LAVH] were operated on by an attending physician... A total of 20 patients (41.7%) were treated by AH, eight (40%) by an attending physician... (p < 0.05)." This is a *post-randomization* imbalance (surgeon assignment), not strictly a baseline patient characteristic, but it does raise the question of whether randomization/allocation processes (e.g., non-blinded assignment influencing surgeon selection) were compromised, given the authors themselves note: "it should be kept in mind that the surgeons performing LAVH were significantly more experienced, which could partly explain the identical duration in both groups."
- While the *patient* baseline characteristics (age, gravidity, parity) appear balanced and compatible with chance, the significant difference in operating surgeon experience between groups is unusual for a properly randomized allocation and could suggest a process issue (e.g., lack of full concealment leading to differential surgeon assignment, or a systematic difference in how cases were allocated to trainees/attendings between arms). This imbalance is a potential indication of a problem with the randomization/allocation process rather than mere chance, and could confound the estimate of the length-of-stay effect.

Given the adequate description of sequence generation and concealment, but the unresolved and statistically significant imbalance in surgeon seniority between groups (a factor plausibly related to outcomes such as length of stay via complication rates or operative proficiency), the judgement is **Some concerns** rather than Low risk.

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- Signalling Q2.1/2.2 (Were participants/personnel aware of assigned intervention?): **Yes**. Blinding was impossible given the fundamentally different surgical approaches (laparoscopic-assisted vaginal vs. open abdominal). The authors explicitly acknowledge: "It also has to be kept in mind that the patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment; thus, their expectations may have influenced the duration of their recuperation."
- Signalling Q2.3 (Deviations arising from trial context): For the outcome of length of hospital stay specifically, the paper states: "Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital." This is a critical, direct source of deviation from a strictly protocol-driven outcome measurement/care pathway: because the decision to discharge was subjective and made by an unblinded patient (and presumably unblinded caregivers), knowledge of intervention assignment could plausibly and directly have influenced the length-of-stay outcome — patients or clinicians expecting shorter recovery post-LAVH (a "newer," less invasive technique) may have been inclined to discharge earlier, and vice versa for AH. This constitutes a deviation from intended intervention arising from the trial context (patients' and clinicians' awareness/expectations) that plausibly affects the very outcome being assessed (**Yes** to Q2.4, deviation likely to affect outcome).
- Signalling Q2.5 (balanced between groups?): No — this self-determined discharge criterion would be differentially influenced by group assignment given differing expectations/preconceptions about each procedure (surgeon experience differences also compound this), so deviations are **not balanced** between groups.
- Signalling Q2.6 (appropriate ITT-type analysis): All 48 randomized patients appear to have been analyzed in their assigned groups (28 LAVH, 20 AH), consistent with ITT principles for this outcome.

Because participants and clinical staff were unblinded, the length-of-stay outcome was determined by a subjective, non-protocolized patient/clinician decision that could plausibly have been influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment, and this deviation was not balanced between groups (further compounded by the differential surgeon seniority), this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

## 3. Missing outcome data

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- Length of hospital stay is an operationally-collected, objective administrative outcome. There is no indication that any of the 48 randomized patients (28 LAVH, 20 AH) had missing data for this outcome; hospital stay is reported for all patients in Table 2, with no dropouts mentioned in relation to this specific outcome.
- The lower response to the follow-up questionnaire (35/48, 72.9%) pertains to different outcomes (post-discharge recuperation, satisfaction) rather than length of hospital stay itself, which would have been captured for all patients during their inpatient admission regardless of questionnaire completion.
- There is no evidence that outcome data were missing for any participant with respect to hospital stay: "Outcome data were available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants" (Signalling Q3.1: **Yes**).

Therefore, this domain is judged as **Low risk of bias**.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- Signalling Q4.1 (Was the method of measurement inappropriate?): The method of counting hospital days is not inherently inappropriate. **No**.
- Signalling Q4.2 (Could measurement differ systematically between groups?): This is a critical concern. The discharge decision was explicitly not based on a standardized clinical protocol but on subjective self-assessment: "Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital." This means the operational definition of "length of stay" was inherently susceptible to differential ascertainment between groups, given different patient/clinician expectations regarding the two procedures. **Yes/Probably yes**.
- Signalling Q4.3 (Were outcome assessors aware of intervention?): The relevant "outcome assessor" for this outcome is effectively the patient (and treating clinicians who approve discharge), and they were clearly unblinded — this is unavoidable given the difference between laparoscopic and open abdominal surgery. **Yes**.
- Signalling Q4.4/4.5 (Could/likely assessment influenced by knowledge of intervention?): This outcome falls into the category of "outcomes that reflect decisions made by the intervention provider/participant" — a category explicitly identified in RoB2 guidance as one where "the assessment of outcome is usually likely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention received." Given strong prior expectations regarding LAVH's minimally invasive benefits (also reflected in patient satisfaction outcomes, where "all of the LAVH patients... would undergo the same procedure again" vs 45% of AH patients), it is likely that both patients and staff had differential expectations about recovery time associated with each procedure, plausibly influencing the discharge decision itself. This is compounded by the German reimbursement structure described ("reimbursement under the German health insurance system is done on a fee per day and not a fee per diagnosis basis"), which the authors note could have permitted longer stays without cost-related pressure to discharge earlier, but does not remove the subjective bias potential.

Given the outcome assessment method (patient self-determined discharge) was non-blinded and highly susceptible to influence by knowledge of intervention assignment, and it is deemed likely that such influence occurred, this domain is judged as **High risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Selective reporting

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- The trial registration/protocol information indicates that length of hospital stay was explicitly pre-specified as the *primary outcome measure*: "We defined the primary outcome measure as length of hospital stay. We hoped to reduce the median hospital stay, for example, at least from 9 to 7 days. For this purpose, we calculated that we would need a sample size of 20 cases per group."
- This outcome is reported clearly and completely in the results (Table 2: median 6.5 vs 10 days, p < 0.05), using what appears to be the single, pre-specified analysis (comparison of medians, presumably via a single specified statistical test as described in the Statistics section) — there is no evidence of multiple eligible outcome measurements (e.g., alternative definitions or time-points of "hospital stay") or multiple competing analyses from which this result was selected.
- There is no indication of selective reporting for this specific outcome: it was clearly the a priori primary endpoint, sample size was calculated around it, and it is reported straightforwardly and prominently in the results, consistent with pre-specified intent.

Therefore, this domain is judged as **Low risk of bias**.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias Judgement for Length of Hospital Stay: **High risk of bias**

**Overall Rationale:** Per RoB 2.0 criteria, a "High" risk-of-bias judgement in any single domain results in an overall "High" risk-of-bias judgement for the outcome. Here, both the "Deviations from intended intervention" domain and the "Measurement of the outcome" domain are judged as high risk, driven by the same fundamental issue: the primary outcome (length of hospital stay) was determined via an unblinded, subjective process ("each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged"), in a trial where blinding of participants and personnel was not feasible, and where there is a plausible, unaddressed risk that differential expectations regarding the two very different surgical approaches (further compounded by differences in surgeon experience between groups) influenced this specific outcome differentially between arms.
