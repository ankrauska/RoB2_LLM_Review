**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The allocation sequence was random, adequately concealed, and baseline characteristics were balanced between the groups. 
b. Rationale: The study states that "Randomization was performed by SRL staff at the end of each interview using a random allocation sequence programmed into CAPI administration software that allowed for complete allocation concealment up to the point of assignment." Furthermore, "The success of randomization was confirmed by the absence of statistically significant differences by study condition at baseline on all characteristics."

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Although participants were aware of their assigned intervention, there is no evidence of deviations from the intended interventions that arose because of the trial context. Furthermore, an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis was used.
b. Rationale: Due to the behavioral nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and personnel was not possible. However, the study utilized an "intent-to-treat mixed-effects random regression analysis," which is an appropriate method to estimate the effect of assignment to the intervention. There were no indications of unbalanced non-protocol interventions driven by the trial context that would substantially bias the ITT effect. 

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: While some outcome data were missing, the attrition rates were relatively low and balanced between groups, and the analysis method accounted for missing data appropriately.
b. Rationale: The study reported that "Of the 519 subjects who completed T1 assessments, 458 subjects (88.2%) completed T2 interviews, and 448 (86.3%) completed T3 interviews." There were "no statistically significant differences in follow-up rates between intervention and control conditions." The mixed-effects random regression model (MIXREG) used for analysis also inherently handles missing data appropriately assuming it is missing at random.

**4. measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The outcome is self-reported by participants who were aware of their intervention assignment, and it is likely that the assessment could have been influenced by this knowledge.
b. Rationale: The primary outcome (Brief Symptom Inventory) is a participant-reported subjective measure. Because the trial is unblinded, the participants (who act as the outcome assessors for self-reported measures) knew whether they received the WRAP intervention or were on the wait-list. As noted by the authors, there may have been an "'anticipation effect' because controls were promised an opportunity to receive WRAP at the end of the study." This lack of blinding for a subjective outcome leaves the measurement highly susceptible to bias.

**5. selective reporting: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The reported outcomes and analyses match the pre-specified analysis intentions outlined in the trial registration.
b. Rationale: The trial was prospectively registered (NCT01024569). The primary outcome reported in the manuscript (Brief Symptom Inventory) aligns exactly with the primary outcome listed in the registry, measured at the specified time points (post-intervention and 6-month follow-up). There is no evidence that the results were selected from multiple eligible measurements or analyses based on statistical significance.
