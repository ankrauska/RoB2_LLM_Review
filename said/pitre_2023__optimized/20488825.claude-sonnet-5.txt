# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment: Simvastatin + IFNb 1a Trial (Outcome: Attack Number)

**Effect of interest**: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, primarily due to lack of information about allocation concealment, despite adequate sequence generation and no evidence of baseline imbalance.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation (1.1 – Yes):** "A computer-based blocking method (four blocks) was used for the randomization." This is an adequate random component.
- **Allocation concealment (1.2 – No information):** The article states "Separate physicians were assigned to randomly allocate patients into two groups" but provides no detail on the concealment mechanism (e.g., sealed envelopes, central allocation). Given that a blocked randomization method was used with a small block size (four), and no information is provided about whether allocation was concealed from the recruiting physicians, there is a plausible risk that the last allocation within blocks could have been predicted. No information allows a judgement of concealment.
- **Baseline imbalance (1.3 – No):** Table 1 shows no significant differences between groups on any baseline characteristic (all p>0.13), including baseline EDSS (p=0.13) and Gd+ lesions (p=0.28), which are the key prognostic factors for relapse. This is compatible with successful randomization.

Applying the algorithm: sequence random (Y), concealment (NI), imbalance suggests problem (N) → **Some concerns**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, driven by lack of blinding information/evidence about trial-context deviations and use of a modified ITT/per-protocol style set of secondary analyses, though the primary analysis appears to follow ITT-like principles.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Blinding of participants and personnel (2.1/2.2 – Probably yes, blinded):** The study is described as double-blind: "assigned (in random and double-blinded fashion)"; "The patients were unaware of their group assignment and all medications were given in similar packages, every 3 months." The trial registry confirms "QUADRUPLE" masking including participants, care providers, investigators, and outcomes assessors. This supports low risk on this component.
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** No specific report of non-protocol interventions or contamination is provided. Given blinding was implemented and described as successful, deviations arising specifically due to trial context are unlikely.
- **Analysis (2.6):** The paper states: "The analyses were performed according to intention-to-treat methods, and patients who withdrew from the study were followed for the whole study period." This suggests an appropriate ITT approach was used for the primary reported result. However, the text is ambiguous: "those of our dropout cases who did use simvastatin or placebo for 6 months (nine patients) were included in the final analysis" — implying that some early dropouts (5 patients who "refused to cooperate" in the first two months, before 6 months) may have been **excluded** from the primary ITT analysis. This raises concern that the primary result may not represent a full ITT population, but rather a modified version with unclear justification for exclusion (exclusion was based on treatment duration, not simply missing data, and could be related to non-adherence).
- This ambiguity in whether all randomized patients were retained in the "main" ITT analysis, combined with no information on whether the exclusion of the 5 early dropouts was related to the trial context or outcome, leads to "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to unclear handling of missing data for the primary intention-to-treat relapse analysis, with imbalance in dropout rates between arms.

**b. Rationale:**
- Per the flow diagram, of 85 randomized (43 simvastatin, 42 placebo): in the simvastatin arm 1 did not receive intervention and 3 had premature withdrawal (3 exacerbation-related dropouts reported in text as "two attacks... two from simvastatin"); in placebo arm, 4 did not receive intervention and 6 had premature withdrawal ("four from the placebo group" had two relapses). Overall, more withdrawals/non-receipt occurred in the placebo group (10/42 ~24%) vs simvastatin (4/43 ~9%), an imbalance in missingness between arms.
- Reasons for withdrawal include experiencing "two attacks" (relapses) – i.e., missingness in the outcome (attack number) is directly related to the true value of the outcome (participants who had more relapses were removed from further follow-up as a "withdrawal criterion"). This is a clear mechanism by which missingness could depend on the true outcome value.
- The text states: "we used the data on the clinical variables for the other analyses" and "patients who withdrew from the study were followed for the whole study period," suggesting the investigators attempted to retain relapse data even after withdrawal (i.e., all relapses up to withdrawal, including the qualifying 2nd relapse, were counted). If this is accurate, the outcome (total relapse count) may have been captured for all/nearly all participants despite study discontinuation, mitigating bias.
- However, this is not entirely clear, and the description of "five patients withdrew without cooperating in the first 2 months" with unclear inclusion status in the final relapse-count analysis (as they may not have reached the 6-month threshold used for "final analysis" inclusion) creates ambiguity about completeness of outcome data.
- Given the imbalance in dropout by arm and the plausible relationship between missingness and outcome (relapse-related withdrawal), but lack of clarity confirming that this fully explains/biases the primary result, judgement is "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk, as outcome assessors (neurologists evaluating relapses) were blinded, and the definition of relapse was clearly pre-specified and did not rely on subjective judgement.

**b. Rationale:**
- Outcome assessment method was appropriate and objective: "any new or worsening neurological deficit in the absence of high body temperature that lasted for more than 24 h after 1-month stability was considered a relapse."
- "Four neurologists were asked to assess the patients' eligibility for the study and perform the follow-up examinations. They were blinded to the patients' assignment to treatment group."
- Measurement/ascertainment method for relapse was identical across both groups—clinic visits, cholesterol/liver enzyme blood tests, and MRI schedules were performed on the same schedule ("every 3 months") for both groups, and study drug packaging was identical, minimizing risk of differential detection.
- Since assessors were blinded and used a relatively objective definition (with only limited judgement, similar to "observer-reported outcomes involving some judgement" but with low potential for bias given blinding was maintained via identical packaging/procedures), the risk is low.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, as the trial registry lists "attack number" as the sole primary outcome measured over "1 year," and this matches the reported outcome, but details of pre-specified analysis methods (e.g., ITT definitions, handling of dropouts) were not available in a prospectively registered, date-stamped statistical analysis plan.

**b. Rationale:**
- The ClinicalTrials.gov registration (NCT00668343) lists the primary outcome as "attack number," timeframe "1 year," consistent with the reported outcome, and no evidence of switching among multiple relapse-related outcome measures (e.g., time-to-relapse vs. total number) — only "total relapse number" is reported as the primary result, matching registration.
- However, no protocol or statistical analysis plan (SAP) was available to confirm whether the exact analytic approach (e.g., Mann-Whitney U-test as primary test, choice of ITT population, per-protocol and LOCF as sensitivity analyses) was pre-specified before unblinding, or whether these multiple analytic approaches (ITT, per-protocol, LOCF, MANOVA) were selectively chosen/reported based on the results.
- The presence of multiple analyses of the same outcome (ITT Mann-Whitney, per-protocol Mann-Whitney, LOCF Mann-Whitney, and MANOVA), all reported with p-values ranging from 0.007 to 0.04, without a clear a priori statement designating one as primary, raises mild concern that the most favorable combination of methods might have been emphasized, though all analyses point in a consistent direction.
- Given the absence of a protocol/SAP to confirm pre-specification and rule out selective analytical reporting, but with consistency between the registered outcome and the reported result, the judgement is "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk" or "High risk."

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

Per Table 1 criteria: no domain was judged "High risk of bias," but multiple domains (randomization process, deviations from intended intervention, missing outcome data, and selective reporting) were judged as "Some concerns." Per RoB2 guidance, "'Some concerns' in multiple domains may lead the review authors to decide on an overall judgement of 'High' risk of bias," and in this case, the accumulation of concerns across four of five domains—particularly regarding unclear allocation concealment, ambiguous handling of withdrawals/missing relapse data (with an outcome-related withdrawal mechanism and imbalance between arms), and lack of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan—substantially lowers confidence in the trustworthiness of the reported relapse-rate result. Therefore, the overall risk of bias for the "attack number" outcome is judged as **Some concerns**, bordering on high risk, and should be interpreted with caution.
