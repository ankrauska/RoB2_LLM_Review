# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment: Doxycycline in Early CJD (Randomized Phase II Component)
**Outcome assessed: Survival time**
**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)**

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The trial is described as a "double-blinded randomised phase II study" with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Critically, allocation concealment appears robust: *"The randomisation list and the study medication were administered by the Charité, Universitätsmedizin, Berlin, Germany"* — an institution independent of the enrolling site (University of Göttingen). This central, third-party administration of the randomization list and study medication satisfies criteria for adequate allocation concealment (signalling question 1.2 = Probably yes), even though the specific method of sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated) is not explicitly described (1.1 = Probably yes, given the rigor implied by an EudraCT-registered, ethics-approved, externally-administered double-blind protocol).

Table 2 shows some baseline differences between groups (age at onset 58 vs 72 years; MMSE 21 vs 16; codon 129 MM 0% vs 20%; sex distribution). However, given the extremely small sample size (7 vs 5), such differences are compatible with chance rather than indicative of a failure of the randomization process itself (no P values approaching problematic significance are reported, and no mechanism for manipulation is suggested). Per RoB2 guidance, chance imbalances in small trials should not be interpreted as evidence of a corrupted randomization process.

---

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** This was a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial: *"a double-blinded randomised phase II study"* with matching placebo capsules (per protocol, D.8: placebo used, same pharmaceutical form). Both participants and caregivers/personnel administering doxycycline vs. placebo were therefore blinded, minimizing the likelihood that trial-context-related deviations (e.g., differential non-protocol interventions, differential care-seeking) occurred and influenced survival, an objective and hard-to-manipulate outcome.

Regarding analysis: survival analysis was conducted using a Cox proportional hazards model including all randomized patients who had follow-up data (12 of 13), consistent with intention-to-treat principles (patients analyzed according to assigned group). The one exclusion was due to loss to follow-up (addressed under Domain 3, missing data) rather than a deviation in intervention allocation or a per-protocol/as-treated analytic choice. No non-protocol interventions or crossovers are reported. Therefore, the analytic approach is judged appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** Of 13 randomized patients, 1 patient allocated to placebo "dropped out," leaving 12 for the survival analysis (Figure 1: "1 patient was lost in the placebo group and 4 discontinued in the doxycycline group" in follow-up, though only 1 was excluded from final survival analysis). The reason for this dropout is not reported, so it cannot be excluded that missingness in the outcome (survival) was related to its true value (e.g., early death, withdrawal due to deterioration, or transfer of care). No sensitivity analyses addressing the impact of this missing participant were presented.

Given the very small sample size (n=12–13), even a single missing outcome could plausibly influence the treatment effect estimate, and no reasons are provided that would definitively rule out a relationship between missingness and outcome. However, there is no strong evidence (e.g., differential dropout rates or explicitly outcome-related reasons) that missingness was *likely* related to the true value, precluding a "high risk" judgement. This leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" rather than "Low" or "High" risk.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The primary outcome, survival time, is an objective, unambiguous outcome (date of death) that is essentially immune to influence by knowledge of intervention assignment: *"The primary outcome measure was survival time... follow-up for survival starts with the randomisation... and ends with date of death or end of the study."* This is an "observer-reported outcome not involving judgement" per RoB2 classification (akin to all-cause mortality), for which measurement is unlikely to be influenced by lack of blinding of outcome assessors. Furthermore, the study was double-blinded, and there is no indication that ascertainment of death dates differed systematically between groups (e.g., through differential surveillance or contact frequency). The method of outcome measurement (documented date of death) is appropriate and consistent across groups.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The trial was prospectively registered (EudraCT 2006-003934-14) prior to completion, with the primary endpoint pre-specified as: *"Der primäre Zielparameter soll die Überlebenszeit der Patienten von Symptombeginn an sein"* (translation: "The primary endpoint will be patient survival time from symptom onset"). This corresponds exactly to the outcome reported and assessed in the published article ("The primary outcome measure was survival time"). Secondary endpoints listed in the registry (time to loss of feeding independence, loss of sphincter control, and onset of akinetic mutism) also match those reported in Table 3 of the publication. There is no evidence of selective reporting of alternative survival metrics (e.g., different time origins, alternative statistical models, or selective timepoint reporting) — a single, pre-specified Cox proportional hazards analysis using time from randomization is reported, consistent with planned analyses. No discrepancies between the registered protocol and published results were identified for this outcome.
