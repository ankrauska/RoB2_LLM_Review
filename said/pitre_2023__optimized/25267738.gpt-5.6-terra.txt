**Assessment scope:** This assessment concerns the randomized comparison among patients with residual vein thrombosis (RVT): continued nadroparin/LMWH for a further 6 months (group A1, *n*=119) versus discontinuation of LMWH (group A2, *n*=123), for recurrent thrombotic events. Group B was not randomized and is not part of this RoB 2 randomized comparison. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment at baseline**.

1. **bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias. Although the groups appeared broadly similar at baseline, the reported allocation process does not provide assurance of either a genuinely random sequence or adequate allocation concealment. In particular, allocation was associated with clinical chart numbers and used fixed blocks of 10, which could have made upcoming allocations predictable. The description of “sealed envelopes” does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, or opened only after irreversible enrolment.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The report states: “**Random assignment was determined by a sealed-envelop technique based on clinical chart number with a block size of 10.**” It does not state that the ordering within blocks was random or computer-generated. A process “based on clinical chart number” and a fixed block size may allow prediction or manipulation of assignments. Under RoB 2, sealed envelopes alone are not sufficient evidence of allocation concealment unless key safeguards are reported.  
   
   Baseline characteristics for A1 and A2 were similar, including age, sex, cancer surgery, chemotherapy, metastasis and index DVT characteristics; thus there is no clear baseline imbalance suggesting a failure of randomization. However, baseline comparability cannot overcome the substantial concern that allocation concealment was probably inadequate.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention. Participants and treating physicians were aware of treatment assignment, but there is no evidence that deviations from protocol occurred because of the trial context, differed between groups, and affected recurrent VTE. The analysis was stated to be intention-to-treat.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The study was open label: “**Patients and treating physicians were not masked to treatment or status of RVT.**” Thus, participants and treating clinicians were aware of assignment. However, for the assignment effect, non-adherence or treatment changes are relevant only when they arose because of the trial context and were inconsistent with the protocol.  
   
   Continuation versus stopping anticoagulation was the intended randomized strategy. In A1, adherence to continued LMWH was high: “**15 of the 119 patients interrupted LMWH for less than 5 days during study treatment.**” No evidence is reported of important non-protocol anticoagulant use, crossover, or differential co-interventions caused by knowledge of assignment.  
   
   Importantly, the authors state: “**Data were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis**.” This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment, provided participants were retained in their randomized groups. Therefore, despite lack of masking, there is no identified trial-context deviation likely to materially bias the assignment-effect estimate.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns. Deaths and decreasing numbers under follow-up occurred in both randomized groups, and the report does not provide a fully transparent accounting of outcome availability, censoring, and reasons for non-complete follow-up for the recurrent-VTE analysis. Cancer progression and death are plausibly related to recurrent VTE risk, so censoring or loss of observation could be informative.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The trial reports that at 6 months after randomization, 108/119 A1 participants and 94/123 A2 participants remained under follow-up; at 12 months, the corresponding numbers were 91 and 77. In addition, “**42 patients (12.1%) died during 12 months of follow-up, all for progression of cancer**”: 12/119 (10.1%) in A1 and 19/123 (15.4%) in A2.  
   
   The article does not clearly distinguish, for each randomized group and each relevant follow-up period, participants with recurrent VTE, deaths, administrative censoring, withdrawal/loss to follow-up, and those with complete outcome observation. It also does not present a sensitivity analysis addressing the impact of censoring or death. Since advanced cancer progression—and hence death—may be associated with thrombosis risk, it is possible that missingness/censoring was related to the true recurrent-VTE outcome.  
   
   There is no direct evidence that missing data caused material bias, and the reported mortality difference was not statistically significant. Nevertheless, the incomplete accounting prevents a low-risk judgement.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias. Recurrent VTE was assessed with objective diagnostic criteria, and all outcomes were reviewed by a central committee blinded to treatment group and RVT status.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** Recurrent DVT was objectively defined using compression ultrasonography, including a “**previously fully compressible segment [that] became no longer compressible**” or “**an increase of more than 4 mm in the diameter of the residual thrombus during compression**.” Suspected pulmonary embolism was assessed using objective diagnostic algorithms, including ventilation-perfusion scanning or helical computed tomography.  
   
   Although treating clinicians and participants were unmasked, the report states: “**All outcomes and deaths were evaluated by a central committee whose members were unaware of the patient’s name, enrolling center, results of RVT assays, and group assignment. Committee members also reviewed all the results of clinical investigations.**” This blinded central adjudication and the objective nature of diagnostic tests substantially reduce the risk that knowledge of treatment assignment influenced classification of recurrent thrombotic events.  
   
   The authors acknowledge possible diagnostic-suspicion bias due to the open-label design, but state that blinded committee assessment reduced this concern. There is no evidence that formal outcome definitions or adjudication procedures differed between groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns. The registration record identifies recurrent DVT and/or PE as the primary outcome, consistent with the publication, but it does not provide a sufficiently detailed, date-stamped outcome definition, follow-up time point, or statistical analysis plan to establish that the reported recurrent-VTE result was analysed according to a prespecified plan finalized before unblinded data were available.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The registry record (NCT00450645) lists the primary outcome as “**Recurrent Deep Vein Thrombosis and/or Pulmonary Embolism**,” which is consistent with the article’s primary endpoint: “**time to recurrent VTE from completion of LMWH**.” This consistency reduces concern that the entire outcome domain was selectively omitted or substituted.  
   
   However, the registry supplies no detailed outcome timing, analysis population, censoring approach, definition of the exact comparison, or prespecified statistical analysis plan. The publication presents several related analyses and time periods, including overall recurrence counts, 12-month cumulative recurrence rates, adjusted hazard ratios, and analyses split into the first and second 6 months after randomization. There is no publicly available pre-unblinding analysis plan enabling assessment of whether the particular reported analysis was selected from multiple eligible approaches.  
   
   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was statistically significant or favoured either strategy. Therefore, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk of bias.
