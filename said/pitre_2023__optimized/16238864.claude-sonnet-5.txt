# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment
**Outcome: Intentions to consume the RDIFV**
**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention**

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** The allocation sequence was generated using a validated random number generator, and there is no evidence of a problem with allocation concealment (booklets were pre-ordered before distribution, removing opportunity for selective enrolment). Baseline comparability across conditions on all cognition variables, including intention, was confirmed statistically.

b. **Supporting evidence:**
- *Sequence generation:* "intervention and control booklets were placed in a single pile in an order determined by a sequence of random integers generated by a true random number generator (Haahr, 2004)."
- *Concealment:* Booklets were pre-randomized into a single pile and distributed sequentially in a classroom/lab setting, without visible allocation and with no reported opportunity for the researcher or participant to select or foresee assignment. No information suggests manipulation of allocation.
- *Baseline imbalance:* "MANOVA performed on pre-intervention behaviour...and cognition measures...revealed no significant differences between the control and experimental groups" and specifically for intention, Table 4 shows a non-significant pre-test ANOVA (F = .24, ns).

---

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** Although participants were necessarily aware of which task (intervention leaflet vs. control questionnaire task) they received, there is no evidence that this awareness led to deviations from the intended intervention (e.g., contamination, non-protocol interventions) that would have influenced the *immediate* post-intervention intention measure, which was collected within the same testing session before any opportunity for behavioural deviation. The analysis appears to include essentially all randomized/completing participants for this outcome, consistent with an appropriate (ITT-type) analysis.

b. **Supporting evidence:**
- Participants were asked "not to discuss the contents of their booklet with other participants over the following week," minimizing between-group contamination.
- Outcome assessed immediately: "After completing the control or intervention task, participants completed a second set of cognition measures identical to those they completed before the intervention/control task."
- No reported exclusions or crossovers prior to this immediate measurement; "In total, 218 people completed the booklet," and cognitive analyses appear based on this full completing sample.
- No mention of failures to implement the leaflet task or non-adherence to the immediate task instructions.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** The intention outcome was measured immediately following the intervention/control task, within the same booklet completed under examination conditions — prior to the 1-week follow-up period during which the reported 33% attrition occurred. There is no indication of missing data specific to the immediate post-intervention cognition measures.

b. **Supporting evidence:**
- "In total, 218 people completed the booklet (115 first-year and 105 second-year students)." All cognitive analyses (e.g., Tables 1–4, ANCOVAs on intention) appear based on this near-complete sample (analyses report df consistent with ~215–218 participants for intention, e.g., "Fð1; 215Þ ¼ 5:33").
- The 33% attrition applied only to the follow-up **behavioural** measures ("At follow-up, 146 people responded... attrition rate of 33%"), not to the immediate post-intervention intention outcome.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: High risk of bias**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** Intention is a participant-reported outcome, and participants were clearly aware of which condition they were in, since the intervention content (explicit persuasive messages and implementation-intention exercises) differed markedly and conspicuously from the control task (questions about the questionnaire itself). Because the persuasive messages explicitly and directly targeted intention (e.g., instructing participants to "make a firm decision now"), and the intention measure was collected immediately afterward in the same session, it is likely that participants' self-reported intention scores were influenced by demand characteristics/awareness of the intervention's purpose, rather than reflecting a stable, unbiased assessment of underlying motivation.

b. **Supporting evidence:**
- *Outcome type:* Intention was measured via self-report Likert-type items (participant-reported outcome), where the "outcome assessor" is the participant.
- *Awareness:* The intervention explicitly stated: "You can do it, so do it this week. Make a firm decision now that you will eat the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables each day this week," whereas controls answered neutral questions about the questionnaire itself — making group assignment highly salient to participants.
- *Immediacy and directness:* "participants completed a second set of cognition measures identical to those they completed before the intervention/control task," measured immediately after receiving persuasive messages explicitly designed to raise intention — a scenario with strong potential for socially desirable or demand-driven responding rather than genuine attitude change.
- The authors themselves acknowledge the closeness of intervention content to the measured construct is a limitation of self-report design, and no steps (e.g., blinded/independent assessment, delayed measurement, objective corroboration) were taken to reduce this risk for the intention outcome specifically.

---

## 5. Selective reporting

**Judgement: Some concerns**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** There is no evidence in the article of a publicly available, date-stamped, pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial protocol establishing in advance that intention (among the multiple TPB/AR cognitive measures collected — attitude, norm, PBC, self-efficacy, intention, anticipated regret) would be analysed and reported in the manner presented. However, all cognitive variables measured appear to have been reported comprehensively (Tables 1–4) with no evidence of selective omission of measures or analyses specifically disadvantageous to the hypothesis. In the absence of registry/protocol information to confirm the reported result matches a pre-specified plan, "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk" is appropriate.

b. **Supporting evidence:**
- The article reports results for all six TPB-related cognitive variables (intention, attitude, norms, PBC, self-efficacy, anticipated regret) at both pre- and post-intervention time points, with no apparent unexplained omissions: "Univariate ANOVAs showed that there were no significant pre-intervention differences on intention, self-efficacy, attitudes, norms, or anticipated regret... see Table 4 for means and F's for intention and anticipated regret."
- No trial protocol, registration number, or statistical analysis plan is cited or referenced in the methods, and the associated trial registration record (doc_id: 4dab5cab-2b44-42f8-8559-2dd9073b18b5) does not appear to provide corroborating pre-specification details predating unblinded data access for this specific outcome, precluding a "Low risk" judgement.
- No indication of multiple alternative operationalisations of "intention" being tested and selectively reported (a single 5-item intention scale is consistently used throughout), which limits (but does not eliminate) concern about selection of the reported result.
