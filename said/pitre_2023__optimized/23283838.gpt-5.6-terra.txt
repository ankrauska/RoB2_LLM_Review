1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants “were randomly assigned to 250mg of DCS or placebo in identical-appearing capsules by a data manager who had no interaction with participants.” This supports independent administration of assignment and is compatible with allocation concealment. However, the article does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table, blocked randomization) or explicitly state that the sequence was concealed until enrolment and assignment.  
   
   Baseline characteristics were generally similar: “Participants did not differ across group in regard to: gender, ethnicity, use of medication, use of antidepressants, LSAS scores at Visit 1, average SUDS for the speech in Visit 1, or Social Phobia Scale ratings at Visit 1.” Although age differed between groups (*p* = .019), this isolated imbalance in a small sample does not by itself demonstrate a failure of randomization. The incomplete reporting of sequence generation and concealment nevertheless prevents a low-risk judgement.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants received either DCS or matching placebo before the first exposure session, and both participants and study personnel were intended to be blinded: “Participants were randomly assigned to 250mg of DCS or placebo in identical-appearing capsules.” The success of blinding was evaluated: “neither participants nor experimenters were able to discern assignment (ps > .15).” Thus, there is no evidence that knowledge of allocation led to differential implementation of exposure, differential co-interventions, or other deviations arising from the trial context.  
   
   The exposure task was individually tailored, but this was part of the intended intervention strategy: the procedure aimed “to standardize, as precisely as possible, experience of anxiety, as measured by SUDS levels,” rather than impose identical speech parameters. There is no indication that tailoring differed systematically because of treatment assignment.  
   
   The reported analysis included only participants who completed Visit 2. For the present effect-of-assignment assessment, exclusion due to absent Visit-2 outcome data is addressed primarily under missing outcome data rather than this domain. Participants with observed outcome data were analysed according to their randomized DCS/placebo group; there is no evidence of an as-treated or per-protocol analysis based on treatment received or adherence.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   e. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   f. **Rationale:** Outcome data at Visit 2 were unavailable for 4 of 34 randomized participants (12%): “Four participants (2 DCS, 2 placebo) did not return for Visit 2 and were not included in any analyses.” Therefore, data were not available for all or nearly all randomized participants. The amount of missing data was balanced numerically between groups, which reduces concern about clearly differential attrition.  
   
   The reported reasons were also broadly similar across groups: two participants became unreachable (one DCS and one placebo), and two chose not to continue (one DCS and one placebo). However, no sensitivity analysis or other analysis addressing the possible effect of the missing data was reported. In a study of subjective anxiety and between-session habituation, non-return or withdrawal could plausibly be related to participants’ anxiety, perceived benefit, or difficulty with the exposure task. There is insufficient evidence that missingness was likely related to the unobserved outcome values, but this possibility cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Between-session habituation was measured using the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS), a standard participant-reported measure of subjective anxiety. The study clearly defined the outcome: categorical response was “a drop of at least 5 points in average SUDS across the exposure across visits.” SUDS ratings were collected repeatedly during each speech and averaged; the authors report “excellent internal consistency” for the combined ratings (“Cronbach’s αs > .85”). There is no indication that outcome measurement methods or timing differed between DCS and placebo groups.  
   
   Although participants were the outcome assessors, they were blinded to assigned treatment, and matching capsules were used. Moreover, the study assessed blinding and found that “neither participants nor experimenters were able to discern assignment (ps > .15).” Thus, knowledge of intervention received was unlikely to have influenced self-reported SUDS ratings materially. The same speech-exposure assessment procedures were repeated at the second visit.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies between-session habituation as the primary outcome and defines it in advance in the Methods: “Our primary measure of DCS’s exposure-facilitating effect was between-session habituation,” operationalized as a decrease of at least 5 SUDS points across visits. The categorical between-session habituation result was reported, including the group comparison: 75% in the DCS group versus 36% in the placebo group, *p* = .030. This consistency reduces concern that the reported result was selected from an unreported set of outcome domains.  
   
   However, there is no available pre-specified statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the exact outcome measurement, threshold for habituation (≥5-point reduction), analysis method, and time-point handling were finalized before unblinded data were available. The study measured SUDS repeatedly within each session and also analysed average SUDS continuously, categorical response, and Social Phobia Scale outcomes. These multiple possible measurements and analytic choices create opportunity for selective selection of a favourable result. The available information does not provide clear evidence that the reported between-session habituation analysis was selected on the basis of results, but it also does not rule this out. Therefore, some concerns are warranted.
