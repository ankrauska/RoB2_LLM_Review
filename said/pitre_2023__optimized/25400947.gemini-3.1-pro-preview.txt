**1. bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**
a. Judgement: High risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The study states it used "simple random sampling" but provides no information on whether the allocation sequence was concealed. Furthermore, there is a highly significant baseline imbalance in a key prognostic factor (cervical effacement). Table 3 shows baseline effacement was significantly different between the MgSO4 and Celecoxib groups (35.93 vs 42, P ≤ 0.0001). The combination of no information on allocation concealment and a severe baseline imbalance suggests a problem with the randomization process.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**
a. Judgement: Some concerns.
b. Rationale: The trial is described as a "single blinded randomized clinical trial." Because the interventions are administered differently (intravenous MgSO4 vs. oral celecoxib capsules) and no double-dummy design is mentioned, it is likely that participants and/or care providers were aware of the assigned interventions. While there is no direct evidence that deviations from the intended intervention occurred due to the trial context, the lack of blinding and absence of a clear intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis statement raises some concerns.

**3. missing outcome data: high risk of bias**
a. Judgement: High risk of bias.
b. Rationale: There are unexplained inconsistencies and missing data in the reported outcomes. The study randomized 600 women (300 per group, as confirmed by Table 3). However, in Table 1 for the Celecoxib group, the frequencies for delivery before and after 48 hours are reported as 227 (75.7%) and 30 (24.3%). The number of participants adds up to 257, not 300, leaving 43 participants unaccounted for. Furthermore, 227 + 30 does not match the percentages provided. This severe discrepancy and missing outcome data for over 10% of the Celecoxib group without explanation leads to a high risk of bias.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Low risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The outcome being assessed is the frequency of delivery after 48 hours. This is an objective, highly definitive clinical event. Even if outcome assessors or care providers were aware of the intervention received (due to the single-blind nature of the study), the measurement of the time of delivery is highly unlikely to be influenced by this knowledge.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
a. Judgement: Some concerns.
b. Rationale: There is no information provided regarding a pre-specified statistical analysis plan or a trial registration that was finalized before unblinded data were available. Without a published protocol to verify the pre-specified primary and secondary outcomes, we cannot completely rule out selective reporting, leading to some concerns.
