# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Topiramate for Olanzapine-Related Weight Gain
**Outcome: Weight | Effect of interest: Assignment to intervention (ITT)**

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, primarily due to lack of information about allocation concealment and unclear description of the randomization method, combined with a notable baseline discrepancy between intended and achieved sample sizes.

**b. Rationale:** 
- Sequence generation: The text states "According to a power analysis, 49 patients were required... The randomization was carried out confidentially by the clinic administration, and arranged so that 25 test persons would be treated with the active drug and 24 with a placebo." No description of the actual method used to generate the random sequence (e.g., computer-generated, random number table) is provided. This warrants "Probably yes" given the reference to a formal, pre-specified allocation ratio, but with genuine uncertainty.
- Allocation concealment: There is no explicit description of how allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants beyond stating it was done "confidentially by the clinic administration." This is vague and does not meet the minimal criteria (e.g., sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes; central randomization office) described in the guidance. This raises to "No information" on the signalling question.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows groups reasonably well-balanced on age, height, initial body weight, weight gain, duration of olanzapine treatment, and current olanzapine dose (all differences appear compatible with chance, no p-values reported suggesting statistical testing, and magnitudes are modest). However, note the discrepancy: 25 vs 24 were allocated (per the text), but only 18 remained in the placebo group by analysis (6 dropped from placebo group only) — this is addressed in Domain 3, not here, since allocation numbers do not suggest a randomization problem itself.
- Overall: Because allocation concealment is not described in sufficient detail to confirm adequacy, and sequence generation method is not explicitly detailed (though a formal randomization process is implied), this leads to "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (Effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, due to lack of information on whether deviations from intended intervention arose because of the trial context, combined with an ambiguous description of the analysis approach (i.e., whether it was a true ITT analysis).

**b. Rationale:**
- Blinding of participants and personnel: The study states, "Both subjects and clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment," and tablets were "supplied in numbered boxes." This supports a "No" (participants unaware) and "No" (personnel unaware) response to signalling questions 2.1 and 2.2, which — if true and effective — would support a low risk of bias in this domain.
- However, topiramate has recognizable side effects (dizziness, paresthesia, cognitive impairment) that could unblind participants or clinicians. The article states: "In isolated cases, dizziness, headache, and paresthesia were reported," which raises the possibility that blinding could have been compromised in some participants, though this was not formally tested (no post-study "guess the allocation" assessment reported). This creates some uncertainty (“No information” practically) about whether blinding was fully maintained throughout.
- Analysis approach: The paper states "data from 43 women (response rate 87.8%) were evaluated" after 6 placebo-group dropouts were excluded ("their data were not further analyzed"). This is NOT a full ITT analysis, as it excludes randomized participants with missing outcome data rather than using an ITT or modified-ITT approach that includes imputed values. This constitutes an analysis excluding participants with missing outcome data (an "mITT" analysis, which is acceptable per RoB2 for this domain, since missing outcome data bias is assessed in Domain 3) — this alone would not downgrade this domain, since mITT excluding missing data is considered acceptable for the assignment-effect analysis per section 5.3.1.
- Given blinding appears to have been maintained (medication supplied in numbered/identical boxes, both parties described as blinded), and no clear evidence of trial-context-driven deviations (e.g., non-protocol interventions, contamination) is reported, but the possibility of unblinding via recognizable side effects is not addressed or tested, we judge this as "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias due to unbalanced attrition between groups, with all dropouts occurring exclusively in the placebo group, and no evidence (sensitivity analyses) that the result is robust to this imbalance.

**b. Rationale:**
- The article states: "Six subjects who failed to appear for more than one evaluation (all from the placebo group) dropped out of the study, and their data were not further analyzed. Finally, data from 43 women (response rate 87.8%) were evaluated."
- This represents differential attrition: 0/25 (0%) dropped from the topiramate group versus 6/24 (25%) dropped from the placebo group. This large disparity in missing data between arms is a strong signal of potential bias (per section 6.1.4.1: "differing proportions of missing outcome data in the experimental and comparator intervention groups provide evidence of potential bias").
- No reasons are given for why these 6 participants specifically failed to appear, and no analysis (e.g., sensitivity analysis, imputation) was conducted to explore whether missingness was related to the outcome (e.g., participants who were not losing weight/were dissatisfied with lack of effect may have been more likely to drop out of the placebo arm).
- Given that weight is the outcome of interest, and dropout from a placebo arm in a weight-loss trial context is plausible to relate to the outcome itself (e.g., dissatisfaction with lack of weight loss), it is likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value, and reasons differed systematically between groups (all missingness occurred in one arm only).
- No CONSORT flow diagram is provided, and no sensitivity analyses were performed to demonstrate robustness of the result to these missing data.
- This satisfies the criteria for "High risk of bias": incomplete outcome data with no evidence of correction, differing rates of missingness between groups, and a plausible mechanism for informative missingness.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as weight was measured objectively using a physical measurement method that would not be influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment, and outcome ascertainment was standardized across groups.

**b. Rationale:**
- Method of measurement: "Every 2 weeks, subjects were seen, weighed..." — this is an objective outcome (body weight), not involving judgement by an assessor. There is no reason to believe the method of measurement was inappropriate.
- Comparability of measurement between groups: Both groups were weighed on the same schedule (every 2 weeks) using presumably the same procedures/equipment across the 10-week study, so ascertainment does not appear to have differed between groups.
- Blinding of outcome assessor: Because weight is an "observer-reported outcome not involving judgement" (analogous to an objective, automated measurement), per section 7.1(3), "the assessment of outcome is usually not likely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention received," even if the person taking the measurement (e.g., clinic staff) was aware of allocation status (though blinding was reportedly maintained: "Both subjects and clinicians were blinded regarding topiramate/placebo assignment").
- Given weight is an objective, unambiguous, non-judgement-based measurement, and staff were reportedly blinded, this domain is judged as low risk.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the weight outcome, as weight loss was clearly a pre-specified primary outcome, measured and reported using a single, consistent method (biweekly weighing) and a single analysis (Mann-Whitney U test, difference in kg with 95% CI), consistent with what would be expected/registered for this type of trial, with no evidence of selective reporting from multiple candidate measures or analyses.

**b. Rationale:**
- The Methods state: "Primary outcome measures were weight checks and self-reported changes on the scales of the SF-36 Health Survey, Bf-S Scale of Well-Being, and the Adjective Checklist EWL-60-S," clearly designating weight as a primary, pre-specified outcome.
- Weight was measured using a single, consistent method (repeated weighing every 2 weeks) and reported using a single metric (final weight/change in kg) with a single statistical test (Mann-Whitney U) and a single effect estimate (difference in kg, 95% CI), with no indication that multiple alternative measurements (e.g., BMI, waist circumference) or multiple alternative analyses (e.g., unadjusted vs. adjusted, different time points selectively reported) were available but selectively omitted.
- No trial registry record was identified/provided in the supplied materials to allow direct comparison against a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinding; however, the outcome (weight) is unambiguous, has only one plausible primary analysis (change in kg over 10 weeks), and the full time-course of weight change is reported graphically (Figure 1) for all measured time points (0–10 weeks), suggesting no selective time-point reporting.
- Given the straightforward, singular nature of the weight outcome and its consistent reporting throughout, with no evidence of selection from multiple eligible measurements or analyses, this domain is judged as low risk of bias.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias for the Outcome "Weight": **High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Although randomization (Some concerns), deviations from intended intervention (Some concerns), and outcome measurement/selective reporting (Low risk) domains raise only modest or no concerns, the domain of **missing outcome data** is judged at **High risk of bias** due to complete asymmetry in attrition (all 6 dropouts occurred in the placebo group only, representing 25% of that arm), with no reported reasons for dropout and no sensitivity analyses to rule out that missingness was related to the outcome. Per RoB 2 guidance (Table 1), a "High" risk of bias judgement in any single domain results in an overall "High risk of bias" judgement for this outcome.
