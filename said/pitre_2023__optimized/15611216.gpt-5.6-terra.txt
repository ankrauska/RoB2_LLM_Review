1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns arise because the report states that participants were randomly assigned, including allocation by weekly time blocks, but does not describe how the random sequence was generated or how allocation was concealed. There was also a statistically significant baseline imbalance in pretest arousal imbalance.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors report a “*pretest–posttest double-blinded experimental design with random assignment*” and state that they “*randomly assigned participants to study groups in time blocks of 1 week*,” with time blocks also randomized as treatment or control weeks. However, the report does not specify a random-number generation method, block sizes, who generated the sequence, or any allocation-concealment mechanism (e.g., central allocation or opaque sequential envelopes).

   Baseline agitation was reasonably balanced (38.97 vs 32.59; *p* = .186), as were the reported demographic and clinical variables. However, the experimental group had significantly greater baseline arousal imbalance than the control group (107.4 vs 84.6 minutes/12 hours; *p* = .012). This single imbalance is not sufficiently extreme to demonstrate a failure of randomization, and it may be compatible with chance, but it adds concern given the absent reporting of sequence generation and concealment. The investigators adjusted for baseline arousal imbalance, but such adjustment does not establish that the randomization process was sound.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline, because the reported analysis excluded randomized participants after randomization, including 10 participants who did not receive the intervention. This is not a full intention-to-treat analysis, and the number excluded could materially affect the result.

   b. **Rationale:** Although the study is described as “*double-blinded*,” only “*data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions*.” Personnel implementing the BACE schedule—nursing staff and the geriatric nurse practitioner—necessarily knew which participants were assigned to BACE, because they developed and implemented altered activity schedules. However, the article does not clearly document trial-context deviations or differential co-interventions that would by themselves establish bias in the assignment effect.

   The major issue is the analysis population. Of 102 participants who “*participated in the study*,” 12 died, 10 were “*dropped from the study for not being given the intervention,*” and 2 were excluded for “*erroneous or missing data*,” leaving 78 participants analysed. In particular, the authors state:

   > “*A participant had to have been given at least one of the prescribed changes in activity schedule … to be included in the analysis.*”

   and:

   > “*Nurses stated the intervention was not given in 10 cases because of being short-staffed, being busy, or having more emergent needs the day of the scheduled intervention.*”

   Excluding participants because the assigned intervention was not delivered violates the principle of analysing participants in their randomized groups when estimating the effect of assignment. Such exclusions may be related to care demands or clinical circumstances that are also related to agitation. With 10 of 102 participants excluded specifically for non-delivery of the intervention, there is potential for a substantial impact on the Group × Time result. The reported result is therefore not an appropriate estimate of the effect of assignment at baseline.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns arise because posttest agitation data were unavailable for a meaningful proportion of randomized participants, no group-specific accounting of missing outcome data is reported, and no sensitivity analysis addresses the potential effect of the missing data.

   b. **Rationale:** The final analysed sample was 78 of 102 enrolled participants. The report indicates that 12 participants died and 2 were excluded because of “*erroneous or missing data*.” Thus, outcome data were not available for all or nearly all randomized participants. For a continuous outcome such as agitation score, approximately 14% of participants having unavailable data owing to death or erroneous/missing data is not trivial.

   The article does not report the numbers who died or had missing/erroneous data by intervention group, whether deaths occurred before posttest measurement, or whether the characteristics and agitation levels of those without posttest data differed from those analysed. No analysis accounting for missing data, and no sensitivity analysis, is reported.

   Death and withdrawal/missingness among people with severe dementia could plausibly be associated with health status and potentially with agitation; however, the available information does not establish that missingness was likely dependent on the unobserved posttest agitation score, nor does it establish differential missingness between groups. Therefore, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because agitation was assessed through structured direct observation by data collectors reported to be blinded to treatment assignment, using the same observation schedule and scoring approach in both groups.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was observed agitation, assessed on pretest and posttest days. Research assistants observed participants “*for 3 min every 15 min*” between 8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. and rated agitation using a 0–100 visual analogue scale with defined intensity parameters. The same method and timing were used across the BACE and control groups.

   Importantly, the article states that “*data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions*.” Thus, the outcome assessors were reportedly unaware of the assigned intervention. The study also describes extensive observer training and reliability monitoring: data collection began only after interrater reliability was at least .85 on five sequential assessments, and reported post-training interrater reliabilities for agitation ranged from .95 to .984.

   Although the visual analogue agitation measure was introduced after the originally planned Agitation Behavior Mapping Instrument could not be rated reliably, the report provides defined scoring criteria and strong interrater reliability. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment methods differed systematically between groups. Accordingly, there is no important evidence of differential outcome measurement bias.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because there is no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan available in the report to verify that the Table 4 agitation-score result was selected from among multiple outcome measurements, time points, covariate specifications, or analyses on the basis of the results.

   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies agitation as the main outcome and reports the planned primary comparison as a repeated-measures ANCOVA of pretest and posttest agitation scores. Table 4 reports the Group × Time interaction, adjusted for pretest arousal imbalance and MMSE; the text also notes that therapeutic activities were added as an additional covariate but were not statistically significant.

   However, the publication does not provide a dated protocol or statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. It reports several analyses of agitation, including:
   - the adjusted repeated-measures ANCOVA in Table 4;
   - within-group paired *t* tests;
   - agitation during arousal-balance and arousal-imbalance periods (Table 5);
   - three additional repeated-measures ANCOVAs using different interaction variables; and
   - analyses involving different covariate specifications.

   These analyses may be clinically and methodologically reasonable, but without a pre-specified detailed analysis plan it is not possible to exclude selective choice of the reported Table 4 model, covariate adjustment, or result. There is no direct evidence that the reported Table 4 result was selected because it was favourable or statistically significant; therefore, some concerns, rather than high risk of bias, is warranted.
