1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias. The allocation sequence was computer-generated, allocation was concealed by an independent offsite investigator using consecutively numbered opaque envelopes, and personnel involved in training and assessment and participants were blinded to allocation.

   b. **Rationale:** The article reports: “An offsite investigator prepared the concealed randomization using a computer generated random number sequence (1:1).” It further states that “Consecutively numbered opaque envelopes containing group allocation were collected from the offsite investigator by the tRNS intervener after initial assessment.”

   These procedures are also consistent with the prospectively registered protocol, which specifies an independent person, a computer-generated random sequence, and consecutively numbered opaque envelopes. The allocation was not revealed to the investigator team until completion of training and follow-up assessments. Although the sample was extremely small (two participants per group), the reported baseline differences in MAS6 and other characteristics do not by themselves demonstrate a problem with randomization; chance imbalance is plausible in such a pilot trial.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to active versus sham tRNS at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants, training staff, assessors, and data analysts were reported to be blinded: “Study personnel involved in assessment and training, along with all participants were blinded to group allocation for the duration of the study.” The stimulation device was kept out of sight, and participants were informed that they “may or may not feel the stimulation irrespective of whether they were receiving active or sham stimulation.”

   There is no report of departures from the assigned intervention attributable to the trial context, differential co-interventions, or unblinding that would plausibly affect MAS6. Both groups received the same reaching-training programme and outcome-triggered peripheral nerve stimulation as required; the intended contrast was active versus sham tRNS. All four reported participants completed all 12 planned training sessions, with no missed sessions. The registry specified that analysis would be conducted on an intention-to-treat basis, and the publication presents outcomes for each randomized participant according to their allocated tRNS group. Therefore, there is no indication that participants were analysed contrary to their randomized assignment.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data for MAS6 appear available for all randomized participants included in the publication at baseline, post-training, and follow-up. Table 3 reports MAS6 values at T0, T1, and T2 for all four participants. The article states: “All four participants completed the 12 training sessions over 4-weeks, with no training sessions missed.”

   No losses to follow-up, withdrawals, unavailable MAS6 assessments, or exclusions from the reported MAS6 outcome analysis are described. Thus, for the four participants reported in this pilot case series, missing MAS6 data were absent.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** MAS6 is a structured clinician-assessed measure of upper-arm function and was the stated primary clinical outcome in the publication: “The primary clinical outcome measure was Motor Assessment Scale item 6 (Upper Arm Function, MAS6).” There is no indication that the measure was unsuitable for assessing upper-arm activity in this population.

   Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation: “Study personnel involved in assessment and training, along with all participants were blinded to group allocation for the duration of the study.” While MAS6 involves some assessor judgement, assessor blinding substantially limits the risk that knowledge of active or sham tRNS influenced ratings. Assessments were made at the same scheduled time points in both groups (baseline, 4 weeks, and 12 weeks), with no indication of differential assessment procedures.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was prospectively registered before the first participant was enrolled. However, the registry identifies the primary outcome as the **composite score of MAS upper-limb items 6–8**, measured at weeks 0, 4, and 12. In contrast, the publication states that “The primary clinical outcome measure was Motor Assessment Scale item 6 (Upper Arm Function, MAS6),” and Table 3 reports MAS6 results but does not present the registered composite MAS6–8 outcome.

   The publication does mention MAS items 7 and 8 as secondary measures, but numerical results for these measures are not reported in the provided results table. This discrepancy means it cannot be confirmed that the reported MAS6 result was selected according to a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before outcome data were available. In addition, neither the registration record nor the publication provides a sufficiently detailed pre-specified analysis plan for the specific MAS6-only result—for example, whether the intended analysis was change from baseline, post-intervention score, follow-up score, or a particular between-group comparison.

   There is not enough evidence to conclude that MAS6 was selected *because of* its result; therefore, a high-risk judgement is not warranted. Nevertheless, the discrepancy between the registered composite primary outcome and the published MAS6-focused reporting raises some concerns about selective reporting.
