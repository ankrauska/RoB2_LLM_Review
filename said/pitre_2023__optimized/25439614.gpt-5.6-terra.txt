1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study is described as a multicentre randomized clinical trial and states that eyes were “assigned randomly” to ranibizumab with prompt or deferred focal/grid laser. However, the report does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (for example, computer-generated randomization or random permuted blocks) or how allocation was concealed until enrolment/assignment. Therefore, the randomization process cannot be confirmed as adequately implemented.

   There is no clear evidence in the report of a problematic randomization process, such as a marked discrepancy in initial group sizes (180 versus 181 eyes were assigned to the two ranibizumab groups). However, baseline characteristics by randomized treatment group are not provided in the supplied 5-year report; Table 1 instead compares 5-year completers with non-completers. Thus, baseline balance between the randomized groups cannot be fully evaluated from this report.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** The comparison was between treatment *strategies* assigned at baseline: ranibizumab with prompt focal/grid laser versus ranibizumab with laser deferred for at least 24 weeks. Subsequent treatment was governed by protocol-defined retreatment criteria: “An assessment for retreatment with ranibizumab, retreatment with laser, or initiation of laser treatment for the deferred group, occurred at each visit. The treatment decision was based on protocol specified criteria.” Consequently, the differing use of laser and numbers of ranibizumab injections are expected components of the assigned strategies, rather than deviations relevant to the effect of assignment.

   Participants and treating personnel were likely aware of whether laser was given promptly or deferred. Nevertheless, there is no evidence that awareness led to important non-protocol deviations arising specifically because of the trial context and likely to affect visual acuity. The report notes other DME treatment in 9/124 prompt-laser completers, of which 7 were protocol-permitted because futility/failure criteria had been met; only 2 treatments were not per protocol. This small number is unlikely to have materially biased the comparison.

   The principal visual-acuity analysis retained the randomized treatment-group comparison and used “all available visit data” in longitudinal models. The exclusion of 14 participants from one site was based on a subsequently determined failure to meet the OCT eligibility criterion at baseline, rather than apparent exclusion according to outcome or treatment received. Thus, the analysis is broadly compatible with estimating the effect of assignment, while the consequences of unavailable visual-acuity data are considered separately under missing outcome data.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** A substantial proportion of randomized participants did not contribute 5-year visual-acuity data. Excluding deaths, the 5-year completion rates among the original randomized participants were 76% (124/163) in the prompt-laser group and 74% (111/150) in the deferred-laser group. Thus, approximately one quarter of participants in each group lacked the 5-year outcome, predominantly because years 4–5 were an extension and some participants did not consent to continued follow-up.

   The authors explicitly acknowledge this limitation: “the 5-year data is absent on about a quarter (excluding deaths) of all randomized eyes mostly due to participants who chose not to continue in the extension phase of the study.” They also report differences between completers and non-completers in factors including age, prior panretinal photocoagulation, lens status, and diabetic-retinopathy severity. These characteristics may be associated with visual prognosis.

   The longitudinal model used available measurements and included adjustments for selected observed characteristics, but there is no reported sensitivity analysis demonstrating robustness to plausible outcomes among those missing at 5 years. Although missingness was relatively similar in magnitude between treatment groups and there is no direct evidence that it depended on unobserved 5-year visual acuity, it is plausible that willingness to continue in a 2-year extension could be related to participants’ clinical course or visual outcome. This leads to some concerns rather than high risk of bias.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was best-corrected visual acuity, assessed using visual-acuity letter scores. This is an appropriate and established method for measuring visual acuity in this setting. The same outcome and follow-up assessment framework were used in both randomized groups.

   Importantly, the authors report “masking of the visual acuity examiners and OCT technicians.” Because visual-acuity assessment was performed by masked examiners, knowledge of whether a participant had received prompt or deferred laser is unlikely to have influenced outcome measurement. There is no indication that the method or timing of visual-acuity ascertainment differed systematically between groups in a way that could bias the 5-year comparison.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Visual acuity was identified as the “Main Outcome Measure[]” for the 5-year report, and the article reports the principal 5-year mean change in visual-acuity letter score, its confidence interval and P value, as well as several categorical visual-acuity outcomes. The reported principal result is clearly identifiable: an estimated mean change of +7.2 letters with prompt laser versus +9.8 letters with deferred laser, with a prompt-versus-deferred difference of −2.6 letters (95% CI −5.5 to +0.4).

   However, low risk of bias in this domain requires confirmation that the specific numerical result was analysed according to a sufficiently detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The supplied report describes the analyses but does not provide a dated statistical analysis plan or enough information to establish that the selected 5-year longitudinal model, time point, handling of outliers (truncation at ±30 letters), covariate adjustment, and choice among multiple reported visual-acuity analyses were all pre-specified before outcome data were examined. The trial registration information referenced in the request does not, as presented here, resolve these analysis-level questions.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported visual-acuity result was selected because it was favourable or statistically significant. Nevertheless, the absence of verifiable pre-specified analysis intentions, alongside multiple possible visual-acuity time points and analytic approaches, results in some concerns.
