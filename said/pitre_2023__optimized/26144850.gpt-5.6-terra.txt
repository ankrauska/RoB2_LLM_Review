1. **bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because although the allocation sequence appears random, allocation concealment was inadequate.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors state that “268 three-digit random numbers from the table were written in the patient registers in advance,” with patients assigned according to whether the pre-written number was even (PB-MDT) or odd (ROM-6). Use of a random-number table supports a random sequence (RoB 2 signalling question 1.1: **Yes**).

   However, recording the random numbers in patient registers *in advance*, with a transparent even/odd allocation rule, means that personnel enrolling patients could potentially know the next allocation before enrolment. There is no indication that the register was inaccessible to recruiters, that allocation was centrally administered, or that opaque sequentially numbered sealed envelopes or another adequate concealment mechanism was used. Thus, allocation was probably not concealed until enrolment and assignment (question 1.2: **Probably no**).

   Baseline characteristics do not show a clear imbalance that is very unlikely to have occurred by chance. For example, mean age was 36.4 versus 40.2 years, and sex and lesion-number distributions were reasonably similar. Therefore, baseline data do not themselves demonstrate a failure of randomization (question 1.3: **Probably no**). Nevertheless, inadequate allocation concealment leads to a high-risk judgement for this domain.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the interventions at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the intention-to-treat effect—i.e., the effect of being assigned to ROM-6 or PB-MDT at baseline. The study was explicitly unblinded: “Blinding to treatment was not undertaken.” Participants and those delivering treatment were therefore aware of the assigned intervention (questions 2.1 and 2.2: **Yes**).

   The principal reason for the high-risk judgement is that the 2-year cure result was not analysed using all randomized participants in their originally assigned groups. The article reports cure rates “of those who completed treatment,” and Table 5 is restricted to treatment completers: 107/134 in the PB-MDT group and 115/134 in the ROM-6 group. At 24 months, outcome data were reported for 100 PB-MDT and 103 ROM-6 participants, rather than all 134 randomized participants per group.

   There were substantial post-randomization treatment discontinuations: 27/134 (20.1%) in PB-MDT and 19/134 (14.2%) in ROM-6. Reasons included intolerance/weakness, swelling of feet, vomiting/diarrhoea, pregnancy, perceived cure, stigma, and loss to treatment. The analysis therefore excluded a substantial number of eligible randomized participants, rather than preserving their randomized groups in an intention-to-treat or modified intention-to-treat analysis (question 2.6: **No**; question 2.7: **Yes**, substantial potential impact).

   There is no clear evidence that deviations arose specifically because of the trial context, rather than ordinary non-adherence or discontinuation. However, this does not resolve the bias introduced by excluding many randomized participants from the reported 2-year cure analysis.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:** Outcome data at 2 years were unavailable for a substantial proportion of randomized participants. Only 100/134 (74.6%) participants assigned to PB-MDT and 103/134 (76.9%) assigned to ROM-6 were followed at 2 years. Thus, cure-status data were unavailable for approximately 25% and 23% of the randomized groups, respectively (question 3.1: **No**).

   No appropriate sensitivity analysis, imputation strategy, or other analysis demonstrating robustness to the missing data was reported (question 3.2: **No**). The reported cure analysis was restricted to those who completed treatment and were followed up.

   Missingness could plausibly be related to true cure status. Reasons for discontinuation included “felt cured/leprosy stigma,” treatment intolerance, vomiting/diarrhoea, and being lost to treatment. Patients who believed themselves cured, those with continuing disease, and those experiencing adverse effects may have had systematically different clinical outcomes from those retained in follow-up. The authors themselves acknowledge an important limitation: “A good number of patients were lost to follow up after release from treatment.” Although this comment is made particularly in relation to relapse ascertainment, the same extensive attrition affects confidence in the 2-year cure estimate.

   The proportions missing were broadly similar between groups, but the amount of missing data is large and the reasons make informative missingness likely (questions 3.3 and 3.4: **Probably yes**). Therefore, the result is at high risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Cure was based on clinical assessment. The authors defined cure as “complete resolution of the lesion or patch becoming flat, hypopigmented and with decrease in size of the lesion and/or regaining of sensation.” This appears to be a clinically relevant and broadly appropriate outcome definition (question 4.1: **Probably no**). The same scheduled follow-up process appears to have been intended for both groups, so there is no clear evidence that the formal measurement method differed by group (question 4.2: **Probably no**).

   However, outcome assessment was unblinded. The report states: “Blinding to treatment was not undertaken.” The trained paramedical worker undertook follow-up examinations and monitored “lesion activity, erythema, infiltration, size, any new lesion and/or new nerve thickening or deformity.” These assessments involve clinical judgement, particularly regarding changes in lesion size, flattening, activity, and sensory recovery. Thus, outcome assessors were likely aware of treatment allocation (question 4.3: **Yes**), and assessment could be influenced by that knowledge (question 4.4: **Yes**).

   Given the subjective and judgement-dependent nature of the clinical cure assessment, the absence of blinded outcome assessment, and the investigators’ interest in demonstrating that ROM-6 was an effective alternative to PB-MDT, it is likely that knowledge of treatment could have influenced assessments (question 4.5: **Probably yes**). This supports a high-risk judgement.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study was registered only after completion of recruitment and follow-up. The report states: “This study is now registered on Clinical Trial Registry of India vide REF/2013/11/005995 dated 22 November, 2013,” whereas recruitment occurred during 2001–2004 and the study follow-up was conducted through November 2010. Therefore, the registration could not provide a prospectively finalized analysis plan before unblinded outcome data became available.

   The publication reports cure outcomes at several time points—release from treatment, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months—and longer-term follow-up data. The 2-year cure outcome is consistent with the stated objective and is described as an “interim observation at 2 years after release from treatment.” However, no prospectively dated protocol or statistical analysis plan is available to verify the pre-specification of the exact 2-year cure definition, analysis population, denominators, handling of defaulters/missing data, or choice of analysis among the multiple reported time points.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported 2-year cure result was selected because it was favourable, nor clear evidence of unreported alternative cure measures or analyses. Consequently, high risk cannot be assigned. Nevertheless, the retrospective registration and lack of a prospectively finalized analysis plan mean that selective reporting cannot be excluded (question 5.1: **No**; questions 5.2 and 5.3: **No information**).
