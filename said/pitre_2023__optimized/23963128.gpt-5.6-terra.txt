1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the study is described as randomized, and the resulting group sizes were reasonably similar, but the report does not describe either random-sequence generation or concealment of allocation.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors state only that “**Residents were randomized to one of three levels of supervision**.” They do not report how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers or random-number table) or how allocation was concealed until enrolment. Thus, the relevant RoB 2 signalling questions on random sequence generation and allocation concealment cannot be answered affirmatively.  
   
   The group sizes were broadly compatible with the intended allocation (direct, *n* = 18; immediately available, *n* = 16; distant, *n* = 19), and Table 1 shows no striking baseline imbalance in age, training level, ICU experience, or confidence. However, the lack of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns** under RoB 2 rather than low risk of bias.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of **assignment** to level of supervision.

   b. **Rationale:** Residents, fellows, and other personnel could not realistically be blinded to the supervision strategy, since the intervention was defined by the supervisor’s physical proximity: direct, immediately available, or distant supervision. The report states that direct supervision involved the “**supervisor in the simulation room with the resident from the beginning of the scenario**,” whereas distant supervision involved a supervisor “**only available on the phone for the duration of the scenario**.”

   However, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding causes bias only if it produces deviations from the intended intervention that arise because of the trial context and are likely to affect the outcome. The differing fellow involvement across groups was intrinsic to, and intended by, the allocated supervision strategy—not a deviation from it. For example, residents in the immediately available group differed in whether they called the supervisor and whether the supervisor came to the bedside; this appears to represent the intended operationalization of “immediately available” supervision rather than protocol-inconsistent contamination.

   The analysis compared participants according to their randomized supervision group using one-way ANOVA: “**we conducted a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the team checklist scores of the supervised scenario with the level of clinical supervision … as the independent variable.**” All 53 randomized residents appear in the three analysed groups (18 + 16 + 19 = 53). Therefore, the reported analysis is consistent with estimating the effect of assignment to supervision strategy.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data for the team checklist score appear to have been available for all randomized residents. The study reports recruitment of “**53 residents**,” and Table 2 reports team checklist scores for all randomized groups: direct (*n* = 18), immediately available (*n* = 16), and distant (*n* = 19), totalling 53 participants.

   The report does not describe withdrawals, exclusions, missing checklist scores, or differential availability of the supervised-scenario outcome. The simulation sessions were conducted in one afternoon per resident, and all scenario performances were video-recorded. Consequently, there is no indication that missing data could have materially affected the reported comparison.

4. **measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was a team checklist score based on the number of clinical tasks completed during a simulation scenario. The authors state: “**A checklist task was considered done if performed by any member of the team … and was reported as a percentage of the total number of items on the checklist.**” The clinical tasks and key actions were predetermined for each scenario, which supports reasonably structured and comparable measurement across groups.

   Nevertheless, several issues preclude a low-risk judgement. First, the authors acknowledge that the scenario-specific checklists were non-validated: “**We recognize the limitations associated with the use of a nonvalidated assessment tool, such as our scenario-specific checklists to evaluate team and resident performance.**” Although they considered the checklists to have face and content validity, they “**did not assess [their] construct validity**.”

   Second, the report does not establish that the assessors of the supervised-scenario team checklist score were blinded to assigned supervision level. Blinding would be difficult from video recordings because direct, immediately available, and distant supervision are visibly or audibly distinguishable. Although checklist completion is more objective than a global subjective rating, coding whether actions or verbal orders fulfilled checklist items can still involve some judgement. The report describes independent raters and adjudication for videotaped performance, but does not clearly state that the team-checklist outcome in the supervised scenario was rated blinded to supervision allocation. There is no direct evidence that knowledge of allocation influenced ratings, so **some concerns**, rather than high risk, is appropriate.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication specifies the team checklist score as an outcome for patient care during supervised scenarios and reports numerical results for all three groups. It also reports other pre-described outcomes, including duration of abnormal vital signs and achievement of definitive management. This consistency reduces concern that only a favourable supervision comparison was reported.

   However, low risk of bias requires confirmation that the reported team checklist-score analysis followed a sufficiently detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The available report does not provide a pre-specified statistical analysis plan, and it does not state that the checklist-score analysis, choice of summary metric, handling of alternative analyses, or comparison strategy were finalized before outcome data were examined.

   In particular, the article reports several patient-care outcomes and several possible analyses, while the report itself notes limitations in the nonvalidated scenario-specific checklists. In the absence of a prospectively finalized, sufficiently detailed analysis plan or clear registry/protocol information confirming the exact outcome measurement and analysis, it cannot be excluded that the reported numerical checklist-score result was selected from multiple possible measures or analyses. Therefore, the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is **some concerns**.
