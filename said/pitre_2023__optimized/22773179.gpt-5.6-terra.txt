**Outcome assessed:** postoperative visual acuity at 3 months (Figure 4), comparing aspheric ablation (ASA) versus tissue-saving/non-aspheric ablation (TSA), for the **effect of assignment to intervention**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because random sequence generation was adequately described, but concealment of the allocation sequence was not reported.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The study was described as a “prospective randomized paired-eye controlled study” in which “one eye was treated with [ASA] and the other eye was treated with [TSA], assigned at random.”  
   - The randomization method was stated: “assigned at random using Microsoft Excel’s random number function.” This is a random component and supports an appropriate allocation sequence.  
   - However, there is no information on allocation concealment—for example, whether the person enrolling patients and planning surgery could foresee which eye would receive ASA or TSA before allocation.  
   - No material baseline imbalance is apparent for the reported refractive characteristics: mean preoperative spherical equivalent was −2.43 D in ASA and −2.54 D in TSA (*p*=0.87). Also, all enrolled eyes had preoperative CDVA of at least 20/20 by eligibility criteria. These data do not suggest a problem with the randomization process.  
   - Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment, despite no evident baseline imbalance, leads to **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to ASA versus TSA at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The assigned intervention was clearly specified: “one eye was treated with the MEL80 standard aspheric profile (ASA) and the other eye was treated with the MEL80 non-aspheric profile (TSA).” All treatments used the same LASIK approach, 6-mm optical zone, and target postoperative refraction of plano.  
   - Personnel delivering the surgery were likely aware of the selected ablation profile, because this is inherent to programming and delivering the laser treatment. However, there is no reported evidence that knowledge of assignment led to non-protocol co-interventions, differential surgical management, or failure to implement either assigned profile.  
   - The protocolized treatment approach makes important deviations arising specifically from the trial context unlikely.  
   - The visual-acuity result appears to include the randomized eyes as allocated. Figure 4 reports results for all 30 eyes at 3 months, with no indication that eyes were analysed according to treatment received rather than randomized allocation, or that eligible eyes were excluded post-randomization.  
   - Therefore, there is no evidence that deviations from the assigned profiles, or the analysis approach, biased the visual-acuity comparison estimating the effect of assignment.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The trial enrolled 15 patients (30 eyes), and the visual-acuity figure explicitly states that it includes “data for 30 eyes at 3 months postop.”  
   - Thus, postoperative visual-acuity data appear available for all randomized eyes at the prespecified 3-month assessment.  
   - The paper reports that “100% of eyes were 20/25 or better” in both groups. There is no report of loss to follow-up, missing visual-acuity measurements, or exclusions from the visual-acuity analysis.  
   - Since outcome data were apparently available for all randomized eyes, missing data could not have materially biased the visual-acuity result.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - Visual acuity was measured as uncorrected distance visual acuity and corrected distance visual acuity before and 3 months after surgery. Visual acuity is a routinely measured, relatively objective clinical outcome when assessed with standardized acuity charts.  
   - The same outcome assessment appears to have been used in both intervention groups at the same postoperative time point. There is no indication that ascertainment procedures, timing, or thresholds differed between ASA- and TSA-treated eyes.  
   - The report does not explicitly state whether visual-acuity assessors were masked to the ablation profile. Nevertheless, visual-acuity measurement involves limited assessor judgement compared with subjective patient-reported outcomes, and there is no indication that knowledge of the assigned laser profile influenced testing or recording.  
   - Therefore, any lack of reported assessor masking is unlikely to have materially biased this outcome.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The report presents postoperative visual-acuity findings in Figure 4, stating that “100% of eyes were 20/25 or better.” However, no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied material.  
   - The article describes several visual and refractive outcomes—uncorrected distance visual acuity, CDVA, spherical equivalent refraction, defocus equivalent refraction, safety, and stability—without clearly identifying a pre-specified primary visual-acuity outcome, a single preferred visual-acuity metric, or a pre-specified analysis strategy for the Figure 4 result.  
   - The supplied materials do not contain the trial-registration record referenced in the request; therefore, it is not possible to verify whether the 3-month visual-acuity analysis, measure, and analysis method were prespecified before unblinded outcome data were available.  
   - Because visual acuity could be reported using multiple eligible measures (e.g., UDVA or CDVA, categorical thresholds, lines gained/lost, or different follow-up time points) and potentially using different analyses, selective choice of the reported result cannot be excluded.  
   - There is no direct evidence that the result was selected based on its statistical significance or direction; hence this is **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.
