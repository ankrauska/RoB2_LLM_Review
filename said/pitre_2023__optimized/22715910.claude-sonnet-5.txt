# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Inhaler Use Technique
## Effect of Interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias due to Randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The publication states only that "Eligible subjects were randomly assigned to one of the three experimental groups... and one comparison group," with no description of the method used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated randomization, block size) and no description of any allocation concealment mechanism (e.g., central randomization, sealed envelopes). The trial registry record likewise provides no further methodological detail on sequence generation/concealment ("allocation": "RANDOMIZED" only). Therefore, signalling questions 1.1 and 1.2 must be answered "No information."

However, there is reassuring evidence regarding balance: "The initial pretest analyses showed no statistically significant differences among the four study groups and also between gender and ethnic groups in relation to the outcomes of interest," and Table 1 shows reasonably even numbers randomized to each of the 4 groups (22, 21, 20, 22) and balanced ethnicity within groups. This is compatible with successful randomization and no evidence of a subverted process.

Per the RoB 2 algorithm, "No information" on sequence generation and concealment, combined with no evidence of baseline imbalance, maps to a domain-level judgement of **Some concerns** (not "Low," because adequate concealment cannot be confirmed; not "High," because there is no indication of a problem with randomization).

---

## 2. Deviations from the Intended Intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** By the nature of this behavioral/educational trial, neither participants nor the community facilitators who delivered the intervention could be blinded to group assignment — participants necessarily knew whether they watched a video (and which one) or read a pamphlet, and the same bilingual facilitators who "facilitated the intervention" also interacted with participants afterward. Thus, signalling questions 2.1 and 2.2 (awareness of assigned intervention) are answered "Yes."

There is no explicit reporting of non-protocol interventions or contamination between arms (e.g., participants in the pamphlet group being inadvertently shown a video, or vice versa), and no indication that trial-context-driven deviations (e.g., recruitment/engagement effects differentially motivating one arm) occurred. The article states: "Because participants in the video and print groups responded to the same pictorial questionnaire during the in-person interviews (and only differed in what materials they viewed or read during the post-test interview)," suggesting a controlled, single-exposure design with low potential for cross-contamination.

Regarding analysis, the study appears to have analyzed participants according to their randomized group (ANOVA/ANCOVA by "study group"), consistent with an intention-to-treat approach modified for the participants who completed the study (see missing data domain below); this would satisfy the definition of an acceptable modified ITT analysis for this domain.

Because participants/personnel were unblinded (as is unavoidable for this intervention type) but there is no clear evidence that trial-context-driven deviations affected the outcome, and because no clear description of deviations or their balance across groups is provided, the domain is judged **Some concerns** rather than "Low" (insufficient information to fully rule out awareness-related differential behavior, e.g., differential attention or coaching by facilitators aware of group assignment) or "High" (no evidence of major unbalanced deviations).

---

## 3. Missing Outcome Data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** Of 92 randomized participants, "85 completed the study," with Figure 1 noting "3 were excluded and 4 discontinued intervention from the educational intervention group" — approximately 7.6% missing outcome data overall for the 3-month follow-up used in this analysis. This proportion is relatively small, and the paper does not report a differential pattern of missingness by intervention group; however, the actual per-group breakdown of the 7 lost participants and the specific reasons for exclusion/discontinuation are **not reported**, so it is not possible to confirm whether missingness in the "Inhaler Use Technique" outcome was related to its true value (e.g., poorer performers dropping out or being excluded differentially by arm) or evenly distributed and non-informative.

The residual degrees of freedom reported for the ANCOVA models (e.g., df = 1, 71 for the time effect) suggest fewer than the full 92 participants were included in the model, consistent with a complete-case analysis rather than a formal ITT analysis with imputation. No sensitivity analyses addressing the impact of the missing outcome data were reported.

Given the modest amount of missing data but lack of detail on reasons/distribution across groups, this domain is judged **Some concerns** rather than "Low."

---

## 4. Measurement of the Outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Inhaler use technique was assessed using "a standardized nine-step observational checklist, which was used by the facilitator to record proper use of different inhalers." This is an **observer-reported outcome involving some judgement** (scoring correctness of each of nine technique steps), which is generally at risk of bias when the assessor is not blinded to intervention assignment.

The same "bilingual and bicultural experienced moderators" who delivered/facilitated the intervention (showing the videos or pamphlet) also conducted the follow-up interviews and, presumably, scored the inhaler-technique checklist, meaning the outcome assessors were **not blinded** to intervention allocation (signalling question 4.3 = Yes). While the article states facilitators "were not aware of the study hypothesis," this does not equate to blinding to which specific intervention (video vs. pamphlet, or which video) a given participant received, since they personally administered that material.

Because scoring of technique steps involves subjective judgement, and facilitators had direct knowledge of which materials a participant received (and plausibly some expectation/investment in observing improvement, especially given they helped develop and deliver the videos), it is plausible that assessment of inhaler technique was influenced by knowledge of intervention received (signalling questions 4.4 = Yes, 4.5 = Likely). The differential effects reported ("Chinese patients showed significantly greater improvements compared to Punjabi patients" and specific group-level differences in Table 3) could reflect true effects but could also be partly attributable to unblinded, judgement-based scoring by facilitators invested in the intervention's success.

This combination — unblinded assessors, judgement-based outcome, and plausible influence — meets the criteria for **High risk of bias** in this domain.

---

## 5. Selective Reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The ClinicalTrials.gov registration (NCT01474928) lists the **primary outcome as "medication adherence"** measured at "one year," and lists secondary outcomes as "Patients' beliefs, perceptions, and attitudes toward asthma self-management" (6 months) and "Educational materials and measurement tools development" (3 months). **"Inhaler use technique" is not explicitly pre-specified as a named primary or secondary outcome** in the registry, nor is a 1-year adherence outcome reported in the publication (the paper reports pretest, 3-month, and a 6-month telephone follow-up only, and the abstract/results focus heavily on inhaler technique and knowledge outcomes rather than the registered 1-year adherence outcome).

Additionally, the analysis strategy described ("Initially, we ran ANOVA... then... ANCOVA... Then interaction terms were added to see if the effect of the interventions differed across time, educational level, age, ethnic group, and gender group") indicates a large number of possible subgroup/covariate analyses were conducted. The results tables (Table 2/3) report numerous stratified comparisons (by time, group, ethnicity, age, gender, education) for inhaler technique, with some significant and some non-significant results selectively highlighted in the text (e.g., emphasis on female and Chinese patients' improvements). Without a pre-specified statistical analysis plan or protocol detailing which comparisons were planned a priori, it is unclear whether the reported comparisons represent the full set of analyses conducted or a selected subset based on the results obtained.

Given the mismatch between the registered primary/secondary outcomes and the outcome/timeframes actually emphasized in the publication, combined with the multiplicity of subgroup analyses without a clearly documented a priori analysis plan, this domain is judged **Some concerns** (insufficient information to confirm pre-specification, but no definitive evidence of results-based selection sufficient to warrant "High risk").
