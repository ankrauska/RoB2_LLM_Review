# Risk of Bias Assessment (RoB 2.0)
## Outcome: SBGM frequency and HbA1c
## Effect of Interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to lack of information on allocation concealment, combined with a plausible risk that the unblinded, resource-based intervention (free vs. purchased strips) could not itself compromise sequence generation, but concealment cannot be confirmed.

**b. Rationale:** The trial states: "The patients were stratified in three categories of income... and they were randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion to two groups of 31 patients each." This indicates a random component was used and stratification by income was employed, supporting "Probably yes" for random sequence generation. However, there is no description of the method of allocation concealment (e.g., sealed envelopes, central randomization) — no information is provided on how the sequence was concealed from those enrolling patients. Baseline characteristics (Table 1) show good balance across groups (age, gender, education, income, diabetes type/duration, HbA1c, insulin dose/injections, SBGM frequency), consistent with adequate randomization and no evidence of baseline imbalance suggesting problems with randomization. Because allocation concealment is not described (no information), but randomization appears otherwise sound with balanced baseline characteristics, this domain is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low," per the algorithm (no information on concealment + baseline balance compatible with chance = Some concerns).

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias due to lack of blinding of participants and personnel, with deviations from intended intervention plausibly arising from the trial context (differential access to test strips) that directly affected the outcomes of interest (SBGM frequency and HbA1c), and these deviations were clearly unbalanced between groups, coupled with a substantial differential dropout that was not addressed through an appropriate ITT analysis for HbA1c/SBGM frequency in the primary results presented.

**b. Rationale:** 
- Participants were not blinded to their intervention group: the NC group received free strips ("100 strips per month") while the C group had to purchase strips ("instructed to purchase additional reagent strips as needed"). This is an inherent, unblindable difference in intervention delivery.
- This directly constitutes a deviation from equal intended intervention context ("trial context") that is expected to influence the very outcome being measured (SBGM frequency, and consequently HbA1c) — patients' awareness of cost/access to strips is intrinsically linked to their behavior (testing frequency), which is the primary outcome. This is a core feature of the intervention itself, but the differential access (unblinded) creates behavioral consequences (e.g., patients in the C group who found cost "too expensive" preferentially dropped out): "the higher dropout rate (~50%) among patients not given free glucometer strips."
- The differential and substantial dropout (**19% in NC vs. 48% in C group**) suggests systematic, non-random deviations from intended follow-up that are related to the intervention/trial context, likely affecting outcome ascertainment and potentially biasing the assignment effect.
- Regarding analysis: the report states, "Efficacy data from the intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward, were analysed by General Linear Model with baseline values as co-variate." LOCF is a single-imputation method that is generally considered an inappropriate/inadequate method to preserve unbiased ITT estimates in the presence of substantial and differential dropout (per RoB2 guidance section 6.1.6, LOCF "should be considered as at low risk of bias only when there is clear justification"), and no such justification is provided.
- Given the magnitude of differential dropout (48% vs 19%) and reliance on LOCF without justification, together with unblinded, context-driven deviations affecting the primary outcome, this domain is judged **High risk of bias**.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias due to substantial and differential missing outcome data between groups, with no adequate sensitivity analysis, and plausible relationship between missingness and the true outcome value (glycaemic control).

**b. Rationale:** 
- Outcome data were not available for all or nearly all participants: "At the end of the study, there were 25 patients remaining in the NC group and 16 patients in the C group, i.e. a dropout rate of 19% in the NC group and 48% in the C group." This is a large and clearly differential proportion of missing data (signalling question 3.1 = No).
- The only compensation described is LOCF-based ITT analysis, which the RoB2 guidance explicitly does not consider sufficient evidence that missing data did not bias the result unless clear justification is given (none provided here) (signalling question 3.2 = No).
- Missingness plausibly depends on the true value of the outcome: the paper states, "we cannot exclude the possibility that patients with intrinsically poorer glycaemic control became discouraged and therefore did less testing or dropped out of the study altogether." Also, "patients in the C group who found the cost of the reagent strips to be problematic may well have dropped out of the study" — directly linking reason for dropout (cost, motivation, poor control) to the outcome itself (signalling question 3.3 = Yes).
- The differing dropout rates between groups (19% vs. 48%), combined with a plausible causal link between poor glycaemic control/motivation and dropout, indicates it is likely that missingness in the outcome depended on its true value and differed by intervention group (signalling question 3.4 = Yes).
- This pattern (large differential missingness, plausible outcome-related reasons, inadequate handling) meets the criteria for **High risk of bias**.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, primarily due to lack of blinding of outcome assessors for SBGM frequency (a device-recorded but behaviorally-driven outcome) and potential differential ascertainment/motivation effects, though objective measurement methods reduce the risk somewhat for HbA1c.

**b. Rationale:** 
- The method of measuring the outcome was not inherently inappropriate: HbA1c was measured with a standardized clinical assay ("HbA1c was measured using the DCA 2000 Analyser"), and SBGM frequency was measured objectively via "downloading the glucometer memory" rather than by patient self-report, reducing potential for reporting bias in the frequency count itself (signalling question 4.1 = No).
- Measurement/ascertainment method itself did not differ between groups — same glucometer model, same HbA1c assay, same visit schedule ("Both groups of patients were followed longitudinally at 2-monthly intervals") (signalling question 4.2 = No).
- However, the outcome assessor for HbA1c and blood glucose could be considered the objective assay/nurse recording data, and the paper states, "The research nurse did not provide any feedback to the patient concerning glucometer readings, random blood glucose readings or HbA1c determinations," and "the nurse logged the results by patient number and no analysis of the data occurred until all the patients had completed the study" — supporting blinding of the research nurse/assessor to the ongoing comparative results (signalling question 4.3 = Probably No for the objective outcomes).
- Nonetheless, patients themselves were not blinded to their own glucometer readings and were aware of their allocation (free vs. purchased strips), and SBGM frequency is a behavioral outcome directly self-determined by participants who were unblinded — this raises the possibility that motivation/awareness of group assignment could have influenced testing behavior (which feeds into both SBGM frequency and downstream HbA1c via insulin adjustment behavior). This is analogous to a "participant-reported/participant-driven" outcome domain, which per guidance is judged to have at least "Some concerns" when participants are unblinded.
- Given the objective recording methods but unblinded participant behavior potentially influencing the outcome trajectory, this domain is judged as **Some concerns**.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to lack of accessible trial registration/pre-specified statistical analysis plan to confirm that reported outcomes and analyses (SBGM frequency and HbA1c) were not selected based on results.

**b. Rationale:** 
- No trial registry entry or published protocol/statistical analysis plan is referenced or cited in the article to confirm that the analyses of SBGM frequency and HbA1c presented (e.g., specific time points: "first 4 months" for SBGM frequency, "6 months" for HbA1c) were pre-specified before unblinded data were available (signalling question 5.1 = No information).
- The study does report a formal sample size calculation a priori using SBGM frequency and HbA1c as primary parameters of interest, which is reassuring that these were intended primary outcomes rather than selected post hoc from many measured outcomes. However, specific reporting of results at selected time points (e.g., emphasis on the "first 4 months" difference in SBGM frequency, and "6 months" and onward for HbA1c) without a pre-specified analysis time point raises some question of whether these specific windows were selected because they showed the most favourable/significant differences (signalling questions 5.2/5.3 = No information).
- Given the absence of a protocol or registry to verify pre-specification of analysis time points and methods, but with clear evidence that the two outcomes of interest were intended a priori (via sample size calculation) rather than cherry-picked outcome domains, the overall judgement is **Some concerns** (not "High," as there is no clear evidence of results-based selection, only absence of verifiable pre-specification).

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **High risk of bias**

Per RoB 2.0 guidance, a judgement of "High risk of bias" in any single domain (here, both "Deviations from intended intervention" and "Missing outcome data") results in an overall "High risk of bias" judgement for this outcome (SBGM frequency and HbA1c), primarily driven by substantial, differential dropout (19% vs. 48%) plausibly related to the true outcome value and group assignment, combined with reliance on an unjustified single-imputation (LOCF) approach in the ITT analysis.
