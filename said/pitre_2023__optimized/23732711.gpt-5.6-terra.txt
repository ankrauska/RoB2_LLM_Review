1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence was random and measured baseline characteristics were broadly balanced, but allocation concealment was not described sufficiently to establish that enrolment personnel could not foresee assignments.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that “one of our study investigators, who had no knowledge of the participants, randomly assigned them by using a computer-generated randomized list.” This supports a random sequence (RoB 2 signalling question 1.1: **Yes**). However, it does not state who enrolled participants, who held the list, or whether allocation was concealed until after a participant had been irreversibly enrolled (question 1.2: **No information**). The statement that the investigator “had no knowledge of the participants” does not itself demonstrate allocation concealment.

   Baseline characteristics were generally similar between daily and discretionary sunscreen groups, including sex, age, skin phenotype, sun-exposure history, smoking, previous sunscreen use, and clinical neck photoaging. The article also states that “there were no differences in phenotype, sun exposure, or pretrial sunscreen use between the intervention and control groups at baseline.” Thus, there is no indication of an important problem with randomization from baseline imbalance (question 1.3: **No**). Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns** despite an apparently random sequence and balanced baseline data.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to daily versus discretionary sunscreen use at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew whether they had been asked to apply sunscreen daily or use it at their discretion; similarly, people delivering the intervention were likely aware of allocation (questions 2.1 and 2.2: **Yes/Probably yes**). However, for the assignment effect, awareness alone does not imply bias. RoB 2 requires evidence that deviations inconsistent with the protocol arose because of the trial context, affected photoaging, and were imbalanced between groups.

   There is no evidence of such trial-context deviations. Reported sun exposure during the trial was similar: “78% of the daily sunscreen group and 76% of the discretionary sunscreen group spent ≥50% of weekend time outdoors” and other sun-protection practices were also similar: “54%” versus “53%” usually sought shade, and “63%” versus “67%” usually wore a hat. Although adherence to the assigned sunscreen strategy differed, as intended—77% of daily-use participants applied sunscreen at least 3–4 days weekly compared with 33% of discretionary users—non-adherence or imperfect adherence that could occur outside the trial context does not bias the effect of assignment.

   The analysis was explicitly according to randomized assignment: “Data were analyzed according to treatment as randomly allocated.” The generalized estimating-equation analysis retained all available repeated outcome measurements, rather than analyzing participants according to sunscreen actually used or restricting the analysis to adherent participants. This is appropriate for the effect of assignment (question 2.6: **Yes**). Therefore, risk of bias from deviations from intended interventions is **low**.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because outcome data were missing for a substantial proportion of randomized/eligible participants, although missingness did not appear related to baseline microtopography grade and sensitivity analyses gave consistent results.

   b. **Rationale:** The primary outcome required assessment of change in microtopography between 1992 and 1996. Of 886 eligible participants with at least one good-quality skin replica, only 604 had replicas at both time points; 213 contributed a 1992 replica only and 69 a 1996 replica only. Thus, approximately one third contributed only one outcome measurement, and outcome data were not available for nearly all participants (question 3.1: **No**).

   The investigators reported that participants with only one replica were “more likely to have severe photoaging of the neck and 2 to 5 total sunburns,” which indicates that missingness was related to some prognostic characteristics. However, “having a missing microtopography grade in 1996 was not associated with baseline microtopography grade,” and the authors state that treatment allocation was not associated with missing grades. They adjusted for factors associated with incomplete data and performed complete-case and multiple-imputation analyses: results “were consistent with results using only those participants with complete photoaging grades” and with “results incorporating multiple imputations of missing data.”

   These findings reduce concern, but do not definitively demonstrate that missingness was unrelated to the unobserved 1996 photoaging outcome. The reported multiple imputation and complete-case analyses are not sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-not-at-random assumptions. Given the substantial amount of missing outcome information, but lack of evidence that missingness was likely dependent on the true unobserved outcome differently by treatment group, this is best judged as **some concerns**, rather than high risk.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Photoaging was assessed using standardized silicone replicas from the same anatomical site—the back of the left hand—at baseline and follow-up. The outcome method was suitable for the outcome domain: the report notes that skin-surface microtopography grading had previously been shown to be a valid measure of photoaging. The grading scale had good reported reliability, with intra- and intergrader weighted kappa statistics of 0.81 and 0.86, respectively (question 4.1: **No**).

   The outcome was collected in the same way in both sunscreen groups, with no indication that the frequency, timing, or method of replica collection differed by intervention group (question 4.2: **No**). Crucially, “experienced assessors who were unaware of treatment allocation graded replicas.” Thus, outcome assessors were blinded to the assigned intervention (question 4.3: **No**).

   Although grading skin microtopography involves some judgement, assessor blinding protects against differential assessment related to sunscreen allocation. The article further notes that microtopography measurement error “seemed to be nondifferential with respect to treatment groups, particularly for microtopography, because assessors were blinded to allocations.” Therefore, measurement bias is at **low risk**.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication clearly identifies photoaging change from 1992 to 1996 as the primary outcome and reports the main daily-versus-discretionary sunscreen result, including the relative odds ratio of 0.76 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.98). It also reports alternative analyses, including unadjusted and adjusted models, complete-case analysis, and multiple-imputation analysis. There is consequently no clear evidence that the reported sunscreen result was selected from multiple outcome measures or analyses because it was statistically favourable (questions 5.2 and 5.3: **Probably no**).

   However, the available registry record is retrospective relative to this trial: the trial was conducted from 1992 to 1996, whereas the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry identifier is ACTRN12610000086066. It therefore cannot demonstrate that a detailed analysis plan for this specific photoaging result was finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. The publication refers to an earlier design/protocol paper, but the available information does not establish a pre-specified analysis plan defining the exact outcome metric, covariate adjustment, and handling of missing data used for this result (question 5.1: **No information/Probably no**).

   In the absence of a prospectively finalized, sufficiently detailed analysis plan, RoB 2 assigns **some concerns** for possible selective reporting, even though there is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected on the basis of its statistical significance or direction.
