**Outcome assessed:** Pain measured using the visual analogue scale (VAS), principally the improvement from baseline to discharge after 25 treatment sessions (active ultrasound plus usual physiotherapy versus sham ultrasound plus the same usual physiotherapy).

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns arise because allocation concealment was insufficiently described, despite reported sex-stratified randomization and sealed envelopes.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The authors report that “*the distribution of subjects to the two treatment groups was performed using sex-stratified randomization, and the allocation sequence was blinded through the use of sealed envelopes*.” This supports use of a random allocation process, although the description “simple random selection” is inconsistent or incomplete given the stated sex stratification. More importantly, the report does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, and opened only after irrevocable enrolment. Thus, allocation concealment cannot confidently be judged adequate under RoB 2.  
   
   Baseline VAS values were broadly similar: median VAS was 7.0 (IQR 1.9) in the control group and 7.4 (IQR 2.3) in the experimental group (*p* = 0.421). Sex distribution was identical (10 men and 17 women in each group), and there is no clear baseline imbalance indicating a failed randomization process. Nevertheless, uncertainty about concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The study states that patients and the assessor were blinded: “*our work is an analytical, prospective, experimental study, with blinding of patients and blind assessor*.” The control group received sham ultrasound, described as the same intervention sequence except that “*the ultrasound was off (sham US)*.” Both groups otherwise received the same massage, exercise and microwave protocols, and both discontinued ultrasound after the first 10 sessions.  
   
   The physiotherapist administering the intervention could not be blinded, as acknowledged by the authors: “*The blinding of the physiotherapist who applied the treatment was not possible*.” However, there is no indication that this knowledge led to non-protocol co-interventions, differential implementation of the shared treatment components, or other deviations arising from the trial context that would affect VAS pain.  
   
   The authors state that “*The analysis was done by intention to treat*,” and the flowchart/report indicate that all 54 randomized participants completed the study: “*During the study no patient was lost or dropped out.*” Therefore, participants appear to have been analysed in their randomized groups, appropriate for the effect of assignment.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   
   f. **Rationale:** The report explicitly states: “*During the study no patient was lost or dropped out.*” The flowchart likewise shows 27 participants randomized to each group and progression through final assessment at discharge. Thus, VAS data appear available for all 54 randomized participants at the relevant assessment points. There is no evidence of differential attrition, missing VAS values, or imputation procedures. Missing outcome data therefore could not plausibly have materially affected the VAS comparison.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** VAS is an appropriate and standard patient-reported measure for pain. Pain was assessed using “*the VAS and algometer*,” with VAS measured before the first, sixth, eleventh and twenty-fifth sessions. The same measurement schedule and methods were used in both groups.  
   
   For VAS pain, the participant is the outcome assessor. Patients were reported to be blinded and the control intervention was sham ultrasound. Therefore, the participant-reported VAS outcome was assessed without known awareness of whether active or placebo ultrasound was received. Although the treating physiotherapist was not blinded, this does not by itself establish that VAS reporting was differentially influenced. There is no evidence that outcome measurement or ascertainment differed between groups.  

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** No dated, sufficiently detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied study report. The article describes multiple VAS assessment time points—baseline, after five sessions, after 10 sessions, and at discharge: “*before starting the 1st (pre-test), 6th (post-test 5) and 11th (post-test 10) sessions and on completing the 25th session (post-test discharge)*.” It also evaluates pain using both VAS and pressure algometry.  
   
   The report presents VAS improvement results after 10 sessions and at discharge, but does not fully report the VAS result after five sessions. Moreover, multiple plausible analyses were available, including final values versus improvement-from-baseline values and analyses at several follow-up points. The reported analysis uses “improvement indices.”  
   
   There is insufficient evidence to conclude that the discharge VAS result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable to active ultrasound; therefore, high risk of bias is not justified. However, in the absence of accessible, prospectively finalized analysis intentions or a detailed trial-registration record confirming the specified VAS time point and analysis, selective reporting cannot be excluded.
