1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The trial reported an appropriate random sequence and adequate allocation concealment. Randomization was performed independently using a computer-generated sequence with randomly permuted blocks of varying sizes, reducing predictability of allocation. Allocation was concealed with sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes.

   The authors stated: “**Randomization was done by a person who was not involved in the trial**” and “**Random sequence generation was done with a computer-generated list of random numbers**.” They further reported “**random permuted blocks with randomized order of block sizes**” of 6 and 4, and that “**allocation concealment was achieved with sequentially numbered and sealed opaque envelopes**.”

   Baseline characteristics were reported for all randomized participants and were broadly comparable between groups. Although some numerical differences were present (e.g., mandibular-plane measures), there is no clear evidence of an imbalance implausible by chance in this small trial (16 participants per group), nor evidence that the randomization process was compromised.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Patients and treating clinicians could not be blinded because the interventions were visibly different: FFRD alone versus FFRD with mini-implant anchorage. The authors explicitly acknowledged that “**blinding of the clinicians and patients to the interventions in each group was impossible**.”

   However, for the assignment-to-intervention effect, lack of blinding only introduces bias in this domain if it led to deviations from the intended interventions that arose because of the trial context, were unbalanced between groups, and were likely to affect patient acceptance. The report provides no evidence of such deviations. Both groups underwent the planned appliance treatment, follow-up procedures were similar, and the appliances were removed at the predefined clinical endpoint of edge-to-edge incisor relationship.

   Importantly, all 32 randomized participants remained in their allocated groups and were included in the analysis: “**No patients dropped out during the trial**” and “**all the patients were included in the analysis**.” The study describes its primary analysis as intention-to-treat, and because no participant dropped out, the analysed sample corresponded to the randomized sample. Thus, there is no evidence that an inappropriate analysis or trial-context-related departures from protocol biased the estimated effect of assignment.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome data for patient acceptance were available for every randomized participant. The CONSORT flow information indicates that 16 participants were randomized to each group, none were lost to follow-up or discontinued intervention, and all 32 were analysed.

   The authors reported: “**The 32 surveys were retrieved from the patients; there were no dropouts**.” They also stated that “**all questions were answered correctly by the patients and their parents**” and that there were “**no missing answers**.”

   Therefore, missing data could not have materially biased the patient-acceptance result.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Patient acceptance was measured using self-completed questionnaires concerning satisfaction, noticeability, functional interference, pain, swelling, oral hygiene, and willingness to recommend the appliance. This is a participant-reported, subjective outcome. The participants necessarily knew which appliance configuration they received, since mini-implants were visibly and physically distinct from FFRD treatment alone.

   Although the authors described blinding of “outcome assessment,” this appears to refer to coding, extraction, and statistical analysis rather than blinding of the actual outcome assessors. For a patient-reported acceptance outcome, the patient is the outcome assessor. The report confirms that patients were unblinded: “**Blinding of the clinicians and patients to the interventions in each group was impossible**.”

   The outcome was also collected after treatment completion, when participants were aware of the intervention received and of their treatment result: “**The patients of both groups were asked to fill out assessment questionnaires … on the day of removal of the FFRD**.” Knowledge of receiving mini-implant anchorage versus conventional FFRD could plausibly influence perceptions of discomfort, inconvenience, appearance, satisfaction, and willingness to recommend the appliance. These are precisely the subjective domains most susceptible to expectation or preference effects.

   The questionnaire method itself was applied similarly in both groups and was not inherently unsuitable. Nevertheless, because the unblinded participants assessed a highly subjective outcome, and their knowledge of treatment was likely to influence their responses, there is a high risk of bias in measurement of patient acceptance.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The article explicitly reports that the trial was not registered and that no protocol was published before commencement: “**This trial was not registered. Protocol: The protocol was not published before trial commencement.**” Consequently, there is no prospectively date-stamped protocol or statistical analysis plan against which to verify that the reported patient-acceptance result, questionnaire items, outcome definitions, timing, and analysis methods were specified before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The publication reports the questionnaire outcomes and states that there were no outcome changes after trial commencement. It also provides results for the individual questionnaire domains and the ridit analyses. Thus, there is no direct evidence that the reported patient-acceptance result was selected from multiple measurements or analyses based on the observed findings.

   However, patient acceptance was assessed through numerous questionnaire items and could potentially have been summarized or analysed in different ways (individual items, functional-interference domains, satisfaction, categorical response distributions, mean scores, or ridit analyses). In the absence of a pre-specified and prospectively available analysis plan, selective selection of the reported result cannot be excluded. Under RoB 2, this warrants **some concerns**, rather than high risk, because there is no affirmative evidence of result-based selection.
