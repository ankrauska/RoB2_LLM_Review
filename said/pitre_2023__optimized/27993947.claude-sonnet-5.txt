# Risk of Bias Assessment: MI-GENES Study - Shared Decision Making Outcome

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The allocation sequence was random and adequately concealed. The trial states: "Randomization was performed by means of a computer-generated random sequence with stratification for age, gender, and positive family history for CHD using the Pocock and Simon method." Sequence generation and study arm assignment were performed by an investigator (HJ) independent of the recruitment process ("One of the investigators (HJ) generated the random allocation sequence and study arm assignment using the computer software described earlier"), and participants were randomized only after completing the first visit and scheduling the second, reducing risk of selective enrollment based on foreknowledge of allocation. Baseline characteristics (Table 1) were well balanced between groups (age p=0.91, gender p=0.78, smoking p=0.19, family history p=0.43, and other lipid/BP measures all p>0.4), with no evidence of imbalance suggestive of a problem with the randomization process.

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** This was an open-label (unblinded) trial by design ("maskingInfo": "NONE") — patients, genetic counselors, and physicians were all aware of group assignment, since disclosure of the genetic risk score itself was the intervention being tested. However, for the SDM outcome, there is no evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose *because of the trial context* in a manner likely to bias the SDM outcome. The disclosure procedure was standardized via the decision aid and delivered by protocol to all participants ("Disclosure of risk in both arms was facilitated by the use of a modified version of the Statin Choice decision aid"), and script language was used consistently ("CHD risk was disclosed using scripted language as follows..."). There is no indication of non-protocol interventions, contamination, or differential implementation between arms that would affect the SDM outcome measurement itself. The analysis appears to include all randomized and consented participants who completed the second visit (206/207 for SDM), consistent with an appropriate (ITT-like) analysis for this outcome.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Outcome data were available for nearly all randomized participants. Of 207 randomized participants, the SDM survey was completed by 206 (only 1 missing), and the physician visit satisfaction survey was completed by all 207. The trial states: "The SDM survey was completed by 206 study participants, and the physician visit satisfaction survey was completed by all study participants." This level of completeness (>99%) is far too small to have made an important difference to the estimated effect, regardless of the reason for the single missing data point. There is no differential loss between arms (103/103 and 103/104 roughly, per Tables 2 and 3), and reasons for any missingness are not suggestive of dependence on the true SDM value.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The method of measuring the outcome was not inherently inappropriate — a validated instrument (SDM-Q) was used, supplemented by an independent quantitative video analysis (OPTION5), which is a strength. Measurement/ascertainment methods were identical between the two groups (same questionnaire, same encounter structure), so there is no differential measurement procedure. However, this outcome is a **participant-reported outcome** (the SDM-Q is completed by patients) and, for the video analysis, involves **observer judgement** (OPTION5 rated by a single non-blinded coder, "TSM"). Because the trial was unblinded and disclosure of genetic risk was itself salient and novel to participants, patients' self-report of SDM could plausibly be influenced by their awareness of which information (genetic vs. conventional) they received — e.g., participants who received the novel genetic information might rate the encounter differently due to a novelty effect or Hawthorne-type response, independent of the true underlying quality of shared decision-making. Similarly, the video rater's awareness of group assignment (not explicitly stated as blinded) leaves open the possibility of assessment being influenced by knowledge of intervention received. There is no explicit statement that the OPTION5 video coder was blinded to allocation. Given these considerations, knowledge of intervention assignment *could* have influenced assessment of this outcome, but there is no strong reason to believe assessment was *actually* materially influenced (the effect estimates were similar and non-significant, and differences would plausibly bias toward, not against, finding a difference). This leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Shared decision-making and physician visit satisfaction were pre-specified as secondary outcomes in the trial registration (NCT01936675), listed explicitly as "Shared decision making (as assessed by survey)" and "Physician visit satisfaction (as assessed by survey)." The publication reports the overall SDM-Q score, all 11 individual SDM-Q items (Table 2), the OPTION5 video-based score, the overall physician satisfaction score, and all 6 individual physician satisfaction items (Table 3) — consistent with a comprehensive, non-selective reporting of this outcome domain and its components. There is no indication that specific time points, subscales, or analysis approaches were selectively chosen based on the direction or significance of results; all pre-specified components of the SDM outcome domain (both self-report and independent observer-rated measures) were reported in full, including non-significant results (e.g., p=0.09 for overall SDM score), suggesting no bias toward reporting only favorable/significant findings.
