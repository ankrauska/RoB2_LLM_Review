1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The study used a random allocation method and centralized randomization. The report states that patients with disease control after induction chemotherapy “were randomly assigned, in a 1:1 ratio” and that “registration and random assignment were coordinated centrally at the KCSG data center.” It further specifies that “the random permutation method was used for random assignment,” with stratification by visceral disease, prior adjuvant taxane therapy, response to induction treatment, and hormone-receptor status. These features support random sequence generation and concealment of allocation until assignment.

   Baseline characteristics were generally balanced between groups. Although there was a nominal imbalance in hematogenous lung metastases (31.9% in maintenance versus 16.5% in observation; *P* = .029), there was no pattern of substantial or implausible imbalances across key prognostic factors, and group sizes were almost identical (116 versus 115). The isolated imbalance is compatible with chance given the many baseline comparisons.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to maintenance chemotherapy versus observation at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This was an open-label trial: the registry specifies “masking: none,” and participants and treating clinicians would necessarily have known whether maintenance chemotherapy was administered. However, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding causes bias only if it led to deviations from protocol that arose because of the trial context, affected progression-free survival (PFS), and were unbalanced between groups.

   The maintenance group had treatment discontinuations without progression, including discontinuation “at the physician’s discretion.” However, the report notes that these patients “did not receive further anticancer treatment, including endocrine therapy, until disease progression was documented.” Thus, there is no clear evidence that differential non-protocol anticancer treatment, contamination, or other trial-context-driven deviations occurred that would bias the assigned-treatment comparison.

   Most importantly, the PFS analysis was conducted according to the intention-to-treat principle: “All randomly assigned patients were included in the efficacy analysis according to the intent-to-treat principle.” This is an appropriate analysis for the effect of assignment to maintenance PG versus observation.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:** PFS was analysed as a time-to-event outcome and participants were censored “at the last clinical contact if patients were lost to follow-up or at the cutoff date for the analysis.” The report does not provide sufficient detail on the number of participants censored for each reason, the timing of censoring by group, or whether follow-up for progression continued after treatment discontinuation.

   There were substantial and markedly unequal non-progression discontinuations. In the maintenance group, only 40/116 participants discontinued because of progression, while 14 withdrew, 13 stopped because of adverse events, 33 discontinued at physician discretion, and 9 discontinued for other reasons. In contrast, 80/115 observation-group participants discontinued because of progression, with far fewer discontinuations at physician discretion (6). The reported reasons for discontinuation therefore differ importantly between arms.

   In particular, discontinuation because of toxicity, patient withdrawal, or physician discretion may be related to clinical status and prognosis, and thus to the subsequent time to progression. Such censoring can be informative in a PFS analysis. The report provides neither an analysis addressing potentially informative censoring nor sensitivity analyses demonstrating robustness to missing progression data. Therefore, it is likely that missingness/censoring depended on the true outcome value and differed between groups.

4. **measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** PFS was based on radiographic evaluation and investigator assessment: “Radiographic studies were performed every 6 weeks,” “Disease response was assessed according to RECIST,” and “PFS and the duration of response were assessed by investigators.”

   The scheduled imaging and use of RECIST version 1.0 support broadly comparable outcome ascertainment between groups, and the measurement method itself was appropriate. Nevertheless, the trial was unblinded and no blinded independent central radiology review or blinded adjudication of progression was reported. PFS assessment includes judgement regarding radiographic progression and timing/confirmation of progression, so knowledge of treatment assignment could potentially influence assessment.

   There is no direct evidence that investigators’ assessments were actually influenced by knowledge of assignment; therefore, the concern is not sufficient for a high-risk judgement. However, the absence of blinded progression assessment in this open-label maintenance-versus-observation comparison raises some concerns.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was prospectively registered (NCT00561119; first posted November 2007), and the registry identifies PFS as the primary outcome. This is consistent with the publication, which states: “The primary endpoint was PFS after random assignment.”

   However, the registry provides only “Progression free survival” with a broad timeframe of “approximately 4 years.” It does not specify the precise PFS analysis, censoring rules, effect measure, adjustment strategy, or a prespecified landmark analysis. No dated statistical analysis plan was available. The publication reports several PFS summaries, including median PFS, a hazard ratio, and a 6-month PFS rate; indeed, it describes the 6-month PFS rate as “the primary endpoint,” whereas the registry does not specify this landmark analysis.

   There is no clear evidence that the reported PFS result was selected from multiple analyses or measurements because it was favourable. Nevertheless, absence of a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan means that selective selection among possible PFS analyses cannot be excluded.
