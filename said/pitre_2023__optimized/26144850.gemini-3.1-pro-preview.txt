**1. bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The allocation sequence was not adequately concealed, which leads to a high risk of bias.
b. Rationale: The study states that "268 three-digit random numbers from the table were written in the patient registers in advance. Patients with even numbers were given PB-MDT and those with odd numbers were given ROM." Having the sequence written in the registers in advance means that the recruiting staff could know the next treatment assignment, removing any allocation concealment. 

**2. deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**
a. Judgement: An appropriate intention-to-treat analysis was not used, and the exclusion of patients who did not complete treatment had the potential for a substantial impact on the result.
b. Rationale: Blinding to treatment was not undertaken, meaning participants and personnel were aware of the interventions. Furthermore, the analysis for cure rate was not an intention-to-treat analysis, as it excluded patients who defaulted on their treatment. The study states, "The cure rate at 2 years was 99% in ROM-6 and 97.0% in PB-MDT group, of those who completed treatment". Given that 27 (20.1%) patients in the MDT group and 19 (14.2%) in the ROM-6 group defaulted, excluding them represents a substantial proportion of the randomized population, which can significantly bias the estimated effect of assignment to intervention.

**3. missing outcome data: high risk of bias**
a. Judgement: A substantial proportion of outcome data is missing at 2 years, and the missingness is likely to depend on the true value of the outcome.
b. Rationale: At the 2-year follow-up, only 100/134 patients in the MDT group and 103/134 in the ROM group were assessed. There is no evidence that the result is robust to these missing data. Missingness could depend on the true value of the outcome because many patients defaulted due to drug intolerance or because they felt they were already cured ("feeling that they were cured"). Ceasing treatment early could directly impact whether they were actually cured at 2 years, making the missing data missing not at random.

**4. measurement of the outcome: some concerns**
a. Judgement: The outcome assessors were aware of the intervention received, and the outcome assessment involved some subjective clinical judgment. 
b. Rationale: The trial was completely unblinded ("Blinding to treatment was not undertaken"). The outcome of "cure" involved clinical assessments such as the patch becoming flat, hypopigmented, decreasing in size, and/or regaining sensation. Testing for loss of sensation and evaluating patch resolution involves some subjective judgment by the examiner, which could potentially be influenced by knowledge of the treatment received.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
a. Judgement: There is no evidence of a pre-specified analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available.
b. Rationale: While the trial was retrospectively registered in the Clinical Trial Registry of India in 2013, the patient recruitment occurred between 2001 and 2004. Therefore, there is no date-stamped protocol or statistical analysis plan available from before the trial started to confirm that the outcomes and analyses were not selected based on the results. However, the reported outcomes (cure rate and relapse rate) are standard for this disease, so there is no clear indication that the specific measurements or analyses were selectively reported from multiple eligible options.
