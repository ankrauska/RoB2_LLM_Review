# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Rate of Emergency Hemostasis

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **SOME CONCERNS**

**a. Judgement:** The trial states randomization used "a random number table method," which supports an adequate sequence generation (Signalling Q1.1: Yes). However, there is no information provided regarding allocation concealment (Signalling Q1.2: No information). Baseline characteristics appear well-balanced (Table shows non-significant P-values for all baseline variables), suggesting no problem with randomization is evident (Signalling Q1.3: No). Per the algorithm (Table 4), when allocation concealment is "No information" and there is no evidence of baseline imbalance, the default judgement is "Some concerns."

**b. Rationale:** 
- "A random number table was produced through Microsoft Excel. The patients were divided into a control group and a therapy group following the random number table."
- No description of concealment mechanism (e.g., sealed envelopes, central allocation) is provided anywhere in the methods.
- Table data show no significant baseline differences (e.g., sex P=1.000, age P=0.245, active esophageal bleeding P=1.000, red sign P=0.829, Child-Pugh score P=0.325, varices grade P=0.779, GOV classification P=0.860), consistent with successful randomization.
- Since concealment cannot be confirmed but there is no evidence of a problem with the process, the domain-level judgement defaults to "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk" or "High risk."

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**a. Judgement:** This was an open-label, unblinded trial by necessity (procedural/endoscopic intervention). Participants and personnel/proceduralists were clearly aware of group assignment (Signalling Q2.1/Q2.2: Yes) because the interventions (simultaneous vs. sequential embolization/EVL) were procedurally and temporally distinct and could not be masked. Critically, in the control group, when hemostasis failed after embolization alone, the treating team explicitly performed EVL as rescue therapy — a deviation from the assigned "sequential" protocol that was directly and causally linked to the outcome being measured (hemostasis) and was clearly a product of the trial context/clinical awareness of treatment failure. This deviation was unbalanced between groups (it only occurred in the control group) and directly affects the outcome definition/ascertainment.

**b. Rationale:**
- "Hemorrhaging in 10 patients among the 20 patients with active esophageal variceal bleeding was not controlled and EVL was performed for the patients whose hemorrhage was not controllable. Hemorrhaging was controlled after EVL."
- This means the "control" group's reported hemostasis outcome for some patients actually reflects the effect of receiving EVL (the therapy-group intervention component) as rescue — a clear deviation from assigned intervention that occurred because clinicians (unblinded, aware of ongoing bleeding) intervened.
- Despite this deviation, the analysis appears to classify these patients' hemostasis based on pre-rescue status (87.8% initial rate reported), suggesting some inconsistency in how the outcome was analyzed relative to assignment, but the deviation itself confounds the true comparative effect of "simultaneous" vs "sequential" strategy on hemostasis.
- No blinding was possible or attempted, and awareness of group assignment plausibly influenced clinical decisions to intervene early in non-responding control-group patients.
- This meets criteria for **High risk**: personnel were aware of intervention assignment, deviations (rescue EVL) arose from the trial context (clinical management decisions in an unblinded setting), these deviations likely affected the outcome (hemostasis), and they were unbalanced between groups (occurred only in the control arm).

## 3. Missing outcome data: **LOW RISK OF BIAS**

**a. Judgement:** Outcome data (hemostasis achieved or not) appear to be available for all randomized participants in both groups (90/90 in therapy group, 82/82 in control group are accounted for in the hemostasis results).

**b. Rationale:**
- "Hemorrhaging was controlled in all 90 patients in the therapy group, the rate of emergency hemostasis was 100.0%. In the control group, hemorrhaging was controlled in 72 out of 82 patients and the rate of emergency hemostasis was 87.8%."
- All 172 enrolled patients (90 + 82) are accounted for in the results with no reported losses to follow-up, withdrawals, or exclusions for this specific short-term outcome.
- No CONSORT flow diagram is presented, but the narrative results account for the full denominator in each group, satisfying the "nearly all" criterion (Signalling Q3.1: Yes).

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **SOME CONCERNS**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome (emergency hemostasis, i.e., control of active bleeding) is an observer-reported/clinician-determined outcome involving some judgement (assessment of whether bleeding has stopped, often via endoscopic visualization and clinical parameters). Outcome assessors (treating endoscopists/clinicians) were not blinded, as blinding was not feasible given the distinct procedural nature of the interventions (Signalling Q4.3: Yes, aware). Assessment of hemostasis could plausibly be influenced by knowledge of intervention received (Signalling Q4.4: Yes), particularly because the definition of "success" was operationally entangled with the decision to give rescue treatment (EVL) in the control group. However, there is no strong, specific evidence (e.g., stated preconceptions, financial or publication interests) indicating that assessment was clearly manipulated based on the group assignment beyond the inherent structural confound described in Domain 2, so escalation to "High risk" for this domain specifically is not clearly justified.

**b. Rationale:**
- Hemostasis was likely assessed via direct endoscopic visualization by the treating (unblinded) endoscopist: "Active gastric fundus variceal bleeding was controlled after embolization with Histoacryl (Figs. A and B); active esophageal variceal bleeding was controlled after EVL (Figs. C and D)."
- No independent, blinded adjudication committee or blinded outcome assessor is described.
- The measurement method itself is appropriate (endoscopic confirmation of bleeding cessation is a standard, valid method; Signalling Q4.1: No) and was applied comparably in principle to both groups (Signalling Q4.2: No/Probably no), but the unblinded assessor's awareness of assignment, combined with the judgement involved in determining "control" of bleeding and timing of rescue intervention, introduces a plausible but not definitively "likely" source of bias.
- This leads to a default judgement of "Some concerns" (aware assessor, outcome could be influenced by knowledge of intervention, but not clearly established as "likely" influenced independent of the deviation already captured in Domain 2).

## 5. Selective reporting: **LOW RISK OF BIAS**

**a. Judgement:** There is no trial registration available for this study (search based on provided doc_id yielded no matching/applicable registry record for this specific trial), and no protocol or statistical analysis plan is referenced in the publication. However, the outcome "rate of emergency hemostasis" is a single, unambiguous, pre-specified primary clinical outcome with only one plausible definition and analysis (proportion of patients achieving bleeding control), leaving little opportunity for selective outcome or analysis reporting.

**b. Rationale:**
- The outcome is dichotomous and simply defined (bleeding controlled: yes/no), with only one method of analysis reported (percentage with Chi-square test): "For enumeration data, the Chi-square test was used."
- There is no evidence of multiple outcome measurements (e.g., different scales, time points) or multiple analytic approaches (e.g., adjusted vs. unadjusted) for this outcome from which a result could have been selectively chosen.
- Given the outcome's simplicity and single mode of measurement/analysis, and absence of indications of selective reporting (e.g., no discrepancy between an available registry entry and the published result), this is judged as low risk despite the absence of a protocol for verification.

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **HIGH RISK OF BIAS**

**Rationale:** Per Table 1 criteria, a judgement of "High risk of bias" in any single domain results in an overall "High risk of bias" judgement for the outcome. Domain 2 (deviations from intended intervention) was judged as high risk because clinicians in the control group administered rescue EVL (the intervention component unique to the therapy group) to non-responding patients in an unblinded setting, directly and asymmetrically affecting the hemostasis outcome in a manner inconsistent with the assigned "sequential" treatment strategy. This introduces substantial risk of bias in the estimated effect of assignment to intervention for this specific outcome, notwithstanding "Some concerns" in randomization and outcome measurement domains, and low risk in missing data and selective reporting domains.
