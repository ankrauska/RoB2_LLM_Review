1. **bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** The randomization process is at high risk of bias because allocation concealment was not described and important baseline imbalances between randomized groups suggest that randomization may not have produced comparable groups.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The report states that, in period B, “*crew members were divided into two groups according to random numbers*,” which supports a random component in sequence generation. However, it provides no information on who generated the random numbers, whether allocation was concealed until enrolment/assignment, or whether assignments could have been predicted or influenced.  
   
   More importantly, the groups appear materially imbalanced in prognostic factors related to muscle damage. The allopurinol group contained 8 high-intensity-work sailors (of 13), whereas the placebo group contained only 2 high-intensity-work sailors (of 10). High-intensity work was strongly associated with higher CK levels in period A. Baseline CK was also substantially higher in the allopurinol group than the placebo group (445.8 versus 275.8 U/L), and baseline AST was higher (31.0 versus 24.2 U/L). These differences raise concern that the groups had different underlying muscle-damage profiles before the intervention. Under RoB 2, absent allocation-concealment information combined with baseline differences suggesting a problem with randomization leads to a high-risk judgement.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Although sailors were reportedly blinded to allocation and no important protocol deviations were reported, the analysis was not appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to allopurinol versus placebo.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The article reports that “*Sailors were blind regarding the group assignment*,” and the comparator was placebo. This reduces the likelihood that participant awareness caused differential non-protocol behaviour. There is no reported evidence that trial-context deviations from the intended interventions occurred or differed between groups. Blinding of carers/personnel and intervention delivery procedures, however, are not described in sufficient detail.  
   
   The major concern is the analysis. The trial reports separate within-group pre/post comparisons: CK decreased from 445.8 to 333.6 U/L in the allopurinol group (*p* < 0.05), while CK changed from 275.8 to 237.3 U/L in the placebo group (*p* > 0.05). The report does not provide an appropriate randomized, between-group comparison of the change in CK or post-race CK adjusted for baseline CK. It therefore relies on the inappropriate inference that a statistically significant within-group change in one arm, but not in the other, demonstrates a treatment effect.  
   
   In addition, four sailors “*finally declined to take part in this phase of the study*” after the crew had reportedly been divided into random groups. The analysis includes only 23 of the 27 crew members, with no intention-to-treat analysis including all randomized participants and no information on the original allocation of the four who declined. This failure to analyse participants according to their assigned group could have had a substantial impact, particularly in this very small trial with marked baseline imbalances.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** There are some concerns because outcome data were not available for all randomized crew members, and the extent and distribution of exclusions by randomized group were not reported.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The study initially involved all 27 crew members. In the randomized intervention phase, “*Four sailors finally declined to take part in this phase of the study*,” leaving 13 sailors assigned to allopurinol and 10 assigned to placebo. Thus, post-race muscle-damage outcomes were unavailable for 4/27 (approximately 15%) of those apparently randomized.  
   
   The report does not state which randomized group(s) these four sailors were initially assigned to, their reasons for declining, their baseline CK/AST values, or whether their absence was related to factors predictive of post-race muscle damage. No sensitivity analysis or other analysis addressing the potential impact of the missing data was reported.  
   
   Because declining participation before the intervention and outcome assessment does not by itself establish that missingness was likely dependent on the unobserved post-race CK/AST values, high risk is not certain. Nevertheless, the incomplete reporting and lack of information about the allocation and characteristics of excluded sailors means that bias due to missing data cannot be confidently excluded.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Measurement of CK and AST, the muscle-damage markers, is at low risk of bias.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** CK and AST were measured using standardized laboratory methods: “*serum CK, and AST activities were spectrophotometrically measured using commercially available reagents on an automatic random access analyser*.” The reported intra-assay coefficients of variation were 4.5% for CK and 4.2% for AST. These are objective biochemical outcomes with little scope for participant or assessor judgement to influence the recorded result.  
   
   Blood samples were collected using the same stated approach in both treatment groups, immediately before and after the racing period. Although the selected timing of the post-race sample may not capture the maximum possible CK elevation—the authors acknowledge that CK can peak later after exercise—this issue applies similarly to both groups and does not indicate differential outcome measurement. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment differed between the allopurinol and placebo groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** There are some concerns about selective reporting because no pre-specified, date-stamped analysis plan was available to verify that the reported muscle-damage result was selected independently of the findings.  
   
   b. **Rationale:** The supplied article describes measurement of multiple related biochemical outcomes—CK, AST, and MDA—at more than one time point and in several participant subgroupings. The article reports that CK decreased significantly in the allopurinol group whereas AST and MDA did not show statistically significant changes. However, it does not identify a pre-specified primary muscle-damage outcome, primary time point, or primary analysis method.  
   
   The trial-registration information referenced in the request was not provided in the materials available for this assessment; therefore, it was not possible to verify whether CK, AST, MDA, the post-race time point, or the within-group analysis approach were specified before unblinded outcome data became available. There is no direct evidence that the CK result was selectively chosen from multiple analyses or measurements based on its statistical significance, so high risk is not justified. However, absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan results in some concerns.
