1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was computerized and concealed until assignment. The report states that randomization was performed by Survey Research Laboratory staff “using a random allocation sequence programmed into CAPI administration software that allowed for complete allocation concealment up to the point of assignment.” It further states that “both interviewers and respondents had no way of knowing each subject’s study condition until after the assignment had occurred.”

   Baseline characteristics were broadly similar between groups, including age, sex, diagnosis, prior psychiatric inpatient care, services received, and site distribution. The authors report “the absence of statistically significant differences by study condition at baseline on all characteristics.” The modest observed differences are compatible with chance and do not indicate a failure of randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to WRAP versus assignment to the wait-list/usual-care condition.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew whether they were assigned to the peer-led WRAP programme or to the wait-list condition, and WRAP facilitators were also aware of allocation. However, for the assignment effect, lack of blinding alone does not imply bias. Bias requires deviations from the protocol that arose because of the trial context, affected the outcome, and were unbalanced between groups.

   Some non-attendance occurred: “233 (84%) received the intervention and 43 (16%) did not,” and participants attended a mean of 5.05 of 8 sessions. Control participants also sometimes attended peer-led support groups. However, these are not shown to have resulted from trial-context deviations from the intended assigned strategies. In particular, access to usual services and peer-support groups appears to be part of the real-world care context rather than evidence that trial personnel systematically undermined the allocated comparison.

   The analysis was appropriate for the assignment effect. The authors state that outcomes were assessed “using an intent-to-treat mixed-effects random regression analysis,” and that “no other subjects were excluded from the analysis for any other reason given the ‘intent-to-treat’ design.” Participants were analysed according to randomized condition; missing data are considered separately below.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not available for all or nearly all randomized participants. Although 555 participants were randomized, the analysed sample comprised only 519 participants: 251 in the WRAP group and 268 in the control group. Thus, 25/276 (9.1%) randomized to WRAP and 11/279 (3.9%) randomized to control were lost before inclusion in the analysed sample. This differential loss is notable.

   For the BSI Global Severity Index, outcome data were available for 224/251 WRAP and 234/268 control participants at immediate post-intervention, and 220/251 and 228/268, respectively, at the later follow-up. Thus, approximately 12–15% of the analysed sample lacked BSI data at each follow-up, in addition to the participants excluded from the analysed sample after randomization.

   Missingness plausibly depended on true psychiatric symptom severity. Reasons for loss included “death or ill health, moving away from the area, and formal withdrawal from the study,” and the report notes that 68% of intervention participants lost to follow-up had received no WRAP sessions. In a population with severe and persistent mental illness, illness, disengagement, and withdrawal may reasonably be related to psychiatric symptoms. The report states that mixed-effects models addressed “missing observations,” but it does not provide sensitivity analyses demonstrating that the BSI result was robust to plausible assumptions about missing outcomes, nor does it adequately establish that missingness was unrelated to symptom severity. The greater post-randomization loss in the WRAP group further increases concern.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The BSI is a valid and established instrument, and the same structured telephone-interview method appears to have been used in both groups. Interviewers were blinded to study allocation: “interviewers were blinded to respondents’ study condition,” with only 4% of follow-up interviews involving disclosure of allocation.

   However, the BSI is a participant-reported outcome: respondents rated how much they had been bothered by 53 symptoms during the previous week. Therefore, the participant—not the interviewer—is the outcome assessor for RoB 2 purposes. Participants were aware of whether they had received the eight-session WRAP programme or were waiting for it. The study itself acknowledges that a limitation was reliance on “participant self-report data that were uncorroborated by clinicians or objective observers.”

   Psychiatric symptom ratings are subjective and could readily be influenced by expectations, perceived benefit, social desirability, or disappointment/anticipation associated with the wait-list condition. Indeed, the authors suggest that improvement among controls may have reflected an “‘anticipation effect’ because controls were promised an opportunity to receive WRAP at the end of the study.” Given the unblinded, self-reported nature of the BSI outcome and the plausible influence of allocation knowledge on reported symptoms, it is likely that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial registry identifies psychiatric symptoms measured using the BSI Positive Symptom Score as a primary outcome, assessed at baseline, 8 weeks, and 6 months after the intervention. The publication reports both BSI Positive Symptom Total and BSI Global Severity Index, each analysed using a condition-by-time interaction in mixed-effects regression. The publication also states that “all outcomes, hypotheses, and statistical analyses presented here were prespecified at the time the proposal was submitted to the federal government for funding consideration.”

   Nevertheless, the available registry record was first posted in December 2009, after the study had started (December 2007) and after primary completion (September 2009). It therefore cannot demonstrate that the detailed outcome-measurement and analysis choices were finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. No contemporaneous protocol or statistical analysis plan is available here to verify the prespecified choice between the multiple eligible BSI indices, the exact time-point analysis, or the modelling strategy.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported BSI result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable to WRAP. Therefore, a high-risk judgement is not warranted. However, the absence of a prospectively available, sufficiently detailed analysis plan means that selective selection among BSI measurements and analyses cannot be excluded.
