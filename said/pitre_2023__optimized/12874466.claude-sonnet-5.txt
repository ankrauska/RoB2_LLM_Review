# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0 for Subjective Global Assessment (SGA) Score

**Preliminary considerations:** This is an individually-randomized parallel-group trial comparing oral sodium bicarbonate vs. placebo in PD patients. The effect of interest is assignment to intervention (ITT effect), as specified in the prompt.

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** 
- Sequence generation: "Individuals were randomized by a computer-generated list" — this indicates a random component (Answer to 1.1: Yes).
- Allocation concealment: The list "was used for packaging of the tablets and then maintained by a third party that was not involved in the conduction of the study... Marked tablet packs were designated for each patient." This suggests reasonable concealment (Answer to 1.2: Probably yes), though full details (e.g., sequential numbering, tamper-proofing) are not described.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows no statistically significant differences in most baseline characteristics; however, there are notable imbalances in comorbidities that were not tested statistically but appear clinically relevant: congestive heart failure (8 vs 12), cerebrovascular disease (6 vs 1), peptic ulcer disease (2 vs 7). The authors themselves note: "Although the treatment group had marginally more patients with underlying heart failure and fewer patients with cerebrovascular disease than the control group, the differences were not statistically significant." Given the small sample size (n=30/group), these differences are plausible due to chance, and the paper explicitly addresses and adjusts for comorbidity (Charlson Index) in later analyses. No P-values are reported for baseline comparisons, so signalling question 1.3 is answered "No information" for formal imbalance testing, but qualitative imbalances are visible.
- Given adequate-appearing concealment and randomization but incomplete reporting of concealment procedures and some baseline imbalances (not clearly tested/reported), this domain is rated as **Some concerns** rather than Low, since there is no information on whether these baseline differences are compatible with chance (no p-values provided for baseline comparisons) combined with only "probably yes" for allocation concealment detail.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Blinding of participants/personnel:** "The appearance, packaging, and labeling of the tablets were identical. Both kinds of tablets had artificial mint flavor to give an identical taste... Both patients and investigators were blinded." This suggests successful blinding (2.1 and 2.2: Probably No).
- However, discontinuation due to side effects occurred asymmetrically-related to the study drugs: "Four patients in the treatment group could not tolerate the study medication because of dyspepsia (3 cases) or dizziness (1 case). Study medication was stopped in 3 patients of the placebo group because of dyspepsia (2 cases) or skin rash (1 case)." Side effects like dyspepsia/dizziness could potentially unblind some participants or caregivers, raising the possibility that a subset of participants/personnel became aware of allocation (partial unblinding), which could lead to deviations from intended intervention arising from trial context (signalling question 2.3).
- There is no explicit statement that these deviations (medication discontinuation) arose from "the trial context" beyond typical clinical response to adverse effects, nor evidence that these were unbalanced in a way likely to bias the SGA outcome (numbers are similar between groups: 4 vs 3).
- **Analysis:** "Analyses were intention to treat irrespective of adherence to treatment regimen" — this is explicitly stated and appropriate (signalling question 2.6: Yes), satisfying Part 2 as Low risk.
- Given mostly successful blinding but some evidence of potential partial unblinding via adverse effects (though balanced in number and not clearly shown to bias outcome), and appropriate ITT analysis, the domain is judged as **Some concerns**, primarily due to residual uncertainty about whether occurrence of these adverse effects led to differential behavioral changes (e.g., could dyspepsia/dizziness reveal allocation and affect participant behavior around diet, hence unconsciously affect SGA/nutritional assessment)? This is not explicitly ruled out.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- All 60 randomized patients (30 per group) appear to have SGA data reported at all time points in Table 3, despite 7 patients (4 treatment, 3 placebo) discontinuing study medication: "Although study medication was stopped in these patients, biochemical tests and nutritional assessment were continued for 52 wk according to the study protocol."
- Deaths occurred (2 in treatment, 5 in placebo) during the 12-month follow-up, but SGA was assessed at 0, 12, 24, 36, and 52 weeks — it is unclear whether these deceased patients contributed SGA data at all time points, particularly at 52 weeks. However, the paper indicates continued follow-up and no explicit loss to follow-up outside of these documented deaths/transfers (2 transferred to hemodialysis, 1 transplant).
- The number of patients contributing to the SGA outcome appears essentially complete for the majority of follow-up, with the drop-off due to death/transplant/technique failure being small (up to 8 patients total across groups by 52 weeks) and roughly balanced between groups, unlikely to substantially bias the result.
- No evidence that missingness in SGA depended on its true value in a way that would differentially bias results between groups.
- Overall, outcome data appear available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants throughout the follow-up period reported in Table 3.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Appropriateness of method:** SGA is a validated, appropriate tool for nutritional assessment in dialysis patients (referencing CANUSA study and others), so signalling question 4.1: No (not inappropriate).
- **Comparability of measurement between groups:** No evidence that measurement/ascertainment differed systematically between groups; same protocol and same observers were used for both groups (4.2: No).
- **Blinding of outcome assessors:** "SGA was performed at 0, 12, 24, 36, and 52 wk by two trained observers who were blinded from the treatment group allocation and biochemical results of the patients." This is a strong statement of blinded assessment (4.3: No — outcome assessors were unaware of intervention).
- However, SGA is a subjective, judgment-based outcome, partially informed by patient self-report of anorexia and weight loss and clinical/subjective judgment of the assessor. Even with formal blinding of the SGA assessors to allocation, if participants themselves were aware of, or suspected, their treatment allocation (e.g., due to differential side effects such as dyspepsia/dizziness noted in the treatment arm), and the SGA process could involve some interaction with/reporting by participants (e.g., regarding appetite/anorexia and weight loss items, which are patient-reported components embedded in the SGA), there is a possibility that outcome ascertainment could be influenced by participant awareness.
- The elaboration in RoB2 guidance notes that "for outcomes that are assessed using an interaction between a participant and a data collector... if either the participant is blinded and the data collector is not, or the data collector is blinded and the participant is not, then the outcome assessors should be considered to be aware of intervention received unless convincing evidence is available to the contrary." Since SGA includes participant-reported elements (anorexia, weight change) and it's unclear whether full blinding of participants was verified (i.e., no formal test of blinding success is reported), there remains a low-to-moderate possibility that participant awareness (via taste, side effects, or other unblinding) could have influenced their reporting of symptoms feeding into SGA.
- Given the explicit blinding of assessors, but some uncertainty about participant-level blinding integrity and the partly subjective/patient-report-dependent nature of the SGA score, the judgement is **Some concerns** rather than Low, reflecting a "could have been influenced" scenario without concrete evidence of high risk.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- SGA was declared as the primary outcome measure a priori: "The primary outcome measure was the SGA score." Sample size calculation was explicitly based on SGA as the primary outcome ("with the SGA as the primary outcome measure... use of sample sizes of 60 achieves 81% power to detect a difference of 1.0 between the groups").
- The trial registration document (doc_id: 4dab5cab-2b44-42f8-8559-2dd9073b18b5) was reviewed for consistency; based on the description provided, there is no indication of discrepancy between the pre-specified primary outcome and what is reported. The overall SGA score is reported (not merely a subscale), fully consistent with a pre-specified single primary composite score, without evidence of selective reporting among multiple SGA scoring methods or time points — the paper reports SGA at all planned time points (0, 12, 24, 36, 52 weeks) as detailed in Table 3, and describes an appropriate repeated-measures ANOVA analysis with post-hoc comparisons, consistent with a pre-planned longitudinal analysis strategy.
- All four component items of SGA (anorexia, weight loss, subcutaneous fat, muscle mass) plus the overall score are transparently reported in Table 3, without evidence of selective omission of unfavorable component results.
- There is no indication that the reported overall SGA result was selected from among multiple alternative analyses or measurements based on favorability of results.
