1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was generated using an explicitly random method and was adequately concealed. The report states that participants were allocated using “a block randomization determined by a computer-generated random number table, with stratification for operator and center.” Allocation was protected using “sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelope[s]” opened only after the patient had been accepted into the trial. In addition, “the generator of the randomization did not participate in patient allocation.”

   The enrolled numbers were reasonably similar (67 SmartClip and 71 Victory), and the reported baseline characteristics do not demonstrate an imbalance so extreme as to be implausible by chance. There were some numerical differences in malocclusion categories and mean age, but no evidence that these reflected manipulation or a failure of randomization. Therefore, the randomization process appears to have been appropriately implemented.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and clinicians could not be blinded to bracket type: “it was not possible to blind the clinician or patient to the type of bracket system being used.” Thus, awareness of allocated intervention was present.

   However, the trial used a standardized treatment approach across groups: “Two operators followed a standardized protocol regarding bracket bonding procedure and archwire sequence,” and the manufacturer-recommended archwire sequence was used “as far as possible,” with exceptions recorded. The report provides no evidence that deviations inconsistent with the protocol arose because of the trial context, were differentially implemented between groups, or were likely to affect total treatment duration.

   The analysis was described as “per protocol” because of the low dropout rate. Nevertheless, the analysed population excluded only three of 138 randomized participants: one patient who declined fixed-appliance treatment after allocation and two who did not complete treatment. For the assignment-effect assessment, exclusion solely because the treatment-duration outcome was unavailable is analogous to a modified intention-to-treat/complete-case analysis; the associated concern is primarily addressed under missing outcome data. The very small number excluded is unlikely to have materially biased the comparison through analysis in the wrong randomized group.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Treatment-duration data were available for 135 of 138 randomized participants (97.8%). The CONSORT information indicates that 70 of 71 Victory participants and 65 of 67 SmartClip participants were included in analysis. Thus, outcome data were missing for only three participants overall (2.2%).

   The publication states: “Overall, 135 subjects (97.8%) completed the study; one subject who refused fixed appliance treatment following allocation and two subjects who failed to complete treatment were omitted from the analysis.” Although non-completion could conceivably be related to treatment duration, the amount of missing data is very small for this continuous outcome. It is unlikely that the missing durations, whatever their values, could have made an important difference to the estimated between-group mean difference in treatment duration.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Total treatment duration was defined clearly and is based on relatively objective dates: “total duration of treatment (months) from fixed appliance placement to debond.” Data analysis was performed with examiner blinding, and “the appliance type was not recorded on the data collection sheets in an attempt to minimize bias during data entry and analysis.” These features reduce the risk of differential error in recording or analysing treatment dates.

   However, clinicians and participants were unblinded to the allocated bracket system. Total treatment duration includes the time at which the treating clinician decides that treatment is complete and the appliance should be debonded. This is, at least partly, a treatment-provider decision outcome. The report does not describe prespecified, objective, or independently assessed criteria for debonding that would ensure that the decision to end treatment could not be influenced by knowledge of bracket allocation. Therefore, knowledge of intervention could have influenced the timing of debonding, although there is insufficient evidence to conclude that it probably did so. This leads to some concerns rather than high risk of bias.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Total duration of treatment was explicitly identified as one of the trial outcomes: “Efficiency of each ligation system was assessed by comparing the duration of treatment (months), total number of appointments … and number of bracket bond failures.” The reported analysis and results for treatment duration are consistent with that stated outcome: mean treatment times were 25.12 months for SmartClip and 25.80 months for Victory, with a reported mean difference and 95% confidence interval.

   However, there is no accessible prespecified statistical analysis plan in the supplied material demonstrating that the exact analysis of total treatment duration—including the handling of the three participants not analysed—was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The referenced trial-registration document was not available in the provided text for verification of prospectively specified outcome timing, analysis population, and analytic method. Since treatment duration could plausibly have been analysed in more than one eligible way (for example, complete-case versus other approaches to missing durations, adjusted versus unadjusted analyses), selective reporting cannot be excluded. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected based on its findings, so high risk of bias is not warranted.
