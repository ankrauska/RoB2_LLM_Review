1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias because the allocation sequence was computer generated, allocation was concealed through telephone inquiry, and the reported baseline characteristics do not suggest an important failure of randomization.  
   b. **Rationale:** The investigators state that “*Procedure type was assigned by a computer-generated randomization list, and concealment of randomization was done by telephone inquiry.*” These are appropriate methods for sequence generation and allocation concealment. Baseline characteristics—including age, gravidity, parity, body mass index/Quetelet index, and sonographically estimated uterine weight—were broadly comparable between groups (Table 1), with no imbalance clearly incompatible with chance. The differing experience of surgeons between groups is post-randomization and does not indicate a failure of the randomization process.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to LAVH versus AH at baseline.  
   b. **Rationale:** Blinding of participants and operating personnel was not feasible for these visibly different surgical procedures. However, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding alone does not imply bias. There is no evidence that deviations inconsistent with the protocol arose because of the trial context and affected length of stay unequally between groups. In particular, there were “*no conversions from LAVH to AH*.” Both groups received a broadly comparable postoperative pathway, including an intra-abdominal drain “*usually removed on postoperative day 2*.”  

   The reported hospital-stay data are presented for all randomized participants—28 allocated to LAVH and 20 to AH—and there is no report of exclusions from this outcome analysis. Thus, the analysis appears consistent with analysing participants in their randomized groups. Although the report does not explicitly label the analysis as intention-to-treat, there is no indication of an as-treated or per-protocol analysis or of post-randomization exclusion relevant to length of stay.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   f. **Rationale:** Length of hospital stay is an in-hospital outcome expected to be available for virtually every participant undergoing hysterectomy. Table 2 reports hospital stay for the full randomized group sizes: LAVH, *n* = 28; AH, *n* = 20. The article reports no withdrawals, losses to follow-up, deaths, or missing hospital-stay data before discharge. The questionnaire response rate was incomplete, but this applied to longer-term patient-reported recuperation and satisfaction outcomes—not the hospital-stay outcome under assessment. Therefore, missing data are unlikely to have materially affected the estimate of hospital stay.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Length of hospital stay was measured as the time until discharge, but discharge was explicitly determined by the unblinded participants: “*Each patient decided for herself when she was ready to be discharged from hospital.*” Participants necessarily knew whether they had received laparoscopic-assisted vaginal hysterectomy or abdominal hysterectomy.  

   Hospital discharge is a decision-based outcome, and—in this trial—the decision was made by the participant herself rather than by a blinded assessor applying objective discharge criteria. Knowledge of having received the less invasive laparoscopic procedure could plausibly and likely influence perceived readiness for discharge, expectations of recovery, and willingness to remain hospitalized. The authors themselves acknowledge that lack of blinding could influence patient-reported recovery-related outcomes: “*the patients could not be blinded to type of surgical treatment; thus, their expectations may have influenced the duration of their recuperation or their satisfaction with the procedure.*” The same concern applies directly to self-determined discharge timing. Therefore, assessment of hospital stay was likely influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies length of hospital stay as the primary outcome: “*We defined the primary outcome measure as length of hospital stay.*” It also reports a numerical result for this outcome, including medians and interquartile ranges in Table 2 (6.5 days for LAVH versus 10 days for AH). This reduces concern that the outcome itself was selectively omitted.  

   However, the supplied information does not include a dated pre-specified statistical analysis plan, nor sufficient trial-registration details to verify that the specific outcome definition, analysis method, and reporting approach for hospital stay were finalized before unblinded data became available. Several potentially eligible analyses of hospital stay could have been undertaken—for example, alternative summary measures, adjusted versus unadjusted analyses, or different handling of its likely skewed distribution. In the absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan and with no assurance that all eligible analyses were reported, RoB 2 indicates **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.
