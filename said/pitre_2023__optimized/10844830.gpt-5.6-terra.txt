1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because the report does not describe a genuinely random sequence-generation method or adequate allocation concealment. Although sealed envelopes were used, key safeguards (e.g., opaque, sequentially numbered, tamper-proof envelopes opened only after irrevocable enrolment) were not reported.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article states: “**Sealed envelopes were used to allocate at random 20 consecutive adults** … to group 1 … or group 2.” This establishes an intention to randomize but does not establish that the sequence had a random component, nor that allocation was concealed adequately. Baseline differences—including sex distribution (9/1 versus 5/5), pulmonary artery pressure (25 versus 19 mm Hg), CPB time (116 versus 84 min), and surgery type—are notable in this very small trial, but are not by themselves sufficiently extreme to demonstrate a failed or manipulated randomization process. Therefore, the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is *some concerns*, rather than high risk.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to high versus moderate inspired oxygen fraction at baseline, because an eligible participant’s ET-1 data were excluded from the analysis after randomization, despite outcome data being available. This could have had a substantial effect in a trial with only 10 participants per group.  
   b. **Rationale:** The intervention necessarily appears to have been unblinded to clinical personnel, since the assigned postoperative \(FIO_2\) differed markedly (1.0 versus 0.35). However, there is no reported evidence that deviations from the oxygen protocol arose because of the trial context or differed between groups.  

   The major concern is the analysis. The authors state: “**Group 2 had an outlier with high arterial ET-1 values (range 3.6–4.7 fmol litre−1) throughout the study**” and “**These data were excluded from the statistical analysis in order to simplify statistical interpretation.**” The figure caption refers to “arterial ET-1 values,” but the report indicates that arterial and mixed-venous peptide measurements were collected repeatedly and analysed together in the reported comparisons. Exclusion of a randomized participant’s data because the values were outlying is not an intention-to-treat-compatible analysis of the effect of assignment.  

   In a study of only 20 participants, excluding one of 10 participants in the moderate-\(FIO_2\) group (particularly one with persistently high ET-1 values) has clear potential to materially alter the venous ET-1 comparison. Thus, the analysis was inappropriate for the assignment effect and the likely impact could be substantial.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias due to missing outcome data.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article reports repeated arterial and mixed-venous blood sampling at all planned time points: before CPB, 20 minutes after CPB, 90 minutes after ICU arrival, and postoperative day 1. There is no report of participant withdrawal, loss to follow-up, failure to obtain venous samples, or differential missingness between groups.  

   The exclusion of the participant with high ET-1 values is an analysis exclusion, not missing outcome data; it is addressed under deviations from intended interventions. The report indicates that the participant’s ET-1 measurements existed (“throughout the study”), supporting that data were available rather than missing.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Venous ET-1 concentration is an objective laboratory outcome. Blood was sampled from the distal lumen of a pulmonary artery catheter, and ET-1 was measured using a standardized sandwich enzyme immunoassay. The authors report that “**Direct measurements of the peptides were performed in duplicate according to the manufacturer’s instructions**,” with reported intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation and an assay detection limit.  

   The same blood-sampling approach, assay method, and measurement time points were used in both intervention groups. Although laboratory personnel’s blinding status is not reported, the outcome was generated by an objective biochemical assay with little scope for subjective assessor judgement to be influenced by knowledge of the assigned \(FIO_2\). There is also no evidence that outcome ascertainment differed systematically between groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** No pre-specified statistical analysis plan or adequately detailed prospectively finalized trial-registration information is available in the supplied material to verify that the reported mixed-venous ET-1 result, time point, and analytical approach were selected before unblinded data were available.  

   The publication describes several outcome time points and both arterial and mixed-venous ET-1 concentrations. It presents serial ET-1 findings in Figure 1 and describes the main comparisons, which provides no direct evidence that a favourable venous ET-1 time point was selectively chosen. However, there were multiple eligible measurements (multiple time points and arterial versus mixed-venous samples) and potentially multiple possible analyses (e.g., final values, within-group changes, and between-group comparisons). Most importantly, the post hoc exclusion of an ET-1 outlier “to simplify statistical interpretation” raises concern that the reported analysis may have been influenced by the observed data.  

   In the absence of a pre-specified analysis plan or registration record detailing the venous ET-1 analysis, selective reporting cannot be ruled out, but there is insufficient evidence to judge this domain as high risk.
