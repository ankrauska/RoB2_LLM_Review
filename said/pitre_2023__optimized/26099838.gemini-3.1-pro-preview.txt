1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias
   a. Judgement: The study clearly describes a randomized process for allocating students to the teaching modules, and baseline characteristics appear balanced.
   b. Rationale: The article states: "Students were randomized into three groups using a computer program." Furthermore, Table 2 shows that there were no significant sex- or age-related differences between the three groups, indicating that the randomization was effective and there are no signs of baseline imbalances that would suggest a problem with the process.

2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias
   a. Judgement: There is no indication of significant deviations from the intended teaching modules, and all students completed the study.
   b. Rationale: The study describes the interventions explicitly and notes that "a total of 120 medical students (54.2% females) participated, and no one dropped out." Since this is a teaching intervention in a controlled setting, there are no concerns about cross-over or non-protocol interventions that would impact the effect of assignment to intervention.

3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias
   a. Judgement: Outcome data were complete for all participants.
   b. Rationale: The article explicitly states that "a total of 120 medical students (54.2% females) participated, and no one dropped out." Therefore, there is no missing outcome data, which precludes any risk of bias in this domain.

4. measurement of the outcome: some concerns
   a. Judgement: While the outcome measurement (a test score) is objective, it is unclear if the assessors marking the test or recording the time were blinded, and the students obviously knew which intervention they received.
   b. Rationale: The outcome was based on an examination paper (10 anatomical and 4 evaluative questions) and time recorded by an invigilator. Because students could not be blinded to the teaching modality, and the subjective evaluative questions could be influenced by their knowledge of the intervention, there are some concerns, especially for the subjective questions, though the objective anatomical questions are less prone to this bias.

5. selective reporting: some concerns
   a. Judgement: There is no mention of a pre-published protocol or trial registration to verify the original analysis plan.
   b. Rationale: The authors report outcomes for correct answers, time spent, and evaluation questions, but without a pre-registered protocol, it is impossible to be completely certain that other outcomes were not measured or that the analysis plan was not altered post hoc. (Note: no trial registration document was provided or referenced in the text).
