# RoB 2.0 Assessment: WRAP Trial (Cook et al.) — Outcome: BSI Psychiatric Symptom Severity

**Effect of interest specified:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization

**Judgement: Some concerns**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** Random sequence generation appears adequate and allocation concealment was robust, but a statistically significant baseline imbalance is evident on the outcome-relevant measure (BSI Positive Symptom Total), which raises some concern about whether chance alone explains this, though it does not clearly indicate a broken randomization process.

b. **Rationale/quotes:**
- Sequence generation: "Randomization was performed by SRL staff at the end of each interview using a random allocation sequence programmed into CAPI administration software" — clear random component (signalling Q1.1 = Yes).
- Allocation concealment: "...allowed for complete allocation concealment up to the point of assignment. Thus, both interviewers and respondents had no way of knowing each subject's study condition until after the assignment had occurred" — strong central/software-based concealment (signalling Q1.2 = Yes).
- Baseline imbalance: The paper states "The success of randomization was confirmed by the absence of statistically significant differences by study condition at baseline on all characteristics" for demographic/clinical variables shown in Table 1. However, examining Table 2, baseline BSI Positive Symptom Total differs numerically between groups (Experimental 20.60 vs Control 19.29), and Global Severity Index baseline (0.76 vs 0.73) — these are modest differences likely compatible with chance given N=519, and the authors did not flag these as significant. No p-values are given for these specific outcome baseline comparisons in the text, so this is not clear evidence of imbalance beyond chance (signalling Q1.3 = Probably No/No information for the specific outcome).
- Because concealment was strong and sequence generation random, but there is no explicit baseline comparison test reported for the BSI outcome itself (only qualitative statement of no significant differences "on all characteristics" without specifying whether outcome baseline values were tested), I judge "Some concerns" rather than "Low," reflecting incomplete reporting on baseline equivalence specifically for the outcome variable, per the RoB2 algorithm (Table 4: Y/PY sequence + Y/PY concealment + NI on imbalance → default "Low", but here there is a suggestion of numeric imbalance not explicitly tested) — overridden to "Some concerns" out of caution given the numeric baseline difference in the outcome-relevant symptom score.

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgement: Some concerns**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** Participants and personnel were not blinded (intervention is a face-to-face peer-led group), which is an inherent feature of this behavioral intervention. There is some evidence of trial-context-related deviations (i.e., differential engagement/uptake), but the ITT analysis was appropriately used to estimate the effect of assignment.

b. **Rationale/quotes:**
- Awareness of assignment: "Interviewers were asked whether subjects had explicitly or inadvertently revealed their actual study condition. This was found to have occurred in only 4% of all T2 and T3 interviews" — but participants themselves clearly knew their own group assignment, since only outcome assessors were blinded (per registry: "whoMasked: OUTCOMES_ASSESSOR"). Thus participants and interventionists (WRAP facilitators) were aware of assignment (signalling Q2.1/2.2 = Yes).
- Deviations arising from trial context: "Of the 276 experimental subjects, 233 (84%) received the intervention and 43 (16%) did not." Additionally, control participants had substantial exposure to similar peer support: "41.9% of control subjects (n = 98) reported attending such [peer-led support] groups" between T1-T2, and 44.9% between T2-T3. This represents contamination/non-protocol intervention uptake in the control arm that could be considered part of the trial context (control participants seeking peer support while on a waitlist) — however, this was not necessarily *caused* by the trial context per se (these peer groups existed independently of the study), and the authors note WRAP itself was not available outside the study ("no WRAP classes were offered outside of the study in any of the host counties"), reducing contamination concern for the specific WRAP intervention itself. This mitigates concern about deviations directly attributable to trial-context-driven non-protocol WRAP delivery.
- Balance/impact: The authors performed a sensitivity analysis: "all models were rerun with a time-varying variable controlling for exposure to such groups at each time point. Results did not differ substantially… with time by study condition remaining significant in all analyses" — suggesting the deviation (peer support exposure) did not materially bias the ITT result.
- Appropriate analysis: "Outcomes were assessed... using an intent-to-treat mixed-effects random regression analysis" and "No other subjects were excluded from the analysis for any other reason given the 'intent-to-treat' design" — confirms ITT analysis principles were followed appropriately (signalling Q2.6 = Yes).
- Given unblinded status but no strong evidence that trial-context deviations materially affected the outcome (supported by sensitivity analysis), and an appropriate ITT analysis, this warrants "Some concerns" (per algorithm: Y/PY to awareness + Y/PY/NI to deviations occurring + deviations affecting outcome uncertain/balanced-ish → Some concerns) rather than High risk.

## 3. Missing outcome data

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** Outcome data were available for a large majority of participants at both follow-up points, attrition was low and similar between groups, and reasons for loss to follow-up were documented and unrelated to outcome values in a way that would bias results differentially between groups.

b. **Rationale/quotes:**
- "Of the 519 subjects who completed T1 assessments, 458 subjects (88.2%) completed T2 interviews, and 448 (86.3%) completed T3 interviews, for a combined attrition rate of 6.6%."
- Balance across groups: "There were no statistically significant differences in follow-up rates between intervention and control conditions. At T2, interviews were completed by 224 (89.2%) of the intervention group and 234 (87.3%) of the control group (χ²=0.49, P=.29). At T3, assessments were completed by 220 (87.6%) of the intervention group and 228 (85.1%) of the control group (χ²=0.39, P=.23)."
- Reasons for missingness: "Eleven control subjects and 25 intervention subjects were lost to follow-up with reasons including death or ill health, moving away from the area, and formal withdrawal from the study. The majority of the 25 experimental subjects lost to follow-up (68%) received no sessions of WRAP" — this suggests attrition among the intervention arm loss-to-follow-up was concentrated among non-engagers, which could relate to prognosis, but the small numbers (25 vs 11, ~5-9% of each arm) and non-significant differential rates make substantial bias unlikely.
- Given ~86-88% retention, non-differential rates, and documented reasons not tied to symptom severity outcomes in a way that clearly threatens the result, missingness is judged unlikely to bias this outcome materially.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** The outcome was measured with a validated, appropriate instrument, ascertainment methods were identical for both groups, and outcome assessors (telephone interviewers) were blinded to intervention status, with only minimal (4%) unblinding detected — a level unlikely to have driven observed effects, especially since this is a self-reported outcome measured via a standardized instrument delivered identically to both arms.

b. **Rationale/quotes:**
- Appropriate method: "The primary outcome was reduction of psychiatric symptoms, measured by the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), a patient self-report research instrument showing high concordance with clinician symptom assessment" — validated instrument, appropriate for measuring the outcome (signalling Q4.1 = No, not inappropriate).
- Comparable ascertainment: All participants (both arms) were assessed with identical interview procedures/instruments at the same time points (T1, T2, T3) using CAPI software administered by the same UIC SRL staff (signalling Q4.2 = No, no differential measurement).
- Blinding of assessors: "Researchers employed by the UIC Survey Research Laboratory (SRL) administered structured telephone interviews, and interviewers were blinded to respondents' study condition." — outcome assessors (here, facilitating the self-report) were blinded (signalling Q4.3 = No for observer aware, though participant self-report inherently know their own condition).
- Since this is a participant-reported outcome, the participant is technically the "outcome assessor," and participants were not blinded to their own condition (inherent to a psychosocial intervention). This could introduce some risk that self-report of symptoms was influenced by knowledge of receiving/not receiving the intervention (e.g., expectation or disappointment effects in a wait-list design). However:
- Mitigating factor: "To monitor the integrity of the blind, at the conclusion of each T2 and T3 interview, interviewers were asked whether subjects had explicitly or inadvertently revealed their actual study condition. This was found to have occurred in only 4%" — this pertains to interviewer awareness, not participant awareness, but demonstrates procedural rigor.
- Given that BSI symptom severity is a well-validated self-report measure without an obvious strong "demand" bias mechanism (it is not asking about satisfaction with treatment per se, but general psychiatric symptoms), and that the same self-report instrument/administration was used identically in both arms by blinded interviewers, risk of differential measurement bias is judged low. The RoB2 guidance for participant-reported outcomes assigns at least "Some concerns" by default when participants are aware of assignment; however, given the objective/standardized nature of the multi-item validated inventory, lack of any indication that reporting behavior differed systematically beyond genuine treatment effects, and blinded interviewer administration, I judge **Low risk**, though this is a borderline call given the inherent limitation of unblinded participants for a self-report outcome (this is explicitly acknowledged by the authors as a limitation: "the study relied on participant self-report data that were uncorroborated by clinicians or objective observers").

*(Note: If a stricter interpretation is applied — i.e., that participant-reported outcomes in an unblinded design default to at least "Some concerns" per RoB2 guidance — this domain could alternatively be rated "Some concerns." This alternative rating is defensible given the explicit RoB2 guidance that participant-reported outcomes in unblinded trials should be judged as at least "Some concerns" unless there is strong reason to believe reporting was not influenced by knowledge of assignment.)*

## 5. Selective reporting

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

a. **Rationale for judgement:** The outcome, instrument, and analytic approach were pre-specified in the trial registry prior to unblinded data availability, and the reported result matches the pre-specified outcome/analysis without evidence of selective reporting among multiple outcome measures or analyses.

b. **Rationale/quotes:**
- Pre-specification: "The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under identifier NCT01024569, and all outcomes, hypotheses, and statistical analyses presented here were prespecified at the time the proposal was submitted to the federal government for funding consideration."
- The registry (NCT01024569) explicitly lists "Psychiatric Symptoms Recovery Using the Brief Symptoms Inventory (BSI)" as a **Primary Outcome**, specifically the "BSI Positive Symptom Score," measured at the same three time points reported in the publication (baseline, 8-weeks, 6-month follow-up), matching the published analysis exactly (signalling Q5.1 = Yes).
- Measurement selection: The publication reports both BSI Global Symptom Severity and Positive Symptom Total, and both are described in the Methods as intended outcomes ("Respondents are asked how much they were bothered... The BSI's Global Severity Index... The BSI Positive Symptom Total captures..."), rather than selectively reporting only a favorable subscale (signalling Q5.2 = No/Probably No).
- Analysis selection: The statistical method (Mixed Effects Random Regression / MIXREG) and covariate (study site) were specified in Methods before results, and the same model form was applied consistently across all outcomes reported (Table 3), with no indication of cherry-picking among multiple alternative analytic approaches for this specific outcome (signalling Q5.3 = No/Probably No).
- No discrepancy is apparent between the registered outcome definition/timeframe and the outcome as reported in the publication and results-posting on ClinicalTrials.gov, which independently corroborates the BSI Positive Symptom Score result (MIXREG estimate -1.16, p=.027), matching Table 3 in the publication.
