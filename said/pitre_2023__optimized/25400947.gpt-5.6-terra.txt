1. **bias due to randomization: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias because allocation concealment was not described and there was a substantial baseline imbalance in cervical effacement, a clinically important prognostic factor for delivery following threatened preterm labour.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states only that participants “were divided in two groups by simple random sampling.” This does not describe how a random allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated numbers) or whether allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants. In particular, there is no information on central randomization, numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or another adequate concealment method.

   Moreover, baseline cervical effacement differed markedly between groups: mean effacement was 35.93% (SD 18.07) in the MgSO4 group and 42.0% (SD 15.64) in the celecoxib group, with *P* ≤ 0.0001. Cervical effacement is directly related to the likelihood of early delivery and is therefore a key prognostic factor for the outcome. Under RoB 2, absent information on allocation concealment together with a baseline imbalance suggesting a problem with randomization leads to a high-risk judgement.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because blinding was incomplete or unclear, and there is insufficient information to determine whether knowledge of treatment allocation led to deviations arising from the trial context. The reported analysis appears to retain the randomized group totals, but this is not explicitly identified as an intention-to-treat analysis.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial is described as “a single blinded randomized clinical trial,” but the report does not state who was blinded. Given the visibly different interventions—intravenous MgSO4 infusion with toxicity monitoring versus oral celecoxib—carers and personnel delivering treatment were almost certainly aware of assigned treatment, and participants may also have been aware.

   The authors describe different monitoring procedures: women receiving MgSO4 were monitored for “urine output, deep tendon reflex, and respiratory rate,” whereas women receiving celecoxib were checked for “nausea and vomiting and skin rush and headache.” However, the report does not provide information on co-interventions, protocol deviations, treatment switching, or whether any deviations occurred because of awareness of allocation or other features of the trial context.

   For the effect of assignment to intervention, lack of blinding alone does not necessarily imply bias; deviations must have arisen because of the trial context and have affected the outcome. As these issues are not adequately reported, there are some concerns. The outcome table includes 300 participants per group, suggesting that all randomized participants were included, but the analysis is not explicitly described as intention-to-treat.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias because outcome data for delivery before/after 48 hours appear to have been available for all 600 randomized participants.

   b. **Rationale:** The study reports randomization of 600 women into two groups of 300. For the relevant outcome, Table 1 reports data totalling 300 participants in the MgSO4 group (240 + 60) and 300 in the celecoxib group (227 plus the reported post-48-hour group). Thus, the reported outcome data appear to cover the full randomized sample.

   There is no reported loss to follow-up, withdrawal, or exclusion from the analysis for this short-term, hospital-based outcome. Although Table 1 contains an apparent typographical inconsistency—the celecoxib “delivery after 48 hours” frequency is printed as 30 while the percentage is 24.3%, which would correspond to approximately 73/300—this does not indicate missing outcome data. It is an internal reporting/data-transcription issue rather than evidence of attrition.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias because delivery occurring after 48 hours is an objective outcome and was likely determined using the same clinical records and time threshold in both intervention groups.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome is based on whether delivery occurred before or after 48 hours following treatment initiation. Delivery timing is an objective, routinely recorded clinical event and requires little subjective judgement by an outcome assessor. The protocol applied the same maximum 48-hour treatment period to both groups.

   Although the report does not clearly state that outcome assessors were blinded, knowledge of treatment assignment is unlikely to affect the factual recording of whether and when delivery occurred. There is also no indication that different outcome definitions, thresholds, or ascertainment procedures were used between groups. Therefore, measurement bias is unlikely to be material for this outcome.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the report to establish that the reported result for delivery after 48 hours was selected without reference to the results.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies arrest of preterm labour for at least 48 hours as a relevant treatment outcome and reports the frequencies of delivery before and after 48 hours. However, it does not provide a dated statistical analysis plan specifying, before unblinded data were available, the precise outcome definition, analysis population, treatment-effect measure, handling of any missing data, and planned covariate adjustment.

   Several outcomes and analyses are reported, including duration of drug use/arrest of contractions, delivery before 48 hours, gestational age at delivery, cervical dilatation, and cervical effacement. Without a sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan, it cannot be excluded that the reported 48-hour result or its specific analysis was selected from multiple possible analyses or time-related outcomes. There is no direct evidence that selective reporting occurred, so a high-risk judgement is not warranted.
