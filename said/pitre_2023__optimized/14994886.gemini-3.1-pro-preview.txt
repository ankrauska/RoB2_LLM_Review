Based on the provided trial report and the RoB 2.0 guidance, here is the assessment of the risk of bias for the outcome of initial pain:

**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
* **a. Judgement:** The trial reports that patients were randomly selected for the intervention, but it does not provide details on the method of random sequence generation or whether the allocation sequence was concealed. 
* **b. Rationale:** The authors state, "The patients were randomly selected for insertion of the 0.014 or 0.016 inch wires." However, there is no information on how the randomization sequence was generated (e.g., computer random number generator) or whether allocation was concealed (e.g., sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes) to prevent selection bias by the enrolling personnel. 

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
* **a. Judgement:** Although the personnel delivering the intervention were aware of the wire size, the participants were likely unaware of the minute difference in wire thickness, and there is no evidence of deviations from the intended intervention that arose due to the trial context. All patients were analyzed.
* **b. Rationale:** The trial compared two very similar wires (0.014 and 0.016 inch NiTi), making it highly probable that participants were effectively blinded to their assigned group. There are no reported deviations from the protocol, and the study states that "The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients," indicating that an intention-to-treat analysis was successfully conducted with no post-randomization exclusions.

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
* **a. Judgement:** Outcome data were available for all randomized participants.
* **b. Rationale:** The publication explicitly states that "The questionnaires were completed by all 109 patients and returned at the following appointment." Therefore, there is no missing outcome data that could bias the results.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
* **a. Judgement:** The outcome was patient-reported, and the participants were likely unaware of the specific intervention they received, meaning their assessment of pain was not influenced by knowledge of the intervention.
* **b. Rationale:** Pain is a subjective, participant-reported outcome measured using a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), which is an appropriate and validated method. Because the difference between a 0.014 inch and a 0.016 inch wire is visually negligible to a patient, participants were practically blinded to the intervention. Therefore, their self-assessment of pain is unlikely to have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
* **a. Judgement:** There is no evidence of a pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial registration available to confirm that the reported analyses were planned before unblinded data were available.
* **b. Rationale:** While the study reports the outcomes specified in the methodology (initial pain, pain duration, pain regions, and pain relief consumption), there is no reference to a pre-registered protocol or analysis plan. Without a date-stamped protocol, it is impossible to definitively rule out that the reported results were selected from multiple eligible analyses or time points based on the significance of the findings.
