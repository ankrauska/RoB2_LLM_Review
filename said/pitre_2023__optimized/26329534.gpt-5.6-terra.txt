1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because the study is described as randomized, but neither the random-sequence generation method nor allocation concealment is reported. Baseline comparability cannot be meaningfully assessed for all randomized pharmacists because the reported demographic table includes only respondents (i.e., a post-randomization subset).

   b. **Rationale:** The authors state that “*Pharmacists who were randomized to the postal mail group*,” email group, or hybrid group, and describe a factorial stratification with 300 observations per subgroup. However, they do not state how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers) or how assignments were concealed before enrolment/allocation. Thus, signalling questions 1.1 and 1.2 are both **No information**.  
   
   Table 1 reports characteristics only among pharmacists who returned completed questionnaires. These are not baseline data for all randomized participants and therefore cannot establish whether randomized groups were balanced before the intervention. Accordingly, signalling question 1.3 is **No information**. Under RoB 2, absent information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**, unless there is evidence of a problem sufficient for high risk of bias.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of **assignment** to delivery method at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew whether they received a postal questionnaire, an email invitation, or a mailed postcard directing them to an online questionnaire; carers/personnel implementing the survey were also likely aware of delivery method. Thus, participants and personnel were not blinded. However, lack of blinding alone does not imply bias for the effect of assignment.  
   
   The delivery procedures, including reminders, were specified as part of each assigned strategy:
   - Postal-mail participants received the questionnaire and, if necessary, a second mailing and reminder postcard.
   - Email participants received an invitation and up to two subsequent email contacts.
   - Hybrid participants received mailed postcards containing the survey link and, if needed, another postcard.
   
   These follow-up contacts were planned components of the intervention strategies, rather than non-protocol deviations arising from the trial context. The report provides no indication that important non-protocol co-interventions or implementation failures occurred differentially between groups.  
   
   Response rates were analysed according to the assigned delivery groups, using the net number of delivered questionnaires as the denominator. The exclusion of undeliverable questionnaires is consistent with the stated estimand of response among eligible recipients; the undeliverable status itself does not represent a deviation from assigned delivery strategy. Therefore, an analysis broadly consistent with assignment-group analysis was used.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** For this outcome, non-return of a questionnaire is itself an observed outcome—non-response—not missing outcome data. The flow diagram gives the disposition of the full randomized sample:
   - Postal: 2,400 allocated; 139 undeliverable; 2,261 received survey; 474 complete responses.
   - Email: 2,400 allocated; all 2,400 received survey; 163 complete responses.
   - Hybrid: 2,400 allocated; 94 undeliverable; 2,306 received survey; 73 complete responses.
   
   The authors explicitly define response rate as “*the percentage of eligible respondents that returned completed questionnaires, with no missing data for any questions*” and calculate it using the number of delivered questionnaires as the eligible denominator. The numbers not returning a completed questionnaire are known and incorporated as non-response, rather than being excluded as unavailable outcome data.  
   
   Some returned questionnaires were incomplete (e.g., 52 of 526 returned postal questionnaires), but the prespecified outcome is the rate of **completed** questionnaires with no missing items. These submissions do not constitute missing data for the defined binary outcome of complete response: they are correctly classified as not meeting the complete-response outcome definition. There is therefore no important missing outcome-data problem for response rate.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The response-rate outcome was operationalized consistently as return of a “*completed questionnaire, with no missing data for any questions*.” The outcome is an objective administrative event: whether a complete questionnaire was received. It does not require subjective judgement by a participant or investigator about the success of the intervention.  
   
   Although the modes differed (postal paper questionnaire versus web questionnaire), the outcome definition—submission of a questionnaire with no missing responses—was the same across groups. The report also specifies how online responses were stored and how postal questionnaires were returned. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment was systematically more intensive in one group in a way that could artificially create differential response classification.  
   
   Knowledge of delivery assignment by participants or survey staff is unlikely to influence whether the study records a completed questionnaire as received. Therefore, the method was appropriate, ascertainment was comparable, and assessment was unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article clearly identifies response rate as a study outcome and defines it in advance in the Methods section. It reports response rates overall, by delivery method, and across the factorial subgroups of questionnaire length, item placement, and gender. Table 2 presents the response-rate results for all 24 delivery-method/length/item-placement/gender cells, and the text reports the planned chi-square comparison: “*Significant differences were detected only among delivery methods (χ² = 400.8, P < 0.01).*” This comprehensive reporting reduces concern that only favourable subgroup results were presented.
   
   However, low risk of bias requires confirmation that the data were analysed according to a sufficiently detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The article provides no protocol, statistical analysis plan, or registration information demonstrating this. The referenced trial-registration source was not available in the material provided here for verification. In addition, response could potentially have been analysed using more than one eligible definition (for example, complete questionnaires only versus all returned questionnaires); the article discusses both complete and incomplete returns, but does not provide a prospectively finalized plan establishing that the complete-questionnaire definition was selected before outcome data were known.
   
   There is no direct evidence that the reported complete-response result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable. Nonetheless, the absence of a verifiable prospective analysis plan leaves **some concerns** under RoB 2.
