1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the random sequence appears appropriate and measured baseline characteristics were reasonably balanced, but allocation concealment was not described.

   b. **Rationale:** The article states that participants were assigned “using a numbered list generated through the random permuted blocks method, applied by a statistician.” This supports use of a random allocation sequence. The statistician “was not involved in data collection or analysis,” but the report does not state whether the numbered list was held centrally, whether assignments were concealed in sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, or whether recruiters could foresee allocations before enrolment.

   Baseline characteristics were broadly balanced (e.g., age, sex, time from symptom onset, injury type), and baseline VAS values were similar: 5.7, 5.7 and 6.3 in the ultrasound, phonophoresis and iontophoresis groups, respectively. These data do not suggest a failure of randomization. Nevertheless, under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** All groups received their assigned physical-agent intervention together with the same co-interventions: standardized exercises and cryotherapy. The article specifies that “all participants completed 20 treatment sessions plus exercise therapy and cryotherapy” and that treatments were delivered by physiotherapists “following standardised protocols.” Thus, there is no reported evidence of differential non-protocol co-interventions, treatment crossover, or implementation failures arising from the trial context that would bias the effect of assignment.

   Blinding information is somewhat inconsistent: the publication calls the study “single-blind” and reports that outcome visits were conducted by “the same blinded rehabilitation doctor,” while the registry identifies the participant as the masked party. However, even if participants and treating physiotherapists were aware of treatment assignment, the report gives no indication that awareness caused deviations from the assigned protocols or unequal additional care between groups.

   The analysis included the initially assigned group sizes reported in the abstract (32, 33 and 34 participants), and the article states that all participants completed the 20 sessions. There is no indication of an as-treated, per-protocol, or post-randomization exclusion analysis. The reported group comparisons therefore appear compatible with an analysis of participants in their randomized groups.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial enrolled 99 participants and reports group sizes of 32, 33 and 34, which total 99. Importantly, it states that “all participants completed 20 treatment sessions plus exercise therapy and cryotherapy.” The outcome table presents post-treatment and 1-month VAS results for all three assigned groups, with no reported attrition, missing VAS assessments, or exclusions from outcome analysis.

   For this continuous outcome, the available information indicates that outcome data were available for all, or nearly all, randomized participants. There is no evidence of differential missingness or of reasons for missing outcome data related to pain severity.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Pain was measured using a patient-reported VAS, defined as a 0–10 scale from “no pain” to “the worst pain.” For a patient-reported pain outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor.

   The interventions were materially distinguishable: ultrasound and phonophoresis were applied for 5 minutes, whereas iontophoresis used galvanic direct current for 20 minutes. Consequently, participants could plausibly identify the intervention received, notwithstanding the registry’s designation of participant masking. The article does not explain how participant blinding was achieved or maintained given these visibly and physically different procedures.

   Knowledge of assigned treatment is likely to influence self-reported post-treatment pain, particularly where the interventions differ in intensity, duration, sensations and perceived sophistication. The primary result was measured immediately after a 20-session treatment course, a setting in which expectation and placebo-related effects are especially plausible. Therefore, for post-treatment VAS pain, it is likely that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The registry identifies VAS pain as the primary outcome: “Pain measure by the visual analogic scale pain score.” The publication reports VAS results at baseline, post-treatment and 1 month post-treatment, including the post-treatment result assessed here. Thus, the outcome domain specified in the registry was reported.

   However, the registry does not specify the measurement time point(s), detailed analytic method, whether final values or change-from-baseline values would be prioritized, covariate adjustment, or procedures for handling missing data. No dated statistical analysis plan is available. The publication reports several outcomes (VAS, CMS and DASH), at multiple time points, and uses both between-group analyses and within-group change analyses. This creates opportunities for selection among eligible outcome measurements and analyses.

   There is no clear evidence that the post-treatment VAS result was selected on the basis of its statistical significance or direction, since the publication presents the VAS results at both post-treatment and 1 month. Nonetheless, absence of a sufficiently detailed, pre-specified analysis plan finalized before outcome data were available means that low risk of bias cannot be confirmed.
