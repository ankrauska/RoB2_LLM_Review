**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
* **Judgement:** The trial reports that participants were "assigned at random," but it provides no information on the method of random sequence generation or whether allocation was concealed. While baseline characteristics do not show obvious major imbalances, the lack of methodological details raises some concerns.
* **Rationale:** The text states, "A total of 92 physician-diagnosed adult asthma patients... were assigned at random to three experimental groups and one comparison group." There is no description of how the randomization sequence was generated or concealed. The authors note, "The initial pretest analyses showed no statistically significant differences among the four study groups," suggesting randomization was likely somewhat successful, but insufficient details lead to a judgement of some concerns.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Although participants and facilitators were aware of the assigned intervention (blinding is not feasible for this type of educational intervention), there is no evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose due to the trial context that would likely affect the outcome. 
* **Rationale:** The study evaluated educational videos versus a pictorial pamphlet. The text notes, "We interviewed each patient alone... The interviews were facilitated by bilingual and bicultural experienced moderators." While blinding was not possible, the delivery of the educational materials appears to have been completed as intended during the sessions, and an intention-to-treat approach is appropriate for this context. 

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** The proportion of missing outcome data is small and unlikely to have a clinically relevant impact on the true results. 
* **Rationale:** Figure 1 ("Patient enrollment and attrition") and the text report that 92 patients were enrolled in the educational interventions and "85 completed the study." This represents a high retention rate (approximately 92%), meaning data were available for nearly all randomized participants, resulting in a low risk of bias.

**4. measurement of the outcome: some concerns**
* **Judgement:** The outcome assessors were likely aware of the intervention received. Since the inhaler technique was assessed using an observational checklist, there is some room for subjective judgement, which could potentially be influenced by knowledge of the intervention group. 
* **Rationale:** The inhaler technique was measured using a "standardized nine-step observational checklist, which was used by the facilitator to record proper use of different inhalers... The assessment of the inhaler techniques during the face-to-face interviews was judged by two observers (the interviewer and the research coordinator)". While the text states moderators "were not aware of the study hypothesis," they were likely aware of the group assignments. Because assessing physical technique requires some degree of observer judgement, there are some concerns about measurement bias.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
* **Judgement:** The trial registration lists "medication adherence" as the primary outcome, while "inhaler use technique" is not explicitly defined as a primary outcome in the registry, though it is reported in the paper. The lack of a fully detailed, pre-specified statistical analysis plan available before unblinding raises some concerns.
* **Rationale:** The ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT01474928) lists "medication adherence" as the primary outcome and does not explicitly list inhaler technique as an outcome measure, although the paper focuses heavily on it. Without a pre-specified, detailed statistical analysis plan confirming how the checklist scores would be analyzed and categorized, we cannot rule out selection of the reported analysis, leading to some concerns.
