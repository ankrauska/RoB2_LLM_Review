**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
* **Judgement:** There are some concerns regarding the baseline imbalances between the two groups.
* **Rationale:** The trial states, "Patients were randomly assigned (ratio 1:1) to receive either doxycycline or placebo. The randomisation list and the study medication were administered by the Charité, Universitätsmedizin, Berlin, Germany," indicating adequate sequence generation and central allocation concealment. However, due to the very small sample size (n=12), there are noticeable baseline differences between the groups (e.g., median age at onset of 58.0 in the doxycycline group versus 72.0 in the placebo group, and baseline MMSE scores of 21 versus 16, respectively). While these are likely chance imbalances typical in small trials, they are prognostically important for survival in CJD and raise some concerns.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** The trial was double-blinded, and appropriate analyses were conducted for the effect of assignment to intervention.
* **Rationale:** The study is described as a "randomised, single-centre, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II trial," meaning participants, carers, and personnel were unaware of the intervention assignments. One patient out of the 13 initially enrolled dropped out, but the survival analysis was conducted appropriately on the remaining 12 patients based on the groups to which they were randomized. There is no evidence of deviations from the intended intervention that arose due to the trial context.

**3. missing outcome data: some concerns**
* **Judgement:** One participant out of a very small randomized cohort dropped out and was excluded from the survival analysis.
* **Rationale:** The publication notes, "One patient who was randomised to the placebo group dropped out, so that 12 patients with sCJD were included in the reported survival analysis." In a trial with only 13 randomized individuals (6 in the placebo group), the loss of a single patient represents a substantial proportion of that study arm (~17%). Because the outcome is survival time in a rapidly fatal disease, dropping out could be related to the true value of the outcome (e.g., rapid deterioration), leading to some concerns regarding missing outcome data.

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** The outcome (survival/death) is objective and was measured appropriately.
* **Rationale:** The primary outcome is survival time, ending in death. This is an objective, observer-reported outcome not involving subjective judgment, making it highly unlikely that the ascertainment of death could have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention received. Furthermore, the trial was double-blinded.

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
* **Judgement:** There is a discrepancy between the primary outcome timepoint specified in the trial protocol and what was reported for the RCT.
* **Rationale:** The trial registration protocol (EudraCT 2006-003934-14) states the primary endpoint as "Überlebenszeit der Patienten von Symptombeginn an sein" (survival time of patients from symptom onset). However, in the published article, the authors note: "In the double-blinded randomised study, follow-up for survival starts with the randomisation... In the observational study... patients are followed up for survival from disease onset." While survival from randomization is standard for an RCT, this represents a deviation from the pre-specified analysis plan, raising some concerns that the analysis may have been altered after data collection began.
