# Risk of Bias Assessment (RoB 2.0): Agatston Score Outcome

**Important preliminary note:** This study is not a randomized trial in the conventional sense of comparing an experimental intervention against a comparator for a clinical/patient outcome. Patients were randomized to two *scanning collimation protocols* (Group A vs Group B), but the outcome of interest (Agatston score) was measured under multiple technical conditions (reconstruction interval and reconstruction increment) applied to the *same* underlying CT acquisition for each patient. This is fundamentally a technical/methodological comparison (largely within-patient, repeated-measures comparison of reconstruction methods) rather than a trial testing a causal intervention effect on a patient outcome. RoB 2.0 is designed for effect-of-intervention outcomes; applying it here is somewhat artificial, but I proceed as instructed, treating "assignment to collimation group" as the intervention and Agatston score as the outcome.

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The article states, "The patients were assigned to two groups at random with 20 patients each: in group A, collimation was 64 × 0.6 mm; in group B, it was 20 × 1.2 mm." No further detail is provided regarding the method of sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number table) or allocation concealment. There is no description of who performed the randomization, whether the allocation sequence was concealed from those enrolling patients, or how allocation was implemented. The text only states age was compared between groups ("The age between both groups was not significantly different (Mann-Whitney U test, p > 0.05)"), but no other baseline characteristics (sex distribution, heart rate, extent of coronary calcification, comorbidities) are compared statistically between groups. Given:
- No information on sequence generation method → cannot confirm "Yes"/"Probably yes" to randomness of sequence.
- No information on allocation concealment.
- Limited baseline comparison (only age reported), so cannot fully rule out imbalance.

This corresponds to "No information" on signalling questions 1.1 and 1.2, and "No information" for question 1.3 regarding most baseline variables (only age is reported). Per the RoB2 algorithm, "No information" on sequence and concealment defaults to "Some concerns."

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** This domain is unusual in this study because the "intervention" (collimation protocol: 64×0.6mm vs 20×1.2mm) is a technical scanning parameter applied deterministically by the scanner and does not depend on participant or clinician behavior, adherence, or awareness. There is no possibility of participants or personnel deviating from assigned collimation, since it is fixed by the acquisition protocol and cannot be altered by participant preference or clinician judgment after randomization: "The MDCT examinations were performed using a commercially available, 64-MDCT scanner...with a gantry rotation time of 330 milliseconds. The patients were assigned to two groups at random...in group A, the collimation was set to 64 × 0.6 mm, whereas in group B, the collimation was 20 × 1.2 mm."

- Blinding of participants/personnel to collimation setting is not clinically meaningful in this context, as it cannot influence care-seeking behavior, engagement, or non-protocol interventions like it might in a clinical drug/procedure trial.
- There is no evidence of any deviation from the assigned collimation protocol.
- All patients appear to have been analyzed according to their assigned collimation group; there is no mention of any switching between the 64×0.6mm and 20×1.2mm protocols.
- The analysis of the Agatston score outcome does not appear to have excluded patients for reasons related to the intervention (see below, missing data section for exclusions due to zero calcium, which is unrelated to collimation group assignment).

Given the deterministic, technical nature of the intervention with no possibility for behavioral deviation, this domain is judged low risk of bias.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** Four of the original 40 patients were excluded from all analyses: "Four patients had to be excluded from the study because no coronary calcium was detected on any of the reconstructions." This exclusion was applied post-randomization and was based on an outcome-related characteristic (absence of detectable calcium), though it is described as happening symmetrically (not clearly stated per group). The report does not specify whether these four exclusions were evenly distributed between Group A and Group B (e.g., "For group A (n = 18)... For group B (n = 18)..." implies 2 from each group of 20, suggesting balance, but this is not explicitly confirmed with reasons per group).

- The reason for exclusion (zero detectable calcium) is arguably not related to the *collimation* intervention groups differentially (unlikely to be influenced by collimation choice), and it affected both groups equally in absolute numbers (2 from each 20-patient group, based on n=18 reported for each group post-exclusion).
- However, exclusion of patients based on their outcome value (zero calcium detected) after randomization constitutes a form of post-randomization exclusion related to the outcome, and there is limited detail on whether this could plausibly bias comparisons, and the original ITT population's outcome data are not reported/imputed for these 4 patients.
- No sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the results to the exclusion of these individuals.
- Because the exclusion appears balanced (2 from each group of 20) and the reason (truly zero calcium) is plausibly unrelated to the intervention (collimation protocol should not cause "true" calcium presence/absence), the risk of bias from this exclusion is not judged as high, but the lack of explicit reporting of reasons/balance by group, absence of sensitivity analysis, and post-randomization nature of the exclusion raise some concern.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The Agatston score was measured using a semi-automated method with human adjudication: "These lesions were automatically identified and marked in color by the workstation. In a second step, a radiologist with 4 years of experience with coronary artery CT and a cardiologist with 11 years of experience with coronary artery CT differentiated between calcium and noise by placing regions of interest around the CAC."

- The outcome assessors (radiologist and cardiologist) were **not blinded** to the collimation group, since scan images inherently reveal the acquisition protocol/collimation (this would be essentially unblindable, as the images from 64×0.6mm vs 20×1.2mm collimation are visually distinguishable and the assessors were directly involved in reconstructing/reviewing the different intervals/increments).
- This outcome (Agatston score, based on region-of-interest placement to differentiate calcium from noise) involves an element of human judgment ("observer-reported outcome involving some judgment"), meaning assessment could, in principle, be influenced by knowledge of which collimation protocol was used.
- However, there is no obvious strong incentive, expectation, or hypothesis favoring one collimation group's Agatston score over the other's, since the study's aims were primarily to assess reconstruction interval/increment effects and objective quantification. This somewhat lowers the plausibility that awareness of collimation group influenced assessment substantially, but does not eliminate concern.
- The paper does not report any blinding procedures or masking of collimation type during scoring, nor does it discuss whether assessors were blinded to reconstruction parameters when scoring successive datasets, raising general concern about differential measurement given the semi-manual scoring process across 5 reconstruction intervals × 2 increments per patient.

Given the involvement of subjective judgment in the outcome assessment method and the absence of any blinding procedure or statement addressing this, but with no clear evidence that this influenced results in one direction, the judgement is "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** No trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan is referenced or available for this study (this is an imaging methodology study rather than a registered clinical trial), so a formal pre-registration comparison cannot be made. However, examining the reported methods and results:

- All pre-specified comparisons described in the Methods section appear to be fully reported in the Results: Agatston score and volumetric score are reported for both reconstruction increments (1.5mm and 3.0mm) and across the diastolic reconstruction interval range (50–70%), for both Group A and Group B, exactly as described in the Materials and Methods ("For each patient, five data sets were created throughout diastole...For each reconstruction, two data sets were calculated with a reconstruction increment of 3.0 and 1.5 mm, respectively...the mean Agatston scores and volumetric scores ± SD and the coefficient of variance were assessed").
- Both statistically significant (volumetric score) and non-significant (Agatston score) results are reported transparently, without evidence of suppression of unfavorable findings.
- Risk group percentile classifications, coefficients of variation, and correlation analyses are all reported as described in the methods.
- There is no indication of selective reporting of only favorable outcomes/analyses; results contrary to the study's apparent hypothesis (e.g., non-significant differences in Agatston score by reconstruction increment) are reported prominently, including in the abstract conclusion.

Given full concordance between planned and reported analyses (as far as can be determined from the single available source) and no evidence of selective outcome or analysis reporting, this domain is judged as low risk of bias.
