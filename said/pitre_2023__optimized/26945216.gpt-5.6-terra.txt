1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence was reported as computer-generated block randomization, but allocation concealment using envelopes was insufficiently described to establish that concealment was secure.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants were allocated using “**computerized block randomization**,” which supports a random allocation sequence. It also states that “**allocation group [was] concealed in envelops**.” However, there is no information that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed/tamper-proof, opened sequentially, and opened only after irrevocable enrolment. Under RoB 2, merely stating that envelopes were used does not provide sufficient assurance of adequate allocation concealment.

   Baseline pain intensity was broadly similar across groups (TENS 69.9, IFT 67.2, control 70.3 mm; *P* > 0.05). There was some imbalance in affected side (*P* = 0.015), but this alone does not clearly demonstrate a failure of randomization, particularly as group sizes were equal (35 per group) and there was no marked imbalance in baseline pain. Thus, the principal concern is incomplete reporting of concealment, rather than evidence that randomization was manipulated or failed.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** All participants received the intervention regimen corresponding to their allocated group: standard care alone, standard care plus TENS, or standard care plus IFT. The intervention procedures were protocolized, and the report states that “**a protocol and a study operation manual were developed**.” Standard care was delivered first, followed by the allocated electrical modality where applicable.

   The principal investigator delivered all treatment and was therefore aware of allocation. However, the study reports that “**participants were blind to the allocation to treatment groups after randomization**.” More importantly for this RoB 2 domain, there is no reported evidence that knowledge of allocation led to differential non-protocol co-interventions, failures in delivery arising from the trial context, or differential deviations that would affect pain intensity.

   All 105 randomized participants completed the study and were included in analysis: “**All completed the study and were entered into the analysis**.” The analyses retained the original three randomized groups, with no indication of as-treated or naïve per-protocol analyses or post-randomization exclusions. Therefore, the analysis was appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to the interventions.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data for pain intensity appear to have been available for all randomized participants. The CONSORT flow chart shows 105 participants randomized, with 35 in each group, and the results state: “**All completed the study and were entered into the analysis**.”

   Table 2 reports pain data for all three groups with *n* = 35 per group. No loss to follow-up, withdrawals, missing pain measurements, exclusions, or imputation procedures were reported. Thus, missing outcome data could not plausibly have materially affected the pain-intensity results.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Pain intensity was a participant-reported, subjective outcome measured using a 100-mm visual analogue scale: “**Pain intensity over the affected upper trapezius at rest was recorded using a 100-mm visual analog scale**.” For such an outcome, the participant is the outcome assessor.

   Although the authors state that participants were blinded to treatment allocation, convincing blinding is unlikely for comparisons involving the control group. The control group received standard care only, whereas TENS and IFT participants received an additional 20 minutes of electrical stimulation with visibly different equipment and electrode arrangements. The authors themselves acknowledge “**not using a placebo for the patients of the control group**” as a limitation. Participants receiving no electrical stimulation could reasonably infer that they were in the control group, while those receiving TENS or IFT could recognize they were receiving an additional active modality.

   Knowledge of receiving an electrical treatment is likely to influence self-reported pain through expectation, placebo, or disappointment effects. This is particularly important because the intervention comparisons included standard care alone versus standard care plus an electrical modality, and because pain was assessed immediately after treatment as well as during short-term follow-up. Therefore, assessment of pain was likely influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study reports pain intensity at the measurement occasions described in the Methods: baseline, immediately after the first treatment, before the eighth treatment, and 1 week after the eighth treatment. Table 2 presents results for all of these time points, which reduces concern that one explicitly described pain time point was omitted.

   However, there is no accessible pre-specified statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the reported pain analysis—change from baseline at each post-baseline time point, compared using one-way ANOVA with Tukey tests—was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The publication describes the study as registered with the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry, but the available information does not provide enough detail from the registry to verify the pre-specified pain scale, time points, analysis metric (change versus final values), comparison structure, and multiplicity approach.

   Because pain could have been analysed in several eligible ways (e.g., final values versus change scores; different post-treatment time points; various pairwise contrasts; adjusted versus unadjusted analyses), and because a prospectively finalized analysis plan cannot be confirmed, there are some concerns regarding possible selection of the reported pain result.
