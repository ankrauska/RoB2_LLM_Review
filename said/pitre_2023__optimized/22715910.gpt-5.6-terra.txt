**Outcome assessed:** inhaler use technique (standardized nine-step observational checklist), assessed from baseline to the 3-month post-intervention follow-up. The assessment considers the effect of assignment to the randomized educational format (video-based interventions versus pictorial pamphlet), regardless of adherence.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that eligible participants “were randomly assigned” to three video groups and one pamphlet group, with broadly similar numbers allocated to each group (20–22 participants per group). However, it provides no description of how the random sequence was generated and no information on allocation concealment.

   The publication states: “Eligible subjects were randomly assigned to one of the three experimental groups … and one comparison group.” The trial registry similarly identifies the allocation as “RANDOMIZED.” These statements support that randomization was intended, but are insufficient to establish that a genuinely random sequence was generated or that enrolment personnel could not foresee allocations.

   There is no clear evidence of an important baseline imbalance suggesting manipulation of allocation. However, detailed baseline characteristics by randomized group are not reported; Table 1 mostly reports characteristics for the 85 completers overall. Therefore, baseline comparability cannot be fully assessed. Under RoB 2, absence of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns** where no baseline imbalance clearly indicates a problem.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew whether they viewed one or two videos or read a pamphlet. The assigned educational formats were therefore not plausibly blinded. The registry describes the study as “SINGLE” masked with the participant masked, but this is inconsistent with the nature of the visibly different interventions and is not supported by the article.

   The article reports that interviews were conducted by “bilingual and bicultural experienced moderators from the same community who were not aware of the study hypothesis.” This reduces concern that assessors consciously promoted the hypothesized intervention, but does not establish that facilitators, carers, or intervention deliverers were unaware of allocation.

   Importantly, for the requested estimand—the effect of assignment—lack of blinding is problematic only if it caused deviations from the assigned intervention because of the trial context, which then affected inhaler technique and were unbalanced between groups. The report does not describe contamination, differential extra asthma teaching, or other non-protocol co-interventions arising because of trial participation. However, it also does not provide enough information to rule these out.

   The analyses appear to retain participants in their allocated groups for the analyses of intervention group and time effects. Participants with missing follow-up data were not included, but this is addressed primarily under missing outcome data; a modified intention-to-treat analysis excluding missing outcomes is considered acceptable for this RoB 2 domain. Overall, uncertainty about trial-context deviations supports **some concerns**, rather than high risk.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   e. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   f. **Rationale:** Ninety-two patients entered the educational intervention, but 85 completed the study. The flow diagram states that “3 were excluded and 4 discontinued intervention,” corresponding to seven participants without complete follow-up information. Thus, inhaler-technique data at follow-up were available for approximately 92% of randomized participants.

   The report states that “92 participated in the interventions” and that “85 completed the study.” Analyses of inhaler technique have denominator degrees of freedom consistent with analysis of a reduced sample, for example, the time effect is reported with “df = 1 and 71.”

   For a continuous observational score, 92% outcome availability is not clearly sufficient to conclude that missing data could not have materially affected the result. The reasons for exclusion/discontinuation and their distribution across the four randomized groups are not reported. No sensitivity analysis or other analysis addressing the possible impact of missing inhaler-technique data is described.

   Missing technique assessments could plausibly be related to participants’ underlying capability, motivation, health status, or engagement with asthma self-management. However, the available information does not establish that missingness was likely related to the true outcome value, nor that it differed by intervention group. This leads to **some concerns** rather than high risk of bias.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Inhaler technique was assessed using a stated standardized nine-step observational checklist, which is an appropriate method for measuring this outcome. The article explains that the checklist was used “to record proper use of different inhalers,” with scores from 0 to 9 depending on the number of correctly completed steps. Therefore, there is no indication that the measurement instrument itself was inappropriate.

   Measurement procedures appear comparable between groups: all participants underwent in-person interviews and observed inhaler-technique assessment at the same scheduled study stages. The article also reports that technique “was judged by two observers (the interviewer and the research coordinator),” which provides some procedural safeguard.

   However, the report does not state that the interviewer or research coordinator assessing inhaler technique was blinded to the educational format received. Since the intervention was a video or pamphlet specifically intended to improve inhaler use, assessors may have been able to identify, or could have asked about, participants’ assigned materials. Inhaler-technique scoring using a checklist is more structured and less subjective than many clinical assessments, but still entails observer judgement regarding whether each procedural step was correctly performed.

   The article’s statement that facilitators “were not aware of the study hypothesis” mitigates the likelihood of deliberate differential assessment, but it does not demonstrate blinding to allocation. Thus, knowledge of intervention could have influenced scoring, although such influence is not clearly likely. This warrants **some concerns**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial registration record does not provide a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan for inhaler use technique. Its listed primary outcome is “medication adherence,” while inhaler technique is not explicitly listed as a primary or secondary outcome. The registry also gives only broad outcome time frames and does not specify the nine-step inhaler-technique score, the selected analysis, the contrast of interest, covariate adjustment, or which follow-up time point would be primary.

   The published article reports several measurement occasions: baseline, an immediate post-intervention assessment, a 3-month post-intervention follow-up, and a telephone assessment six months later. Yet it explicitly states: “This article summarizes the analyses from pretest and 3-month post-intervention follow-up data.” The report also describes multiple potential analyses, including ANOVA, ANCOVA, adjustment for age, sex, ethnicity and educational level, and interaction analyses.

   There is no accessible pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available, and the reported materials do not permit verification that the reported inhaler-technique result was selected from multiple time points or analyses without regard to its findings. Nevertheless, there is no direct evidence that the reported 3-month inhaler-technique result was selected because it was favourable or statistically significant. Therefore, RoB 2 supports a judgement of **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.
