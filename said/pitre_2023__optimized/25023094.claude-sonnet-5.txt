# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment
**Outcome: Pain (Visual Analogue Scale, VAS)**
**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)**

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- *Sequence generation:* The authors report "sex-stratified randomization," which implies a random component within strata (probably yes to randomness). No further detail on the specific mechanistic method (e.g., computer-generated numbers) is given, but stratified randomization is generally implemented with an underlying random element.
- *Allocation concealment:* "The distribution of subjects to the two treatment groups was performed using sex-stratified randomization, and the allocation sequence was blinded through the use of sealed envelopes." While details (opacity, sequential numbering, tamper-proofing) are not explicitly confirmed, the description supports a judgement of probably yes for concealment.
- *Baseline imbalance:* Table 1 demonstrates no statistically significant difference between groups on the pre-test VAS score (p = 0.421) or on age or sex distribution (p = 1.000). Although some joint-mobility variables showed borderline p-values (0.05–0.09), these are not related to the pain/VAS outcome being assessed here, and the authors explicitly state, "we believe that these differences did not affect the comparability of groups, as both groups evolved similarly." No evidence indicates a problem specific to the randomization process for the pain outcome.

Combining: allocation concealed, no important baseline imbalance for VAS, sequence probably random → **Low risk of bias**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:**
- *Participant blinding:* The study is described as one "with blinding of patients," and the control group received a placebo (sham) ultrasound identical in appearance/procedure to active ultrasound. Thus, participants were likely unaware of their allocation (2.1 = No/Probably No).
- *Personnel blinding:* The authors explicitly acknowledge a limitation: "The blinding of the physiotherapist who applied the treatment was not possible, which constitutes another limitation of the study." Thus, carers/people delivering the intervention were aware of group assignment (2.2 = Yes).
- *Deviations arising from trial context:* Because personnel were unblinded, there is potential for the physiotherapist administering treatment to behave differently between groups (e.g., differential intensity of massage, exercise supervision, or reassurance) in ways inconsistent with protocol. However, the article provides no explicit information indicating that such deviations actually occurred or that they differed systematically between the experimental and control groups. This corresponds to a "No information" response for signalling question 2.3.
- *Analysis:* "The analysis was done by intention to treat," and no participants were lost to follow-up ("During the study no patient was lost or dropped out"), supporting an appropriate ITT analysis (2.6 = Yes).

Because personnel delivering the intervention were unblinded and there is no information about whether this led to protocol-inconsistent deviations affecting the pain outcome, but an appropriate (ITT) analysis was used, the domain is rated as **Some concerns** rather than "Low" (which would require assurance that no such deviations occurred) or "High" (which would require evidence that unbalanced deviations likely affected the outcome).

---

## 3. Missing outcome data

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- The authors explicitly state: **"During the study no patient was lost or dropped out."**
- The flow diagram (Figure 1) confirms that all 54 randomized participants (27 per group) were followed through pre-test, post-test at session 5, post-test at session 10, and discharge (session 25), with complete VAS data reported for all participants in Tables 1–3.
- Since outcome data were available for all randomized participants, missingness could not have biased the result.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
- The VAS is a participant-reported outcome; therefore, the "outcome assessor" is effectively the patient. Because the trial used a genuine placebo (sham) ultrasound and is described as having "blinding of patients," participants were likely unaware of their treatment allocation. This minimizes the risk that self-reported pain was influenced by the knowledge of intervention received.
- Additionally, the study explicitly states there was a "blind assessor" who collected outcome measurements (VAS, algometer) using standardized instruments and protocol at fixed timepoints (pre-test, session 5, session 10, discharge), further supporting low risk of differential measurement/ascertainment between groups.
- There is no indication that the method of measuring pain (VAS) differed between groups or was applied inconsistently.
- Because participants (the true "assessors" for VAS) were blinded and the outcome assessor administering the measurement was also blinded, knowledge of intervention assignment is unlikely to have influenced the recorded pain scores.

---

## 5. Selective reporting

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:**
- The trial reports VAS pain outcomes at multiple consistent timepoints (pre-test, post-test at session 5 [implied], post-test at session 10, and at discharge/session 25) in a manner that appears complete across both groups (Tables 1–3), with no evidence of omitted or partially reported pain data.
- However, no statistical analysis plan or trial protocol is referenced in the article, and there is no explicit indication that the analyses reported (Student's t-test, Mann-Whitney U, Welch's t-test — chosen "as appropriate" based on normality testing) were pre-specified before unblinded data were available. The flexible, post hoc selection of statistical test type based on data distribution ("we then tested... using the Student t-test... or the Mann-Whitney U-test, as appropriate") raises some uncertainty as to whether analytic choices could have been influenced by the pattern of results, although this is a common and defensible practice (choice driven by normality, not by results/significance).
- Without access to a pre-registered protocol or statistical analysis plan confirming that the timing, scale, and analytic approach for the VAS outcome were specified in advance of unblinded data, it is not possible to fully rule out selective analytic choices.
- Given the absence of a retrievable pre-specified analysis plan/protocol confirming timing and methods for the VAS outcome, but no overt evidence of selection based on results, the domain is judged as **Some concerns** rather than "Low" or "High."
