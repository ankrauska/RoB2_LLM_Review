# Risk of Bias Assessment: MADIT-CRT Trial
**Outcome:** All-cause death or nonfatal heart failure events
**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Patients were "randomly assigned in a 3:2 ratio" and "stratified according to clinical center and ischemic status with the use of an algorithm that ensured near balance in each stratum." Although the exact method of allocation concealment is not explicitly described, this was a large, well-resourced multicenter trial (110 centers, sponsored by industry with formal data coordination centers), and there is no indication of an inadequately concealed allocation process (e.g., no alternation, no predictable sequence). Baseline characteristics (Table 1) were well balanced between groups (age, sex, ejection fraction, QRS duration, comorbidities, medications), with no evidence of clinically important imbalances suggesting a problem with randomization. Given the size and rigor of the trial infrastructure, a judgement of "Probably yes" is warranted for both random sequence generation and allocation concealment, and there is no evidence of baseline imbalance suggesting subversion of randomization.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** 
- **Blinding:** This was an open-label trial. "The treating physicians were aware of study-group assignments," and patients/personnel were clearly aware of intervention assignment (CRT device implantation is not blindable in the same way as a drug). The masking field in the registry confirms "masking: NONE."
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** There is direct evidence of deviations related to awareness of assignment affecting the heart-failure component of the composite outcome: "The diagnosis of heart failure, which was made by physicians who were aware of study-group assignments, required signs and symptoms consistent with congestive heart failure that was responsive to intravenous decongestive therapy..." The authors themselves acknowledge this as a potential source of bias: "It is possible that the investigators' knowledge of study-group assignment contributed in some way to the lower frequency of heart failure in the CRT–ICD group." This suggests that non-blinded assessment of the heart failure component of the composite (i.e., decisions to treat/hospitalize, which are unconscious/conscious threshold-lowering behavior in an unblinded context) could have been influenced by the trial context, favoring the CRT arm. However, all-cause death (which is objective) is not susceptible to this bias, and the effect of unblinding may not have been substantial given the size of the treatment effect and consistency across many subgroups. There were also crossovers reported (91 patients crossed from ICD-only to CRT–ICD, 82 the reverse), but these did not arise from failure of concealment (mainly due to technical failure of lead placement or physician-driven crossover after events), and are relevant more to the per-protocol effect than the ITT effect.
- **Appropriate analysis:** "Data analysis was performed according to the intention-to-treat principle," and all randomized patients were included and analyzed in their assigned groups.

Because participants/personnel were unblinded and there is direct textual evidence (from the authors) that this may have influenced ascertainment/diagnosis of the heart-failure component of the outcome (though not likely death), but no clear evidence this substantially biased the composite result, this domain is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low" or "High."

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Outcome data (death or heart failure event, whichever came first) were available for essentially all randomized patients. The article states: "A total of 44 patients in the CRT–ICD group (4.0%) and 55 in the ICD-only group (7.5%) declined to continue participating in the study, were withdrawn by a physician, or were lost to follow-up." Because the primary outcome is a time-to-event (death or first HF event) endpoint, and the trial was event-driven with active follow-up through a common termination date, and analysis was by intention-to-treat including all 1820 randomized patients, the amount of missing outcome data does not appear large enough to bias the effect estimate. There is no evidence that missingness differed systematically between groups in a way likely related to the true outcome (the modest imbalance in withdrawal, 4.0% vs. 7.5%, is unlikely to be large enough to materially bias this common, well-ascertained composite outcome with 372 events observed).

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The outcome is a composite of all-cause death (an objective outcome, unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of treatment assignment) and nonfatal heart-failure events (which required clinical judgement and were diagnosed by unblinded treating physicians). The report states plainly: "Heart-failure events were diagnosed by physicians who were aware of the treatment assignments," although "they were adjudicated by a committee that was unaware of assignments." Even with blinded adjudication, the initial trigger for a heart-failure diagnosis/treatment decision (an "outcome that reflects decisions made by the intervention provider," e.g., decision to administer decongestive therapy or hospitalize) was made by unblinded personnel, and such decisions could plausibly be influenced by the awareness of assignment (e.g., a lower threshold to treat/diagnose HF symptoms in the ICD-only, non-CRT arm, or differential clinical suspicion). The authors themselves raise this concern in the Discussion: "It is possible that the investigators' knowledge of study-group assignment contributed in some way to the lower frequency of heart failure in the CRT–ICD group." Given this direct acknowledgment of potential influence on a component of the outcome that made up the majority of events (318 of 372 primary events were heart-failure events), but blinded adjudication provides some mitigating protection, this domain is judged as "Some concerns" (not "High," because of the blinded adjudication committee, and not "Low," given the unblinded initial diagnosis/decision process for the dominant component of the composite).

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The trial was registered prospectively (NCT00180271, registered 2005-09-16) prior to the completion of enrollment (2004-2008) and results posted, with the primary outcome ("Mortality From Any Cause or First Heart Failure (HF) Event") clearly pre-specified in the registry and matching the outcome reported in the publication ("primary end point was death from any cause or a nonfatal heart-failure event"). The statistical analysis plan, including the group-sequential design, stopping rules, and stratified Cox proportional-hazards/log-rank analysis, was pre-specified as noted in the design paper (reference 9, published in 2005 before trial completion) and described in detail in the Methods. The numerical result for the primary outcome (HR 0.66; 95% CI, 0.52–0.84; P=0.001) is consistent between the registry results and the published article. There is no indication that this particular result was selected from among multiple alternative analyses or outcome definitions based on the results obtained; the composite primary endpoint, its definition, and the statistical approach were specified in advance and reported as pre-specified. Therefore, there is no evidence of selective reporting for this specific result.
