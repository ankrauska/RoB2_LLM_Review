1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because the sequence-generation method was adequate, and reported baseline characteristics do not indicate an important randomization failure, but allocation concealment was not described.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors state that “a random number table was produced through Microsoft Excel” and that patients “were divided into a control group and a therapy group following the random number table.” This supports a random allocation sequence. The groups were reasonably similar in the reported baseline variables, including age, sex, Child–Pugh score, portal-vein width, variceal characteristics, and active oesophageal bleeding.

   However, there is no information on how the random-number sequence was implemented or concealed from personnel enrolling patients (e.g., central randomization, sequential opaque sealed envelopes, or independent allocation). Under RoB 2, absence of information about allocation concealment leads to **some concerns** when there is no baseline evidence indicating a randomization problem.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention strategies at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to simultaneous embolization plus EVL versus embolization with planned EVL two months later—not the effect of perfect adherence to either intervention.

   Participants and treating clinicians were necessarily aware of treatment allocation because the interventions differed in timing and procedures. Nevertheless, the report does not indicate deviations caused by the trial context, such as differential use of non-protocol co-interventions because clinicians or patients knew the allocation. Somatostatin was administered in both groups: “Somatostatin was administered before and after the operation” in the therapy group, and “Somatostatin was also administered before and after the operation” in the control group.

   The emergency-hemostasis analysis used all randomized participants as denominators: 90/90 in the therapy group and 72/82 in the control group. Thus, the reported comparison corresponds to an intention-to-treat-type analysis of the assigned strategies. Although the 10 control-group patients with uncontrolled hemorrhage subsequently received EVL as rescue treatment, this occurred after failure of initial emergency hemostasis and therefore does not appear to alter the emergency-hemostasis result being assessed. There is no evidence that post-randomization exclusions or an as-treated/per-protocol analysis were used.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data for emergency hemostasis appear available for all randomized participants. The article reports emergency-hemostasis results using the complete randomized denominators: “Hemorrhaging was controlled in all 90 patients in the therapy group” and “in the control group, hemorrhaging was controlled in 72 out of 82 patients.”

   There is no indication that participants were lost to follow-up, withdrawn, excluded, or otherwise missing from the emergency-hemostasis analysis. Therefore, missing outcome data could not plausibly have had an important effect on the reported result.

4. **measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Emergency hemostasis was assessed clinically/endoscopically as whether “hemorrhaging was controlled.” The outcome is clinically relevant and direct visualization of active bleeding or cessation of bleeding is reasonably objective. There is no indication that different measurement instruments, thresholds, or follow-up procedures were systematically used in the two groups.

   However, neither participants nor treating clinicians could have been blinded, and no blinded independent outcome adjudication is described. The report does not provide a detailed, pre-specified operational definition of emergency hemostasis or specify whether assessment was performed by an independent assessor. The determination that bleeding was “controlled” can involve some clinical judgement, particularly regarding ongoing or recurrent bleeding and the decision that rescue treatment is required. Knowledge of the assigned intervention could therefore have influenced outcome assessment, although the available information does not establish that such influence was likely or substantial. This warrants **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication reports the outcome of interest and gives numerical results for both groups (90/90 versus 72/82), so there is no direct evidence that the emergency-hemostasis result was selectively omitted or that a clearly more favourable result was chosen from among reported alternatives.

   However, the article does not report a protocol or statistical analysis plan finalized before access to unblinded outcome data. The trial-registration information referenced in the question was not available in the material supplied here to verify prospectively specified outcome definitions, time points, or analysis methods. In addition, “emergency hemostasis” is not operationally defined in sufficient detail to exclude alternative definitions or analyses. Since multiple outcome definitions or analytic choices could have been possible and pre-specified analysis intentions cannot be confirmed, RoB 2 indicates **some concerns**.
