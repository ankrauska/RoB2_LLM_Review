1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The trial reports an appropriate random sequence-generation method: patients “were randomized to telephone contact or no telephone contact using a **computer-generated list of random numbers**.” The groups were equal in size (15 per group), and the authors report that “**Baseline characteristics were similar for both groups**,” with no evident indication in the report of an important baseline imbalance.  
   
   However, there is no information on allocation concealment—e.g., whether the computer-generated sequence was held centrally, administered by an independent party, or concealed in sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes until enrolment. Since the method used to prevent recruiters from foreseeing the next assignment is not reported, the randomization-process domain has **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to telephone follow-up versus no telephone follow-up at baseline.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants and the pharmacist delivering the intervention were necessarily aware of assignment: the intervention consisted of weekly pharmacist telephone contact for 12 weeks, compared with no telephone contact. Nevertheless, for the assignment-effect estimand, lack of blinding is problematic only if it caused deviations from the intended interventions because of the trial context that could affect compliance.  
   
   The article does not report important departures from the allocated intervention or differential non-protocol co-interventions attributable to trial participation. The weekly calls were the intended experimental intervention, not a deviation from it. The report also states that “**none of whom was lost to follow-up**,” and results are presented for the randomized groups (15 telephone-contact and 15 no-contact patients). There is no indication that participants were analysed according to treatment actually received rather than their randomized group, nor of post-randomization exclusions. Thus, the available evidence is consistent with an intention-to-treat comparison of assignment to the intervention.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** The report explicitly states: “**Thirty patients were enrolled in the study, none of whom was lost to follow-up.**” Compliance was assessed at 6 and 12 weeks using pill and packet counts and at 1 and 2 years through pharmacy refill records. There is no reported missingness in compliance data, differential follow-up, or exclusion of participants due to unavailable compliance outcomes.  
   
   Although the report does not provide a detailed participant flow diagram, its explicit statement that no participant was lost to follow-up, together with reporting of long-term refill-record outcomes, supports the judgement that outcome data were available for all or nearly all randomized participants.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Compliance was measured using relatively objective methods: “**pill and packet counts**” at the 6- and 12-week visits and pharmacy “**refill information**” at 1 and 2 years. The compliance thresholds were defined in advance in the methods: patients returning more than 20% of prescribed medication or filling fewer than 80% of prescriptions were classified as noncompliant.  
   
   The same outcome-assessment methods and thresholds appear to have been applied in both randomized groups at comparable time points. Although personnel conducting pill/packet counts may have known group assignment, pill/packet counts and pharmacy refill records leave limited scope for subjective judgement to be influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment. The statement that pill and packet counts were “**not performed within the patient’s view**” also reduces the opportunity for patients to influence the count at the assessment visit. Therefore, there is no important evidence that measurement or ascertainment differed between groups or was materially influenced by awareness of the allocated intervention.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The publication describes planned compliance assessments at four time points—6 weeks, 12 weeks, 1 year, and 2 years—and reports short- and long-term compliance findings. This is reassuring, and there is no clear evidence that a favourable time point or only one of the two medications was selectively reported. The report presents results for both lovastatin and colestipol and discusses both short- and long-term outcomes.  
   
   However, no pre-specified statistical analysis plan is available in the supplied material, and the referenced registration record is not provided in the information available here. Consequently, it cannot be confirmed that the particular reported compliance analyses—including the selected compliance metrics, thresholds, time points, and statistical analyses—were specified before unblinded outcome data were available. In accordance with RoB 2, absence of a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan leads to **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.
