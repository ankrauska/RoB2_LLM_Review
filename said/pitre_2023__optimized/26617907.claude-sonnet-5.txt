# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Surgical Time
## Comparison: Percutaneous microwave ablation (Group A) vs. Laparoscopic resection (Group B)
## Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise because although the study reports use of a "random number table" (an adequate method of sequence generation), there is no information provided about allocation concealment. Without evidence of concealment, it cannot be ruled out that foreknowledge of assignment could have influenced enrollment or group assignment, particularly given this is a surgical trial where blinding of the allocator is often difficult to achieve and rarely reported in such reports.

**b. Rationale:** The article states: "Using the random number table method, 90 cases were divided into group A and group B (n=45)." This supports a "Yes" answer to signalling question 1.1 (random sequence generation). However, there is no mention of any allocation concealment mechanism (e.g., sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, central randomization). This leads to "No information" on signalling question 1.2. Baseline characteristics (age, sex, tumor size, nodule number) appear balanced between groups ("The two groups did not show significant differences in general information (P>0.05)"), so there is no indication from baseline imbalance of a problem with randomization (signalling question 1.3: No). Per the RoB 2 algorithm: Random sequence = Yes/Probably Yes; Allocation concealment = No information; Baseline imbalance = No → this combination maps to "Some concerns."

---

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** This is a high risk of bias because participants, and especially trial personnel/surgeons performing the intervention, could not be blinded due to the fundamentally different nature of the two interventions (percutaneous ablation vs. laparoscopic surgery). Surgical time, as an outcome, is directly determined by the operating personnel and is highly susceptible to differences in how procedures are conducted, timed, and reported based on knowledge of which intervention is being performed. There is no indication of any measures taken to standardize measurement of surgical time or prevent awareness from influencing conduct.

**b. Rationale:** By nature of the interventions being compared (percutaneous microwave ablation vs. laparoscopic resection), it is impossible to blind the surgeons/interventionalists performing the procedures — they are aware of which technique they are using in real time. The article provides no evidence of blinding of personnel: "Cases in group A were treated by modified percutaneous microwave ablation..." and "Laparoscopic resection was performed for group B" describe fundamentally different procedures conducted by aware personnel. Because personnel were aware of intervention assignment (Y to signalling question 2.2), and there is no information suggesting the trial context led to non-protocol deviations, but surgical time is an outcome intrinsically tied to how the procedure was performed and reported by aware personnel, deviations related to the trial context affecting this outcome cannot be excluded. Given the lack of any explanation of standardized surgical time definitions (e.g., start/stop points) across the very different procedure types, and no blinding was possible or attempted, the risk that awareness of intervention influenced conduct/reporting of this outcome is high. This maps to "High risk of bias" per the algorithm for question 2.1/2.2 = Yes, with no mitigating information on deviations being balanced or unlikely to affect the outcome.

### 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as surgical time is a procedural, intraoperatively recorded variable that would be available for essentially all participants who underwent their assigned procedure. There is no indication of missing data for this outcome.

**b. Rationale:** Table 1 reports surgical time data for all 45 patients in Group A and all 45 patients in Group B (n=45 in each group, consistent with numbers randomized), with no indication of exclusions, dropouts, or missing values for this outcome. The signalling question "Were data for this outcome available for all, or nearly all, participants randomized?" can be answered "Yes." No further signalling questions in this domain need to be answered, and the result is judged to be at low risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

---

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise because surgical time is an outcome that could plausibly be measured or ascertained differently between intervention groups given the different nature of the two procedures, and the outcome assessors (the surgical/procedural teams themselves, who record start/stop times) were not blinded — indeed, blinding was impossible. However, this outcome is relatively objective (a recorded time interval) with limited room for subjective judgement, which somewhat reduces the concern.

**b. Rationale:** The measurement of surgical time depends on defining start and end points of the procedure, which could differ inherently between a percutaneous ablation procedure and a laparoscopic surgical resection (e.g., whether time includes anesthesia induction, positioning, ablation confirmation via contrast-enhanced ultrasound, etc.). The article does not specify a standardized definition of "surgical time" applied identically across both procedure types: "The needle was inserted under CT guidance. The tumors were ablated successively..." versus "An arc incision measuring 1 cm was made... After exploration... trocars were inserted... and the tumors were resected." Since outcome assessors (the operating teams) were necessarily aware of intervention assignment (unblinded, as blinding is not feasible in this surgical comparison), and the outcome, while largely objective, still involves some potential for differential ascertainment (question 4.2: could differ between groups — plausibly "Yes" or at least "No information"), this raises "Some concerns." This is not elevated to "High risk" because surgical time is a routinely and objectively recorded clinical measure (e.g., from anesthesia/procedure records) with limited scope for the awareness of group assignment to distort the recorded value once a consistent definition is applied.

---

### 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as surgical time was a pre-specified outcome explicitly described in the methods as a criterion for efficacy assessment, and the result is fully reported in the paper in a manner consistent with a single, pre-specified measurement and analysis, with no evidence of selection from multiple alternative analyses or measurements.

**b. Rationale:** The Methods section states: "Surgical time, intraoperative blood loss and incidence of complications were recorded for the two groups," and this is reported straightforwardly in Table 1 with a single mean ± SD value and single t-test comparison per group, with no evidence of multiple time-point measurements, multiple analytic approaches, or selective subgroup reporting for this variable. No trial registration record was identified/available (per the doc_id provided for trial registration) to compare against the published outcomes for this specific trial, limiting ability to fully verify a pre-specified analysis plan; however, in the absence of any indication of multiple eligible measurements or analyses for surgical time (a single, unambiguous, objectively defined variable), and given straightforward, single-method reporting, there is no signal suggestive of selective reporting for this outcome. This corresponds to "No information" on signalling question 5.1 but "No/Probably no" on questions 5.2 and 5.3, which maps to "Low risk of bias" (or at minimum "Some concerns" if a stricter interpretation of absent registration information is applied) — here judged as **Low risk of bias** given the single, objective nature of this measurement with no plausible alternative eligible measures/analyses.
