1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns arise because the method used to generate the allocation sequence and to ensure allocation concealment was insufficiently described. Baseline characteristics do not indicate an evident failure of randomization.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that the coordinator “had prepared 100 sealed nontransparent envelopes, of which 53 contained allocation to the intervention group and 47 to the control group” and that allocation occurred by the patient/caregiver “drawing one envelope.” It does not state that the envelopes were shuffled or otherwise arranged according to a random sequence. Nor does it confirm sequential numbering, opaque tamper-proof envelopes, independent preparation, or procedures preventing the coordinator who prepared the envelopes from influencing allocation. Thus, random sequence generation and concealment cannot be confidently judged adequate.

   The final 14 participants were allocated in a 2:1 ratio, deliberately altering the originally balanced allocation ratio. This does not itself demonstrate bias, but reinforces the lack of a clearly documented randomization procedure.

   Baseline patient and caregiver characteristics were reasonably similar between groups. For example, mean MMSE score was 14.4 versus 15.3, severe dementia was present in 36% versus 24%, and the reported P value for distribution of dementia severity was .244. These differences appear compatible with chance in this small trial and do not themselves suggest a problem with randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants, caregivers, and the dementia family care coordinator were necessarily aware of allocation because the intervention was an intensive, individualized nurse case-management programme. However, for the assignment-effect estimand, awareness alone does not create bias. Bias would require deviations from the intended intervention that arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, affected institutionalization, and were unbalanced between groups.

   The extensive contacts, counselling, service coordination, home visits, training courses, and access to the coordinator were components of the intended intervention, rather than deviations from it. The report does not identify important non-protocol co-interventions, crossovers, or protocol failures attributable to the trial context.

   Analysis was according to randomized group: the report describes Kaplan–Meier and Cox-regression analyses comparing the intervention and control groups, and states that there were “no dropouts from the study during the 2-year follow-up.” Therefore, there is no indication of an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis that would fail to estimate the effect of assignment.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Follow-up status was reported for all 100 randomized participants at 1 and 2 years, and the authors state that there were “no dropouts from the study during the 2-year follow-up.” Institutionalization and deaths at home were obtained from the Social Insurance Institution register. This substantially reduces concerns about ordinary loss to follow-up.

   However, the time-to-event analysis censored participants at death: “In the survival analyses, times were censored at death if the patient died in community care before institutionalization.” Death is a competing event for institutionalization and is potentially related to the underlying probability and timing of institutionalization in this frail dementia population. By 2 years, 9/53 (17%) intervention participants and 8/47 (17%) control participants had died at home. These proportions were similar between groups, which is reassuring, but the report does not provide an analysis addressing whether censoring at death was non-informative or a competing-risk/sensitivity analysis.

   Therefore, there is no strong evidence that the result was materially biased, but the assumption that death censoring was unrelated to the subsequent institutionalization process is uncertain.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome—placement in long-term institutional care—was clearly defined and its occurrence was obtained from the Social Insurance Institution register. This is an appropriate and relatively objective method for ascertaining institutionalization.

   Importantly, the report states that permanent institutionalization was decided by local expert groups, usually including “a chief physician, head nurse, and social worker,” and that these groups “were blind to the allocation and generally unaware that a patient was even participating in the study.” Thus, the persons making the key decision that determined the outcome were unaware of treatment allocation.

   Although the intervention was intended to support continued community care and might appropriately influence circumstances leading to institutionalization, this is part of the intervention effect rather than differential outcome measurement. There is no indication that institutionalization was measured differently in the two groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies time to institutionalization as the “main outcome measure” and reports relevant Kaplan–Meier estimates at 6, 12, and 24 months, together with a time-dependent Cox regression analysis. It also explains why a time-dependent model was used: “the assumption of proportional hazards was not met in the data.” These features are consistent with reporting the stated main outcome.

   Nevertheless, there is no accessible pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The study was conducted in 1993–95, before prospective trial registration became routine, and the provided material does not document a prospectively registered specification of the time points, handling of death as a competing event/censoring event, or the time-dependent Cox-model approach. The availability of several possible analyses for this time-to-event outcome—including alternative approaches to deaths and different follow-up time points—means selective selection of the reported analysis cannot be excluded.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was statistically significant or favorable; therefore, high risk of bias is not justified.
