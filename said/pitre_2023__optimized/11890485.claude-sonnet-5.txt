# Risk of Bias 2.0 Assessment
**Outcome: Time to institutionalization**
**Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)**

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The allocation sequence was generated using a random method: "the dementia family care coordinator had prepared 100 sealed nontransparent envelopes, of which 53 contained allocation to the intervention group and 47 to the control group. Allocation to the groups was made by each patient (or her/his caregiver if the patient was unable) drawing one envelope." This suggests a random component (1.1: Probably yes).

However, allocation concealment is questionable: the *same person* (the dementia family care coordinator) who generated and held the envelopes subsequently delivered the entire intervention and had an active role in the study, raising a possible conflict of interest. There is no description of sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, tamper-proof envelopes, nor independent oversight of envelope preparation/administration (1.2: Probably yes/no information — some concern).

Additionally, the randomization process was altered mid-study without pre-specification: "the final 14 patients from the city of Kuopio were randomly allocated at a ratio of 2:1 to the intervention group... or the control group... to give more patients the opportunity to receive support." This post-hoc change to the allocation ratio, decided by study personnel, is a deviation from the original randomization design and raises concern about process integrity, even though the authors argue (post-hoc) that "the different ratio caused no bias."

Baseline characteristics were reasonably balanced (no statistically significant differences in age, sex, diagnosis, MMSE, Barthel Index, living arrangement), though there was a notable (non-significant) imbalance in dementia severity (36% severe in intervention vs. 24% in control), which is clinically relevant given the paper's own conclusion that severity modifies the intervention effect. This imbalance is compatible with chance but adds uncertainty.

Given the concealment concerns and the unplanned change to allocation ratio by study personnel (who were not blinded to the process), a judgement of **Some concerns** is warranted.

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Participants, caregivers, and the intervention deliverer (dementia family care coordinator) were necessarily aware of group assignment, as blinding is not feasible for this type of complex psychosocial/case-management intervention (2.1/2.2: Yes). 

However, for the *effect of assignment to intervention*, the key question is whether deviations inconsistent with the protocol arose *because of the trial context* and affected the outcome. There is no evidence that trial-context-driven deviations (e.g., contamination, non-protocol interventions differentially affecting institutionalization) occurred. The intervention itself (support, counseling, crisis management) was the assigned intervention, not a deviation from it. The control group received "usual services provided for geriatric patients in community care," and there is no indication of important co-interventions or crossover between groups: "the coordinator had no extra money to buy services for the patients, only those services within the financial means of the patients were used." (2.3: Probably no).

Furthermore, the outcome (institutionalization) was determined by an independent panel "blind to the allocation and generally unaware that a patient was even participating in the study," which further insulates the outcome from awareness-related deviations.

The analysis was consistent with intention-to-treat principles: "There were no dropouts from the study during the 2-year follow-up," and all 100 randomized patients were accounted for and analyzed according to assigned group (2.6: Yes).

Therefore, both components of this domain support a low risk of bias judgement.

---

## 3. Bias due to missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Outcome data (institutionalization status) were available for all 100 randomized participants across the 2-year follow-up: "There were no dropouts from the study during the 2-year follow-up (Figure 1)." The Figure 1 flow diagram accounts for every patient in both groups as either remaining at home, institutionalized, or deceased at home. 

Deaths during community care were handled appropriately using a survival analysis approach, with "times... censored at death if the patient died in community care before institutionalization," which is a valid and standard approach for handling this competing risk in time-to-event analyses, rather than treating it as missing data.

Since outcome ascertainment was complete for all randomized participants and appropriate censoring methods were used, there is no meaningful risk of bias due to missing outcome data.

---

## 4. Bias in measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The method of measuring the outcome (time to placement in long-term institutional care, obtained from the national Social Insurance Institution register) was appropriate and objective, not reliant on subjective judgement from participants or intervention staff. Measurement/ascertainment methods did not differ between groups, since institutionalization decisions were made through a standardized administrative process: "a group of experts (usually a chief physician, head nurse, and social worker) makes the decision to permanently institutionalize patients when they or their caregivers have expressed the need for institutionalization."

Critically, these outcome assessors were blinded to intervention allocation: "These groups of experts were blind to the allocation and generally unaware that a patient was even participating in the study." Given this independent, blinded, largely administrative/objective determination of the outcome, knowledge of intervention assignment is unlikely to have influenced outcome ascertainment.

---

## 5. Bias in selection of the reported result: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The primary outcome (time to institutionalization) is clearly defined in the Methods and is reported fully and consistently with pre-specified intent in the Results, using appropriate survival/Cox regression analysis. There is no evidence of selective reporting among multiple candidate outcome measurements or analyses for this particular outcome — the result is reported with hazard ratios, confidence intervals, and Kaplan-Meier estimates at multiple time points (6, 12, 24 months), consistent with a pre-specified analytic approach.

However, no trial protocol or prospectively registered statistical analysis plan is available/cited for this study (it predates mandatory trial registration, being conducted in 1993–1995 and published in an era before registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov were standard). Without a pre-specified, date-stamped analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available, it is not possible to confirm that the outcome measurement (time to institutionalization) and the specific analytic approach (time-dependent Cox regression, chosen post hoc because "the assumption of proportional hazards was not met in the data") were not influenced by knowledge of the results. Indeed, the authors explicitly note they adapted the statistical model because the standard Cox proportional hazards assumption was violated, indicating some degree of data-driven analytic choice.

Given the absence of information on a prospective analysis plan and evidence of at least one data-driven analytic decision, but no clear evidence of outcome-based selective reporting, the judgement is **Some concerns**.
