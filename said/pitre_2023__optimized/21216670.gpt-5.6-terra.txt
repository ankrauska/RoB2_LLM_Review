1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was generated randomly and concealment was adequately described. The report states: “Randomisation was balanced by centre, with an allocation sequence based on a block size of four, generated with a computer random-number generator by the pharmacist at the coordinating centre.” Allocation was “concealed by use of sequentially numbered opaque envelopes.” The pharmacist, rather than enrolling investigators, assigned participants according to the randomization list.  
   
   The groups were equal in size (59 per group), and baseline characteristics were broadly comparable. There were modest imbalances—e.g., baseline FMMS was higher in the fluoxetine group (17.1 versus 13.4) and prior stroke more frequent (17% versus 7%)—but these are plausible chance imbalances in this relatively small trial and do not, by themselves, indicate a failure of randomization. The primary analysis adjusted for centre, age, previous stroke, and baseline FMMS.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to fluoxetine versus placebo at baseline.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants, carers, site staff, and investigators were masked. Fluoxetine and placebo were identical in appearance and packaging: “The placebo was identical to the active drug in appearance and packaging.” The report further states that “Patients, carers, all site study staff, and investigators were masked to treatment assignment.” Thus, there is no indication that knowledge of allocated intervention led to differential co-interventions or other protocol deviations arising from the trial context. All participants received physiotherapy and standard organized inpatient stroke care; physiotherapy was intentionally delivered according to usual procedures at each centre, and centre was included in the regression model.
   
   Adherence was high and similar between groups: mean relative dose intensity was 94.8% with fluoxetine and 94.0% with placebo. The primary analysis included all participants with available primary-outcome data (57/59 fluoxetine and 56/59 placebo). Although this is not a complete ITT analysis because participants with missing outcome data were excluded, it is an appropriate modified ITT approach for estimating the effect of assignment; the implications of the missing data are considered separately below. No patients were analysed according to treatment received or excluded because of non-adherence.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   f. **Rationale:** The primary FMMS outcome at day 90 was available for 113 of 118 randomized participants (95.8%): 57/59 in the fluoxetine group and 56/59 in the placebo group. For a continuous outcome, this level of outcome-data availability is generally sufficient for a low-risk judgement when the missing observations are unlikely to materially change the result.
   
   Missing data were balanced between groups (two fluoxetine and three placebo participants). The flow diagram reports the reasons: one death and one withdrawal in the fluoxetine group, and one death and two withdrawals in the placebo group. Deaths occurred one per group, while withdrawals were attributed to severe hypoxia, kidney tumour, and pulmonary embolism. These reported reasons do not show an important between-group differential pattern related to motor recovery. No evidence suggests that the small amount of missing FMMS data biased the reported comparison.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** FMMS is an appropriate and validated measure of motor recovery after stroke. The authors describe it as “widely used for assessment of motor recovery after stroke” with “excellent intra-rater and inter-rater reliability and validity.” It was assessed at comparable scheduled times in both groups (baseline, day 30, and day 90).
   
   Outcome assessors were masked to allocation. The report states that “physicians assessing the outcome were masked to group assignment” and, more broadly, that all site study staff and investigators were masked. Although FMMS assessment by a physiotherapist involves some judgement, masking substantially protects against differential outcome assessment. There is no indication that measurement procedures or ascertainment differed between the fluoxetine and placebo groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The trial registration specifies the same primary outcome domain and time point as the publication: “Progression in the Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale” at “M3 (3 months).” The article likewise defines the primary outcome as change in FMMS from baseline to day 90. This consistency reduces concern that the primary outcome domain or primary follow-up time was selected after observing the results.
   
   However, the registry entry does not provide a sufficiently detailed, date-stamped statistical analysis plan for the specific reported result. In particular, it does not pre-specify the analytic method, covariate adjustment, use of change scores versus final values, handling of missing outcome data, or whether the total FMMS score rather than upper- and lower-limb subscores would be the principal analysis. The report presents both unadjusted day-90 values and adjusted change-score analyses, with adjustment for baseline imbalances. These analyses may be appropriate, but it cannot be verified that the reported adjusted primary analysis was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.  
   
   There is no direct evidence that the reported FMMS result was selected on the basis of its magnitude or statistical significance; therefore, high risk of bias is not warranted. Nonetheless, the absence of a detailed pre-specified analysis plan leads to some concerns.
