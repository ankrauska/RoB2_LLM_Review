1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence was reported as random but allocation concealment was not described.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that women “**were assigned randomly via random number tables**,” which supports use of a random allocation sequence. Baseline demographic and outcome measures were reported not to differ significantly between conditions: “**One-way ANOVAs showed that at pre-treatment the women’s GHQ, RSS… did not differ significantly across conditions**.” Thus, there is no evident baseline imbalance suggesting a failed randomization process.  
   
   However, there is no information on whether the random-number-table allocation was concealed from staff recruiting/enrolling women (e.g., central allocation, independent allocation, or sequential opaque sealed envelopes). Under RoB 2, lack of information about allocation concealment leads to *some concerns* when baseline data do not otherwise suggest a problem.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew their allocated treatment because the interventions were distinct psychological therapies (supportive counselling, stress management, or alcohol-focused couples therapy [AFCT]). Therapists also necessarily knew the assigned intervention. The report describes distinct treatment content, including non-directive counselling in the supportive-counselling group and cognitive restructuring, homework, and behavioural strategies in stress management.

   The study included some features intended to maintain protocol delivery: “**Written treatment manuals provided**” the therapy sessions; sessions were videotaped and reviewed in weekly supervision “**to ensure adherence to the treatment protocol**”; and therapists were assigned equal numbers of clients in each condition. Further, the low uptake of conjoint treatment in AFCT was explicitly accommodated in the intervention strategy: “**If the man refused to participate in therapy, the woman received the stress management treatment**.” Therefore, this particular change appears consistent with the intended AFCT strategy rather than clearly a protocol deviation arising from the trial context.

   Nevertheless, the article does not report whether unblinded participants or therapists introduced differential co-interventions, behavioural changes, or other deviations specifically because of trial participation. It also states that adherence to suggested behavioural changes was not formally assessed: “**We did not assess adherence to those suggestions formally.**” This absence of information about deviations arising from the trial context, in an unblinded psychosocial intervention trial, gives *some concerns*. The repeated-measures analyses retained participants in their randomized groups among those with outcome data; exclusions owing to missing outcomes are considered under the missing-data domain rather than this domain.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because outcome data were missing for a substantial proportion of randomized women and missingness was plausibly related to their burden and distress.

   b. **Rationale:** Sixty-one women were randomized, but only 47 completed treatment and only 44 provided data at 6-month follow-up: “**Sixty-one participants were allocated randomly**”; “**Forty-seven participants completed treatment and 44 of these participants provided assessed data at 6-month follow-up**.” Thus, follow-up outcome data were unavailable for 17/61 women (approximately 28%). The RSS analysis also appears based on the smaller follow-up sample (Table 1: 17 + 14 + 13 = 44).

   The reasons for missing data strongly suggest that missingness could depend on the true burden/distress outcome. The authors state: “**most of these women reported they had left their partners soon after beginning the programme**” and “**all the women who dropped out from treatment declined further assessment**.” Leaving a severely distressed and alcohol-affected relationship is plausibly strongly associated with women’s subsequent burden, psychological distress, and wellbeing. The direction of resulting bias cannot be reliably predicted: women who left may have had either particularly severe distress at dropout or subsequently improved after leaving.

   Although the authors report no statistically significant association between treatment condition and dropout status, they acknowledge that “**the small number of dropouts overall give this analysis low power to detect differential attrition**.” No appropriate missing-data adjustment or sensitivity analysis demonstrating robustness to plausible missing outcomes was reported. Therefore, the risk is high.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Women’s burden and distress were assessed using self-reported instruments: the Relative Stress Scale (RSS) and the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ). The article states that “**The women’s burden and distress were assessed using the Relative Stress Scale (RSS)… and the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ)**.” These are appropriate and established instruments for the intended constructs; the RSS is described as assessing “personal distress, life upset and negative feelings,” and the GHQ as a validated screening measure for common psychological disorders.

   However, the outcome assessors were the participants themselves, and they were aware of their assigned treatment. For these participant-reported, subjective outcomes, knowledge of treatment is especially likely to influence reporting. This concern is substantial because the interventions explicitly sought to alter women’s appraisals and coping. For example, stress management included “**cognitive restructuring**,” challenging self-blame and responsibility-related beliefs, and enhancing pleasant activities; supportive counselling offered a supportive, non-judgemental setting. Such treatment content could change questionnaire responses through expectations, perceived benefit, or reframing of distress, independently of any underlying change in burden.

   Since the comparison includes an active contact control, the direction and magnitude of bias are uncertain. Nonetheless, for subjective self-reported burden/distress in an unblinded psychotherapy trial, it is likely that knowledge of intervention received influenced outcome assessment. This warrants a *high risk of bias* judgement.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies women’s burden/distress as a primary outcome area and reports results for both prespecified instruments, RSS and GHQ, at post-treatment and 6-month follow-up. It also reports the relevant treatment-by-time interaction tests and the means at all three assessment points. This is reassuring and there is no direct evidence that a particular reported RSS or GHQ result was selected because it was favourable.

   However, no prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan is available in the material provided. The trial report describes several outcomes, two measures within the burden/distress domain (RSS and GHQ), and repeated outcome assessments (pre-treatment, post-treatment, and 6-month follow-up). The report additionally presents more than one analytical approach for some outcomes—for example, repeated-measures ANOVA and an ANCOVA for percentage days intoxicated—demonstrating analytic flexibility in the study overall. The supplied information does not establish that the particular burden/distress result was analysed according to an analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The trial-registration information referenced in the request does not, in the materials available here, provide a sufficiently detailed prospective outcome-measurement and analysis specification to rule out selection among instruments, time points, or analyses. In the absence of direct evidence of selective reporting, but without a pre-specified analysis plan adequate to exclude it, RoB 2 indicates *some concerns*.
