**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The trial used an adequate random sequence generation method and concealed allocation, with no significant baseline imbalances.
b. Rationale: The authors state that participants were "allocated to one of three treatment interventions using computerized block randomization and allocation group concealed in envelops." Additionally, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics (including baseline pain intensity) were comparable across the three groups with no statistically significant differences, indicating a successful randomization process.

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: There were no deviations from the intended interventions that would impact the effect of assignment, and all randomized participants were analyzed in their assigned groups.
b. Rationale: Although the treating therapist was aware of the assigned interventions ("The principal investigator acted as both outcome assessor... and as therapist administering all study treatment"), there is no evidence of deviations from the protocol driven by the trial context. Furthermore, an intention-to-treat approach was effectively utilized, as "All completed the study and were entered into the analysis," meaning no participants were excluded from the analysis post-randomization.

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: There is no missing outcome data for this trial.
b. Rationale: The study reports a 100% retention and follow-up rate. The authors state, "There were 105 individuals who expressed interest in participating in the study. All completed the study and were entered into the analysis (Fig. 3)." Therefore, the risk of bias due to missing data is low.

**4. measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Pain intensity is a subjective, participant-reported outcome, and adequate blinding to the physical interventions is highly improbable, making the outcome measurement susceptible to influence from knowledge of the intervention.
b. Rationale: The trial is described as "single-blind," but participants received physical interventions (Standard care vs. TENS vs. IFT) that produce distinct physical sensations, making true blinding of participants virtually impossible. Furthermore, the principal investigator "acted as both outcome assessor... and as therapist administering all study treatment." Since pain intensity was measured using a subjective 100-mm visual analog scale, the participants' and the unblinded assessor's knowledge of the received intervention likely influenced the assessment of the outcome. 

**5. selective reporting: some concerns**
a. Judgement: While the reported data match the methodology described in the text, the absence of a detailed pre-specified statistical analysis plan prevents confirming that no selective reporting occurred.
b. Rationale: The study reports all expected data points for pain intensity across the multiple time points described in the methods (baseline, after first treatment, before eighth treatment, 1 week after eighth treatment). However, without reviewing the full, date-stamped trial protocol and statistical analysis plan from the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry, it cannot be definitively ruled out that the reported analyses were not selected from multiple eligible analyses.
