1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The double-blind phase used a computer-generated randomization sequence, with measures to conceal allocation from investigators. The report states: “eligible patients were assigned to escitalopram or placebo treatment according to a **computer-generated randomisation list**.” It further states that the “details of the randomisation series were unknown to any of the investigators” and were held in “sealed opaque envelopes.” Patients were enrolled sequentially and allocated the lowest available randomization number in blocks of four.

   Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics at randomization were reported to be similar: “There were no differences in demographics and clinical parameters between the two treatment groups in the randomisation period.” The group sizes were also close to the intended 1:1 allocation (placebo 157; escitalopram 163). There is no indication of an important baseline imbalance or of manipulation of allocation.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants, investigators, and other study personnel were blinded during the randomized treatment period. Study tablets were described as having “identical appearance, taste or smell,” and “all study personnel and participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire duration of the study.” Thus, there is little reason to expect differential co-interventions, differential management, or other trial-context deviations arising from awareness of assignment.

   The intervention strategy was implemented as intended: patients allocated to escitalopram continued their established 10- or 20-mg dose, whereas those allocated to placebo underwent a specified one-week taper if previously receiving 20 mg before switching to placebo. This taper was part of the planned intervention strategy, rather than an unplanned deviation.

   The primary analysis was described as using the ITT population and used time-to-event methods (log-rank test, Kaplan–Meier curves, and Cox proportional hazards model). Although the reported ITT set excluded two randomized patients who had not met the response criterion for entry into the randomized phase, these patients were apparently ineligible for randomization under the protocol. This small exclusion is unlikely to have materially affected the result. The analysis otherwise retained patients in their allocated randomized groups.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   e. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   f. **Rationale:** The primary outcome was time to relapse. Relapse itself was handled as an event, and 119 of 320 randomized patients relapsed. However, some participants discontinued for reasons other than relapse: “During the 24-week double-blind period, the overall withdrawal rate, excluding relapses, was comparable for both treatments: 7.9% for patients treated with escitalopram (13 patients), and 8.9% for patients treated with placebo (14 patients).”

   These similar withdrawal proportions provide reassurance that differential censoring between groups was limited. Nevertheless, approximately 8% of participants in each group had follow-up censored before the end of the double-blind period for reasons other than relapse. In a time-to-relapse analysis, censoring is unbiased only if it is non-informative. The report does not provide sufficiently detailed reasons for these non-relapse withdrawals by treatment group, nor a sensitivity analysis assessing whether informative censoring could have changed the estimated hazard ratio.

   Therefore, while there is no clear evidence that missing outcome data biased the result, it remains possible that withdrawal or loss to follow-up was related to subsequent relapse risk. This leads to some concerns rather than low risk of bias.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Relapse was prospectively defined as either “an increase in the Y-BOCS total score of ≥5 points from the time of randomisation, or an unsatisfactory treatment effect (lack of efficacy) as judged by the investigator.” Almost all observed relapses were based on the quantitative Y-BOCS criterion: “The Y-BOCS relapse criterion accounted for almost all of the relapses (116 out of 119 patients, 97%).” Thus, the potentially more subjective investigator-judgement component contributed to only three relapse events.

   The Y-BOCS is an established clinician-rated OCD measure, and raters received training: “Only those investigators who had actively participated in rater training sessions prior to inclusion of patients into the study were allowed to rate patients.” Ratings were made by the same investigator at each visit whenever possible, supporting consistency of measurement.

   Crucially, investigators, personnel, and participants were blinded throughout the double-blind period. Hence, assessors were unlikely to know treatment allocation, substantially limiting differential assessment of symptom worsening or investigator-determined lack of efficacy. Outcome assessments occurred at scheduled, comparable intervals in both groups. No evidence indicates that outcome ascertainment differed between treatment groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication clearly identifies time to relapse from the start of double-blind treatment as the prospectively defined primary outcome: “The prospectively defined primary analysis of efficacy was the time to relapse from the start date of double-blind treatment.” The reported primary analysis—two-sided log-rank comparison of time to relapse—is consistent with this stated outcome, and the article also reports the corresponding Cox-model hazard ratio (placebo versus escitalopram, HR 2.74; p<0.001).

   The report presents supportive analyses, including analyses censoring relapses during the first 7 and 14 days after randomization and a post-hoc analysis restricted to relapses meeting the Y-BOCS criterion. These are identified as secondary, sensitivity, or post-hoc analyses rather than being substituted for the primary result. The primary result itself is fully reported.

   However, the available material does not include a dated, detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. Although the trial registration may establish that relapse/time to relapse was a pre-specified primary outcome, the information provided here is insufficient to verify that the precise outcome definition, analysis population, censoring rules, model specification, and choice among potentially eligible analyses were all finalized before unblinding. Accordingly, there are some concerns, rather than a low-risk judgement, for selective reporting.
