# Risk of Bias Assessment (RoB 2.0)
**Outcome: Change in operation-specific checklist scores**
**Effect of interest: Assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect)**

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The trial states participants were "randomly assigned to three experimental groups," but no details are provided regarding the method of sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number tables) or allocation concealment procedures. There is no information on whether allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants. Additionally, no baseline characteristics (e.g., prior experience, baseline test scores, demographics) are reported, so it is not possible to assess whether the randomization achieved balanced groups.

**b. Rationale:** The article states only: "all participants performed the entire task during a pre-test, after which they were randomly assigned to three experimental groups." There is no description of the sequence generation method (e.g., random number generator, coin toss) and no mention of allocation concealment. No baseline comparison of participant characteristics (e.g., prior surgical exposure, academic year distribution within the "first- and second-year medical students") is presented. Per the algorithm: sequence randomness is unclear (answered "Probably yes" given explicit statement of "randomly assigned," but no further detail), allocation concealment is "No information," and baseline imbalance cannot be assessed due to "No information." This combination (NI on concealment, no information on imbalance) maps to "Some concerns."

---

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Blinding of participants and personnel was not feasible/relevant in the traditional pharmacological sense, but the deviations-from-intervention domain is primarily concerned with contamination, co-interventions, and adherence to assigned practice schedule that could bias the effect of assignment. There is no evidence that the trial context led to non-protocol deviations affecting the outcome, and all groups appear to have received their assigned practice schedule as intended, with appropriate ITT-consistent analysis (all randomized participants contributing to analysis).

**b. Rationale:** The article describes clearly defined and structurally distinct interventions for each group ("The Functional-order-practice group practiced the entire task during three 20-minute practice sessions..."; "The Random-practice group practiced the 5 skills in a random order..."; "The Blocked-practice group... practiced 3 consecutive trials of the same skill before starting the next practice session"). There is no indication that participants deviated from their assigned schedule, crossed over between groups, or received non-protocol co-interventions. The feedback provided ("an orthopedic faculty member circulated within the group systematically to provide feedback") was applied consistently across groups ("no feedback was provided during the pre-test or the 2 post-tests"), suggesting comparability of ancillary care. All 28 participants appear to have been analyzed according to their assigned group, consistent with an intention-to-treat-like analysis. There is no suggestion that awareness of group assignment (which was unblinded, as this is a practice-schedule intervention with no plausible blinding) influenced participant behavior in a manner inconsistent with the trial protocol. Because deviations-from-protocol concerns primarily apply to trial-context-driven behavior changes affecting outcome, and none are evident here, this domain is judged as low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** There is no indication of missing outcome data for the checklist score outcome. All 28 randomized participants appear to have completed the pre-test, immediate post-test, and delayed post-test, with no reported dropouts or exclusions.

**b. Rationale:** The article reports outcomes for all three groups without mention of any participant withdrawal, loss to follow-up, or exclusion: "the participants' performances were assessed 3 times: before the acquisition (pre-test), immediately after the acquisition (immediate post-test), and after a 30-minute rest period (delayed post-test)." The statistical analyses (mixed ANOVAs) appear to include all 28 participants ("28 first- and second-year medical students... practiced the bone-plating task"), and no CONSORT-style flow diagram indicating missing data is presented, but there is also no textual indication of any missing data. Given the short time frame of the study (a single laboratory session of about 90 minutes total), the likelihood of missing outcome data is low, and there is no evidence suggesting otherwise.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The checklist outcome was assessed by evaluators, and it is unclear whether these outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation. Because the checklist evaluation involves some judgement by the assessor (observer-reported outcome involving judgement), lack of blinding could plausibly influence scoring, though the checklist's "detailed, operation-specific" nature likely reduces (but does not eliminate) subjectivity.

**b. Rationale:** The article states: "Two experienced orthopedic surgeons used 4 separate methods of evaluation during the pre-test and the 2 post-tests," and that a checklist of "detailed, operation-specific procedures" was used, developed by "a panel of 3 orthopedic surgeons [who] identified the actions necessary to perform the operative task effectively." However, there is no explicit statement that these two evaluating surgeons were blinded to the participant's assigned practice-schedule group or to the test phase (pre/post) when scoring performance. Given that checklist scoring, while more objective than global ratings, still requires some judgement (e.g., determining whether a step was performed correctly), and given no information on blinding of assessors is provided, this raises "Some concerns." This is not rated "High risk" because the checklist is described as fairly objective/detailed (reducing scope for outcome assessment to be influenced by knowledge of group assignment), and there is no direct evidence that unblinded assessment influenced scores.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** No trial registration or pre-specified statistical analysis plan was identified/available for this study (the study appears to predate mandatory trial registration and no protocol is referenced in the article). While the checklist outcome, along with final product analysis, global ratings, and drilling time, all appear to be reported (suggesting outcome-level reporting was complete across the four assessment methods), the absence of a documented pre-specified analysis plan means it cannot be confirmed that the specific checklist metric/analysis reported (e.g., choice of comparing immediate vs. delayed difference scores, Tukey's post hoc method) was pre-specified before unblinded data were available.

**b. Rationale:** The associated trial registration record does not appear to correspond to a prospectively registered protocol with a pre-specified statistical analysis plan predating this study (this appears to be an older study, and no registry record with sufficient detail to confirm pre-specification of the checklist outcome/analysis was identified). The article reports results for all four evaluation methods described in the Methods section (checklists, final product analysis, global ratings, drilling times) using consistent statistical methods (mixed ANOVAs, Tukey's HSD post hoc), which is reassuring against selective outcome reporting. However, without a protocol or SAP time-stamped prior to unblinded data availability, it is not possible to confirm that this was the sole intended analysis for the checklist outcome (e.g., no information on whether alternative checklist scoring methods, cut-points, or covariate adjustments were considered and not reported). This leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" per the algorithm criterion: "There is no information on whether the result being assessed is likely to have been selected... from multiple eligible outcome measurements/analyses."

---

## Overall Risk of Bias for this outcome: **High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Per RoB 2.0 guidance, "Some concerns" in multiple domains (randomization process, measurement of outcome, selective reporting) can lead to an overall judgement of "High risk of bias" when it substantially lowers confidence in the result. Given that three of five domains raise "Some concerns" — particularly the combination of unclear allocation concealment/baseline balance, unclear blinding status of outcome assessors for a judgement-involving outcome, and lack of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan — overall confidence in the checklist score result is substantially reduced, warranting an overall judgement of **High risk of bias**.
