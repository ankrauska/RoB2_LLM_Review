1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants “were randomly allocated to one of four study conditions,” but does not describe how the allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers, random-number table) or how allocation was concealed before enrolment/assignment. Therefore, random sequence generation and allocation concealment cannot be verified. There is also no baseline characteristics table by randomized group that would allow assessment of whether important baseline imbalances suggested a problem with randomization.

   The relevant report states: “**Participants were randomly allocated to one of four study conditions**,” but provides no further methodological details. Under RoB 2, a statement that allocation was random without information on sequence generation or concealment is insufficient for a low-risk judgement. There is no direct evidence of a non-random process or of major baseline imbalance; therefore, the appropriate judgement is **some concerns**, rather than high risk.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew which educational material they received, and the study personnel/programme delivering the intervention were also effectively aware of condition. However, there is no clear evidence that deviations arising specifically from the trial context—such as differential co-interventions or departures from the teaching protocol—occurred or affected delayed diagnostic performance.

   The principal concern is the analysis. The delayed diagnostic-performance analysis did not include all randomized participants in their assigned groups. Five participants who did not complete delayed follow-up were excluded, and two further participants with available delayed-test data were excluded after randomization because they spent less than five minutes on the delayed diagnostic test and had very low scores:

   > “**Five enrolled participants failed to complete the delayed session of the study … and were excluded from the analysis.**”

   > “**One participant from the BC and CB group each spent less than five minutes on the delayed diagnostic test… This left a final sample size of 94 participants.**”

   Thus, 7 of 101 initially allocated participants were excluded from the final delayed-test analysis. In particular, excluding two participants based on post-randomization test behaviour and very low outcome performance is not consistent with an intention-to-treat analysis for the effect of assignment. Such exclusions could materially alter group means and the reported condition-by-time interaction, especially in this small study. The exclusions were also not evenly distributed: the CB group decreased from 25 to 21 participants and the BC group from 25 to 24, while the SC and FL groups retained 25 participants each.

   Consequently, the analysis was not appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention, and there is potential for a substantial impact on the delayed diagnostic-performance result. This warrants **high risk of bias**.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:** Five of 101 enrolled/randomized participants did not complete the delayed session and therefore had missing delayed diagnostic-performance data. This corresponds to approximately 5% missing outcome data. For a continuous outcome such as percentage diagnostic-test performance, outcome data from about 95% of participants will often be sufficient under RoB 2 to conclude that missing data are unlikely to have made an important difference to the estimated intervention effect.

   The report identifies the extent and reasons for the missing delayed-session data:

   > “**Five enrolled participants failed to complete the delayed session of the study (3 from the CB group and 2 from BC)… and were excluded from the analysis.**”

   Although the missing data occurred only in the CB and BC groups, the overall amount missing is small. There is no reported evidence that non-completion was related to the unobserved delayed diagnostic performance, nor are outcome-related reasons for dropout reported. The two additional exclusions of participants who completed delayed testing are addressed under deviations from intended interventions, rather than as missing outcome data, because diagnostic outcomes were available for those participants.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Delayed diagnostic performance was measured using a standardized diagnostic test comprising clinical vignettes with four possible diagnoses:

   > “**The diagnostic test consisted of 12 clinical vignettes for which students had to identify the correct diagnosis from a list of four possible disorders.**”

   The same type of outcome measurement was used across all intervention groups, and alternate test versions were counterbalanced between immediate and delayed testing. The authors also report that “**analysis of test items showed both tests to be of similar difficulty**.” The cases and learning materials were developed by an expert clinician and independently reviewed by another clinical expert, with prior use in similar studies.

   Participants were aware of their assigned instructional condition, as blinding was not feasible. However, the outcome was scored according to correct versus incorrect responses to multiple-choice diagnostic questions. Knowledge of the allocated instructional format is unlikely to influence the scoring of responses or produce differential outcome ascertainment between groups. There is no indication that different diagnostic tests, thresholds, scoring methods, or testing opportunities were used by condition. Therefore, measurement of delayed diagnostic performance is judged to be at **low risk of bias**.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies diagnostic-test performance as the primary analysis:

   > “**The primary analysis was performance on the diagnostic test using a mixed, repeated-measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA)**…”

   However, the report does not provide access to a dated protocol or statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the specific delayed diagnostic-performance analysis, covariates, outcome scoring approach, and planned comparisons were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The supplied registration information does not contain sufficient substantive detail to verify these features.

   There were multiple potentially eligible outcome measurements and analyses, including immediate and delayed diagnostic performance, two diagnostic-test versions, memory-test outcomes, total study time, time per vignette, main effects, interaction effects, and post hoc between-group comparisons. The published report does not establish that the reported diagnostic result was selected from these alternatives according to a pre-specified analysis plan. For example, the authors report a significant condition-by-time interaction and post hoc comparisons, but the pre-specification of these comparisons cannot be confirmed.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported delayed diagnostic-performance result was selected on the basis of its statistical significance or direction; therefore, high risk is not justified. Nevertheless, absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan in the available information results in **some concerns**.
