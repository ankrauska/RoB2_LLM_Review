# RoB 2.0 Assessment: BACE Intervention Trial - Agitation Score Outcome

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise due to significant baseline imbalance in arousal imbalance time between groups, despite adequate randomization methods being described.

**b. Rationale:** 
- Sequence generation appears random: "We randomly assigned participants to study groups in time blocks of 1 week... We also randomized time blocks as treatment or control condition weeks." (Signalling Q1.1: Probably Yes)
- Allocation concealment is not described in detail; there is no explicit description of how allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants. No information is provided on this. (Signalling Q1.2: No information)
- Critically, baseline imbalance was found: "There was a statistically significant difference between the treatment and control groups in time spent in arousal imbalance at pretest (t = 2.57, p < .01), with the experimental group averaging 7.16 hr (SD = 2.5) and the control group averaging 5.64 hr (SD = 2.7)." Table 1 confirms this (p=.012) for arousal imbalance, though other baseline variables (gender, FAST, age, MMSE, LOS, pretest agitation) were not significantly different.
- This single significant imbalance on a variable directly relevant to the outcome (arousal imbalance is the target of the intervention and predicts agitation) raises concern about whether randomization was successful, though the authors appropriately controlled for it statistically.
- Per the algorithm: Random sequence (Y/PY) + Allocation concealment (NI) + Baseline imbalance suggesting problem (Y/PY) → **Some concerns**.

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to lack of blinding of personnel (nursing staff) implementing the intervention, combined with evidence of non-compliance with intervention delivery that arose from the trial context, though appropriate analysis methods were used.

**b. Rationale:**
- Participants and personnel: "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions." However, this refers to *outcome assessors* (RAs) and *participants*, not necessarily to *nursing staff/carers* who implemented the new activity schedule. The nursing staff necessarily knew whether a participant was in the treatment condition, since they had to implement schedule changes: "the nursing staff implemented the new activity schedule." (Signalling Q2.2: Yes - personnel aware)
- Deviations arising from trial context: "Nurses stated the intervention was not given in 10 cases because of being short-staffed, being busy, or having more emergent needs the day of the scheduled intervention." This is evidence that carers (unblinded) failed to implement the intervention for practical/contextual reasons — this reflects a real-world implementation issue tied to trial context (staff workload), though arguably this reflects normal clinical constraints rather than a trial-specific artifact.
- These 10 participants were excluded, not analyzed as randomized in the reported comparison: "Twelve died, 10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention." This exclusion of randomized participants due to non-delivery could be viewed as deviation from strict ITT principles, but the paper describes efforts to verify no intervention was given (two independent coders confirmed no dose given), suggesting these are legitimate protocol-consistent removals rather than result-driven exclusions.
- No other important non-protocol interventions or contamination described; the design deliberately controlled activity type ("preserving, to the extent possible, the same activities as on Day 1"), and therapeutic activity sessions were measured and used as a covariate to control for confounding co-intervention.
- Analysis: The primary analysis (repeated measures ANCOVA using pretest/posttest agitation for those randomized and given the intervention) is best characterized as a modified ITT approach with exclusion of 10 participants not receiving any intervention dose plus 12 deaths and 2 with erroneous data — this exclusion (10 dropped for non-receipt) is a deviation from full ITT, but the number is modest (10/102 ~10%) and unlikely to substantially bias effect of assignment to intervention (Signalling Q2.7: Probably No — not substantial impact).
- Overall, awareness by personnel + documented deviations from protocol (10 non-delivered cases) that could plausibly relate to trial context (staff workload) but likely balanced/random in nature, combined with an analysis approach that is not strict ITT but with limited potential impact, leads to a judgement of **Some concerns** rather than High risk.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to incomplete reporting of outcome data for all randomized participants and unclear reasons/balance of the substantial proportion excluded before final analysis.

**b. Rationale:**
- Outcome data were not available for all randomized participants: Of 102 who began the study, "Twelve died, 10 were dropped from the study for not being given the intervention, and 2 were excluded for erroneous or missing data. The final sample was 78." This represents roughly 24% attrition from the enrolled sample (102 → 78).
- No sensitivity analyses were reported to assess robustness of the results to this missingness (Signalling Q3.2: No).
- Could missingness depend on the true value of agitation? Death (12 participants) in a frail long-term-care dementia population could plausibly be related to underlying health status, which could correlate with agitation levels, though this is speculative. The 10 dropped for "not being given the intervention" is a missing-intervention issue rather than missing-outcome-data per se (addressed in Domain 2), but if outcome was also not measured for these participants, that constitutes missing outcome data.
- No information is given on the distribution of these exclusions (deaths, non-implementation, erroneous data) by treatment vs. control group, so it is not possible to determine whether missingness proportions/reasons differed by group (Signalling Q3.4: No information, leading to conservative judgement rather than "High").
- Given lack of clarity on balance of missingness by group and no sensitivity analysis, but no strong specific evidence that missingness was clearly related to the true outcome value and differed by group, this is judged as **Some concerns** rather than High risk.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as outcome assessors were blinded, the measurement method was appropriate, and assessment was unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of intervention assignment.

**b. Rationale:**
- Outcome assessors (RAs) were blinded: "Data collectors and subjects were blinded to treatment and control conditions." (Signalling Q4.3: No - not aware)
- The outcome (agitation) was measured via direct observation using a structured, validated visual analog scale with detailed intensity parameters (Table 3), rather than by clinician judgement/decision or participant self-report, reducing subjectivity: "RAs marked the visual analog scale by utilizing the agitation intensity parameters shown in Table 3." This is an observer-reported outcome involving some judgement, but rigorous training and reliability checks were used: "Following training sessions, interrater reliabilities were... .95 to .984 for agitation."
- Measurement method was appropriate and consistent across groups — same protocol, same blinded observers, same timing (8:00 a.m.–8:00 p.m.) for both pretest and posttest, in both groups (Signalling Q4.1: No; Q4.2: No — measurement did not differ between groups).
- Since outcome assessors were blinded, and given the objective, structured nature of the measurement tool with high interrater reliability, there is low risk that assessment was influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, as no trial protocol or pre-registration was identified/available to confirm that the analyses reported (including choice of covariates, the ANCOVA models, and subgroup models) were pre-specified prior to unblinded outcome data being available.

**b. Rationale:**
- No trial registration record was located/provided for this study (this trial predates widespread mandatory registration and no registry ID is referenced in the article), so it is not possible to confirm whether the analysis plan (including choice of covariates such as arousal imbalance and MMSE, and the decision to run three additional exploratory ANCOVA models for MMSE severity, sleep amount, and verbal skill subgroups) was pre-specified before unblinded data were available (Signalling Q5.1: No information).
- The paper reports multiple analytic approaches for the same outcome domain (agitation): the primary repeated measures ANCOVA, a stratified paired t-test analysis, analysis stratified by arousal balance/imbalance periods (Table 5), and three additional subgroup interaction models (MMSE severity, sleep, verbal skills) — it is unclear whether these were all pre-specified or represent multiple analyses from which the most favorable/interesting results were selected and reported (Signalling Q5.3: No information).
- There is no evidence of egregious selective reporting (e.g., all analyses conducted appear to be reported, including non-significant results such as the control group paired t-test and the subgroup interaction models which were also non-significant), which mitigates concern somewhat.
- Given the absence of a protocol/registry to verify pre-specification, but no clear evidence of results-dependent selection, this is judged as **Some concerns** rather than High risk.
