**1. bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The allocation sequence was random, generated using "a block design (blocks of 8) with computer-generated random number sequences." The allocation was adequately concealed, as it was maintained in "individual presealed envelopes" and managed by the pharmacy, which "implemented masking and random assignment... and was responsible for dispensing medications with identical markings." Furthermore, baseline characteristics were comparable between groups, with the authors noting, "No significant differences were found between drug and placebo groups on sex; age; education; race; income; etiology of injury; time since injury; injury severity; or baseline measures of depression, anxiety, or QOL." 

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** Both participants and personnel were blinded to the intervention assignment. The study used identical medication packaging: "All medications were packaged in 25-mg quantities and had the same markings so that increases in the number of pills taken in both drug conditions were identical." Since the trial was successfully double-blinded, deviations from the intended intervention that arose due to the trial context are unlikely. Additionally, the researchers performed an appropriate analysis for the effect of assignment to intervention, stating, "An intent-to-treat analysis compared the outcomes of all study participants." 

**3. missing outcome data: some concerns**
* **Judgement:** Some concerns.
* **Rationale:** Of the 52 eligible participants randomized, 11 (21%) dropped out (5 from the sertraline group and 6 from the placebo group). Five participants dropped out immediately after the initial assessment. While the proportion of missing data is balanced between the groups, there is evidence that missingness may depend on the true value of the outcome, as "intent-to-treat analyses found differences between the last assessment HAM-D score for those who dropped out of treatment as compared with the assessment HAM-D score of those participants who remained in treatment (F=4.0, P=.052), with the dropouts tending to have higher HAM-D scores." Although the authors used random-effects modeling to handle repeated measures with some missing data, the extent and nature of the dropouts raise some concerns that missing outcome data could bias the result. 

**4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The outcome (HAM-D score) is a well-established and appropriate clinician-rated tool for assessing depression severity. The measurement of the outcome was identical between intervention groups. Importantly, the outcome assessors were blinded to the intervention received: "a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist rated the clinical severity of the participant’s depressive (HAM-D) and anxiety (BAI) symptoms independently from the physician... both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received." Therefore, the assessment of the outcome is highly unlikely to have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention.

**5. selective reporting: low risk of bias**
* **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
* **Rationale:** The trial was registered (NCT00233103), and the primary outcome specified in the registry is the HAM-D score at the end of treatment (10 weeks), which matches the primary outcome reported in the manuscript. The reported data are consistent with the intended outcome measurements, and the authors reported both the total HAM-D score as well as accepted unidimensional subscales of the HAM-D without selecting results based on statistical significance. There is no evidence that the reported numerical result was selected from multiple eligible analyses or measurements to favor a particular finding.
