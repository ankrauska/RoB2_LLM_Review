# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Prevalence of depressive symptoms (EPDS score ≥ 11)
## Effect of Interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise because although the sequence generation and allocation concealment appear adequate, there is a notable baseline imbalance in skin color between groups, which was not adjusted for in the primary analyses and may indicate a chance imbalance of borderline statistical significance.

**b. Rationale:** 
- Sequence generation: "The randomization was performed by a researcher not involved in the data collection using the participant identification (subject ID) after stratification for EPDS score and previous history of depression." This suggests a random component, though the specific method (e.g., random number generator) is not explicitly described → **Probably yes** for random sequence.
- Allocation concealment: "Participants and all research assistants and technicians responsible for running both the cohort study and the RCT were blinded to the study allocation." This, combined with identical capsules and a researcher not involved in data collection performing randomization, suggests concealment was likely adequate → **Probably yes**.
- Baseline imbalance: "At the baseline, there were no differences between groups, with the exception of skin color. Non-white women were more frequent in the control group (82.1%) compared with the fish oil (59.4%)" (p=0.055). This difference approaches significance and skin color could plausibly be associated with dietary patterns, socioeconomic factors, or psychosocial stressors relevant to depression risk, raising some concern about whether this reflects a chance finding or a problem with randomization. However, given only one baseline variable is imbalanced (out of many tested) and P=0.055 is not below conventional thresholds, this is likely compatible with chance.

Given: (i) allocation concealment appears adequate, (ii) the sequence was probably random, but (iii) there is a borderline baseline imbalance that raises minor concern (though most likely due to chance) → **Some concerns** is the most defensible judgement, as the imbalance was not definitively excluded as a chance finding and no formal statistical adjustment for confounding by skin color was reported for this outcome.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Participants, carers, and outcome assessors were blinded (double-blind design with identical placebo capsules), which is a strength. However, there was very substantial post-randomization attrition (32/60 = 53% did not complete the trial), including switching from randomized allocation to non-receipt of intervention, raising concerns about deviations from intended intervention arising from the trial context. Additionally, though authors report an ITT analysis, the effective ITT population had substantial post-randomization loss.

**b. Rationale:**
- Blinding: "randomized, placebo-controlled, double blind trial." Capsules were "identical regarding color, shape, size and packing." This supports low risk of bias due to participant/personnel awareness.
- However: "After randomization (second trimester visit), there was a random loss of 6 women in the fish oil group... and 4 in the placebo group... for reasons apparently not related to the study. In addition, 1 woman in the fish oil group declined participation." Further: "After the trial commencement, 12 women dropped out of the intervention (7 women from fish oil and 5 from control group)."
- This is a substantial deviation—effectively 11/32 (34%) of fish oil group and 9/28 (32%) of placebo group did not receive/complete the assigned intervention. While reasons given were largely not attributable to trial context (transferred care, miscarriage, stillbirth, gestational complications), the sheer scale of attrition, combined with limited detail on whether all these deviations were truly unrelated to intervention experience (e.g., nausea/vomiting differentially affecting continuation), raises some concern.
- Analysis: The paper states, "All analyses were performed using both the pre-protocol and intention-to-treat (ITT) procedures. The ITT population included all women randomized, regardless of whether or not they dropped out or fully adhered to the intervention." This is appropriate methodologically for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention.
- Because participants/personnel were blinded and deviations do not appear clearly attributable to trial context (i.e., not due to unblinding effects), but the scale of dropout after randomization is high and not fully explained as balanced/unrelated to prognosis, this domain is best rated **Some concerns** rather than Low.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** A very high proportion of randomized participants (28/60 = 47%) did not have EPDS ≥11 data at the T3 (postpartum, primary) endpoint under the ITT analysis, and there is no strong evidence that this missingness was unrelated to the true value of the outcome (depressive symptoms), particularly given the nature of the outcome (a psychiatric symptom likely to influence care-seeking, dropout).

**b. Rationale:**
- From the flow diagram/results: 60 women were randomized (32 fish oil, 28 control). At the T3 postpartum endpoint, Table 3 shows only 32 women completing the trial: "A total of 32 women completed the trial." Even in the "ITT" analysis in Table 3, only 32/32 fish oil and 28/28 control are shown but numbers analyzed at T3 are only 8/32 and 7/28 — indicating that the "ITT" label used by the trialists does not correspond to full ITT with all originally randomized participants' outcomes.
- Reasons for missingness include: "1 reported stillbirth," "1 reported miscarriage," "2 missed 2nd trimester visit," "7 women from fish oil and 5 from control group [dropped out]," "1 woman... reported pre-eclampsia," "1 woman in the control group had a stillbirth," "2 women in the fish oil group missed the [postpartum] appointment."
- These reasons include pregnancy complications (stillbirth, miscarriage, pre-eclampsia) that could plausibly be related to underlying health/psychological status, and general dropout for unspecified reasons is common — this raises the possibility that missingness in EPDS status could depend on true depressive symptom levels (e.g., women more distressed might be less likely to return for follow-up, or vice versa).
- The proportions of missing data appear reasonably similar between groups but are large in absolute terms (>40% missing for the outcome at T3 in the ITT population as actually analyzed), which is far above the 5% threshold generally considered "small enough" to exclude bias, especially for a dichotomous outcome like EPDS ≥11 where the true difference is likely modest.
- No sensitivity analyses were reported to assess robustness of the primary dichotomous outcome to different assumptions about missing data patterns.
- Given: (i) outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants, (ii) no evidence/sensitivity analysis demonstrating no bias, (iii) missingness could plausibly depend on the true outcome value (via pregnancy complications and general attrition affecting a psychiatric symptom outcome), and (iv) reasons for dropout (miscarriage, stillbirth, pre-eclampsia, and unexplained dropout) suggest a real possibility that missingness is related to the outcome → **High risk of bias**.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The outcome (EPDS ≥11) was a validated, appropriate measurement tool, assessment methods did not differ between groups, and although the EPDS is a participant-reported outcome (self-report questionnaire), the study was double-blind, meaning participants were unaware of their treatment allocation, which is unlikely to lead to differential measurement error.

**b. Rationale:**
- Method appropriateness: "The Portuguese version of the EPDS was validated in a sample of mothers from Pelotas, southern Brazil... the cutoff of ≥9 presents 91.3% of sensitivity and 54.7% of specificity... At the study end-point... the cutoff point of ≥11 (83.8% of sensitivity and 74.7% of specificity) was used." This is a validated and appropriate instrument, not inappropriate for the purpose → answer "No" to inappropriate measurement.
- Consistency between groups: The EPDS was administered identically to both groups at the same time points ("T0... T1... T2... T3") using the same standardized instrument and procedures, with no indication that ascertainment methods differed by group.
- Outcome assessor: EPDS is a participant-reported outcome; thus, the participant is the outcome assessor. Because "Participants and all research assistants and technicians responsible for running both the cohort study and the RCT were blinded to the study allocation," and capsules were identical in appearance, participants remained unaware of their treatment allocation, making it unlikely that their self-report was influenced by knowledge of allocation.
- While participant-reported outcomes generally raise at least "Some concerns" if the assessor (participant) is aware of allocation, here blinding was successfully implemented (identical placebo, double-blind design, side effects reported similarly in both groups: "Eight women (4 at the fish oil and 4 at the control group) reported nausea or vomiting"), suggesting no differential unblinding via side effects.
- Given adequate blinding of the outcome assessor (the participant) and no evidence that measurement differed between groups → **Low risk of bias**.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The prevalence of EPDS ≥11 (the primary outcome analyzed here) matches the pre-specified primary outcome as registered, and results are reported comprehensively across all time points and analysis populations without evidence of selective reporting from multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registry (NCT01660165) lists the primary outcome as "Major depressive episode," and the sample size section of the article states: "The original sample size calculation was based on a prevalence of postpartum depression ranging from 26.9 to 39.9%... We designed this study to detect a 5% reduction in the prevalence of EPDS ≥11 from baseline of 27% to 22% after the study intervention." This confirms EPDS ≥11 prevalence was a pre-specified, prospectively planned primary outcome, consistent with what is reported in the article: "Prevalence of EPDS ≥11 was the primary outcome."
- The full time-course of EPDS ≥11 prevalence is reported for all time points (T0, T1, T2, T3) and for both ITT and per-protocol populations, and also for the pre-specified subgroup (previous history of depression), without evidence of selective omission of unfavorable results: "The prevalence of EPDS ≥11 did not differ significantly between fish oil and control at any time in pregnancy or postpartum for neither the ITT or per protocol analysis, nor even when analysis considered only those with previous history of depression."
- There is no indication of selection from multiple alternative outcome measurements (e.g., only one measurement scale, EPDS, was used and reported consistently) or multiple alternative analytic approaches for this specific dichotomous outcome (a single cutoff of ≥11 was pre-specified and consistently applied, and both logistic regression and simple prevalence comparisons were reported using consistent methods).
- Some discrepancy exists between the trial registry's stated primary outcome ("Major depressive episode," which suggests use of the MINI diagnostic interview) and the article's designation of EPDS ≥11 prevalence as the primary outcome; however, this reflects a documented and transparent choice in the article's own methods/sample size justification rather than evidence of results-based selective reporting. Both the EPDS-based screening outcome and diagnostic interview data appear to have been objectives from the outset (the MINI was administered at baseline for eligibility purposes), and there is no indication that EPDS ≥11 was chosen post hoc because it produced more or less favorable results.
- Given no evidence of selection based on the direction, magnitude, or statistical significance of results, and full transparent reporting of the null result across all time points and subgroups → **Low risk of bias**.
