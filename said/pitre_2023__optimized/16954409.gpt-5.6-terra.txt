**Outcome assessed:** clinical pregnancy rate, defined as clinical pregnancy documented by transvaginal ultrasound “6 weeks after the IUI.” The assessment applies to the reported three-group randomized comparison (rFSH/follitropin alfa versus uFSH and hMG), with the effect of **assignment** to intervention at baseline.

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the sequence was computer generated, but allocation concealment was insufficiently described.  
   b. **Rationale:** The authors state that participants were “prospectively randomized using computer-generated random numbers,” which supports a random allocation sequence. Baseline characteristics were similar across groups, including age, infertility duration, and BMI; there is no evident baseline imbalance suggesting failure of randomization.  
   
   However, allocation used “sealed envelopes by specialist nurse.” The report does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, or opened only after irreversible enrolment. Under RoB 2, merely stating that sealed envelopes were used does not provide adequate assurance that the allocation sequence was concealed. Therefore, there are some concerns rather than low risk of bias.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because blinding of participants and personnel was not reported and there is no information about whether trial-context-related deviations occurred; however, the clinical-pregnancy analysis appears to include all randomized participants in their assigned groups.  
   b. **Rationale:** This was apparently an open-label comparison of different gonadotrophin products. The article does not report blinding of participants, specialist nurses, clinicians, or personnel delivering ovarian stimulation/IUI. Thus, participants and carers were probably aware of assigned intervention.  
   
   The protocol permitted individualized gonadotrophin-dose adjustment and cancellation of cycles with decreasing estradiol or more than three follicles ≥16 mm. These appear to be protocol-specified clinical-management procedures rather than necessarily deviations from intended intervention. However, the article does not report whether other non-protocol co-interventions, differential management, or deviations arising because of the trial context occurred and differed between groups.  
   
   For the effect of assignment, the reported clinical-pregnancy denominators correspond to all randomized participants: 21/81, 11/80, and 10/80. The article also states that “only one cycle per patient was performed.” Thus, there is no clear evidence of a per-protocol or as-treated analysis, nor of post-randomization exclusions from the reported result. Nevertheless, absence of information on trial-context deviations in an apparently unblinded trial leads to some concerns.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because clinical-pregnancy data appear available for all randomized women.  
   b. **Rationale:** The trial randomized 241 women: 81 to rFSH, 80 to uFSH, and 80 to hMG. Table II reports clinical pregnancies using these same group denominators: 21/81 (25.9%), 11/80 (13.8%), and 10/80 (12.5%). Thus, the reported clinical-pregnancy outcome appears to have been assessed for all randomized participants.  
   
   Although cycles could be cancelled according to prespecified criteria, the clinical-pregnancy rates use the full randomized denominators rather than excluding women with cancelled cycles. No losses to follow-up or outcome-data exclusions affecting the clinical-pregnancy analysis are reported. The missing-data figure description is not sufficiently detailed to contradict the numerical denominators reported in the paper.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because clinical pregnancy was assessed using the same objective ultrasound-based method across groups, and knowledge of treatment allocation is unlikely to have materially affected ascertainment.  
   b. **Rationale:** The authors specify that “clinical pregnancy was documented by TV-US, 6 weeks after the IUI.” This is an appropriate and reasonably objective outcome-assessment method. The same transvaginal-ultrasound assessment and timing were specified for all treatment groups.  
   
   Blinding of outcome assessors was not reported, so assessors may have known intervention assignment. However, the presence of a clinical pregnancy on ultrasound at a prespecified time point involves limited subjective judgement compared with participant-reported outcomes. There is no indication that ultrasound surveillance, diagnostic thresholds, or timing differed between groups. Consequently, lack of reported assessor blinding is unlikely to have materially biased this outcome.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because clinical pregnancy was declared as the primary outcome and was fully reported, but no sufficiently detailed prospectively finalized analysis plan or accessible trial-registration information is available to exclude selection among outcome measurements or analyses.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article explicitly states: “The primary outcome measure was clinical pregnancy rate,” and reports the result for all three groups: 25.9% (21/81) with follitropin alfa, 13.8% (11/80) with uFSH, and 12.5% (10/80) with hMG (overall *P* = 0.046). This consistency between the stated primary outcome and the reported outcome reduces concern. The outcome definition is also reported: clinical pregnancy documented by transvaginal ultrasound at 6 weeks after IUI.  
   
   However, the publication does not provide a pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available. The trial-registration record referenced in the request was not available in the supplied material, so it cannot be used to verify prospectively specified outcome definitions, timing, planned comparisons, or analysis methods. There is also no clear evidence that all eligible analyses—such as pairwise comparisons between each gonadotrophin preparation or alternative handling of cycle cancellation—were pre-specified and reported. Therefore, selective reporting cannot be ruled out, resulting in some concerns.
