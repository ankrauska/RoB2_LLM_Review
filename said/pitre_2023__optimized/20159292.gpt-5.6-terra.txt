1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias. Randomization used a minimisation algorithm with a random element, allocation was centrally administered through an automated telephone system, and baseline characteristics were closely balanced.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants were allocated “with a minimisation algorithm incorporating a random element.” Minimisation factors were surgeon, age, and breast density. Randomization was administered by the “University of Leeds Clinical Trials Research Unit’s automated 24-hour telephone randomisation system,” which provides credible allocation concealment from recruiting personnel until assignment.

   Baseline balance does not suggest failure of randomization: age younger than 50 years was 23% in each group; median age was 57 years in both groups; and breast-density categories were also virtually identical. The authors explicitly report that “Characteristics were well balanced between the groups.” The slightly unequal group sizes (816 versus 807 in the intention-to-treat population) are compatible with chance and minimisation with a random component.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to MRI versus no additional MRI at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This was an open-label imaging-strategy trial, so participants, surgeons, radiologists, and other clinical personnel would generally have been aware of allocated intervention. However, for the assignment effect, lack of blinding alone does not indicate bias. RoB 2 requires evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose because of the trial context, were inconsistent with the protocol, affected the outcome, and were unbalanced.

   There were deviations in intervention receipt: among 816 participants allocated MRI, 761 received MRI; among 807 allocated no MRI, nine received MRI. The report also identifies reasons for not receiving MRI, including technical failures, MRI not being available before surgery, refusal, non-attendance, claustrophobia, and scans being uninterpretable. These deviations appear principally to reflect real-world implementation and patient-level circumstances, rather than trial-context deviations introduced by knowledge of allocation or deliberate undermining of the protocol.

   Importantly, the primary analysis was intention-to-treat: “Analysis was by intention to treat.” All 816 participants allocated MRI and all 807 allocated no MRI were included in the primary-endpoint analysis. Thus, participants were analysed according to their randomised assignment, despite non-receipt or cross-over. This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to the MRI strategy.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were available for nearly all randomised participants. The primary endpoint was analysed for 816 MRI-group participants and 807 no-MRI-group participants. The flow diagram indicates only four participants lost to follow-up in the MRI group and six in the no-MRI group, with a further small number with unavailable primary-endpoint data. These numbers are very small relative to the total randomised population (1623).

   The primary outcome was relatively common—153 MRI participants and 156 no-MRI participants had a reoperation outcome—so the small number of participants without definitive follow-up could not plausibly have made an important difference to the estimated effect. Although the authors state that patients lost to follow-up were “regarded as not having a primary endpoint event,” the amount of missingness was under approximately 1% per group and was similar between groups. There is no indication that missingness was meaningfully related to reoperation status or differed in a way likely to bias the result.

4. **measurement of the outcome: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Reoperation itself is an objectively documented event, and repeat surgery within 6 months should be readily ascertainable from clinical and surgical records. Histopathological assessment followed NHS Breast Screening Programme/Royal College of Pathologists guidance. These features reduce the potential for simple misclassification of whether a further operation occurred.

   However, the trial was open label, and the outcome partly reflects clinical decisions made by surgeons who knew whether MRI had been performed. RoB 2 treats outcomes reflecting intervention-provider decisions as potentially susceptible to knowledge of intervention assignment. This is particularly relevant because MRI findings could directly alter surgical management: “A change in clinical management that was attributable to MRI findings was proposed for 55 patients (7%) in the MRI group,” and initial mastectomy was more frequent in the MRI group (58/816) than in the no-MRI group (10/807).

   The composite primary outcome also included a “pathologically avoidable mastectomy at initial operation.” These cases were reviewed by the DMEC using pre-established definitions, which provides some protection. Nevertheless, the report does not clearly state that the DMEC or other outcome adjudicators were blinded to allocation or MRI findings. There is no direct evidence that outcome assessment was manipulated by knowledge of allocation; therefore, high risk is not warranted. Nonetheless, the open-label design and provider-decision component of the outcome raise some concerns.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was prospectively registered (ISRCTN 57474502), and the publication reports the stated primary endpoint: the proportion of participants with repeat wide local excision or further mastectomy within 6 months of randomisation, or a pathologically avoidable initial mastectomy. The numerical result is fully reported for both groups: 153/816 (19%) in the MRI group versus 156/807 (19%) in the no-MRI group; adjusted OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.75–1.24; p=0.77. The article also describes the analysis as intention-to-treat and reports that logistic regression adjusted for the minimisation factors was used.

   However, low risk of bias in this RoB 2 domain requires a sufficiently detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The available information confirms prospective registration and consistency of the primary outcome, but does not provide a dated, detailed statistical analysis plan that prespecifies all relevant analytic decisions for this result, such as handling of missing primary outcome data and the exact primary model. The report also describes prespecified and ad-hoc exploratory subgroup analyses, indicating that more than one analysis was possible, although there is no evidence that the primary reoperation result itself was selected because of its findings.

   Therefore, there is no evidence of selective reporting of a favourable result, but insufficient information to establish low risk of bias.
