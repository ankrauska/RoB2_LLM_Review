# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Neuropsychological Test Performance

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:** The study states "randomized study" and describes a "1:1, randomized study" design, but no specific method of sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated randomization, random number tables) is described in the article or supplementary material referenced. There is no description of allocation concealment mechanisms (e.g., sequentially numbered sealed envelopes, central randomization). The trial registry entry does not clarify these methodological details either.

Baseline characteristics (Table 1) show no statistically significant differences between groups across a wide range of variables (age, gender, education, IQ, MoCA, BMI class, comorbidities, genotype, MCI type), with all p-values >0.10. This is compatible with successful randomization and does not raise concerns about the process itself.

Applying the algorithm: sequence generation = No information; allocation concealment = No information; baseline imbalance = No (compatible with chance). Per Table 4 of the guidance, "No information" on both sequence generation and allocation concealment, combined with no evidence of baseline imbalance, defaults to "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk," because there is no information to answer the key signalling questions about randomization and concealment methods.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:** This is an open-label trial; the masking information indicates only outcome assessors were blinded ("whoMasked": ["OUTCOMES_ASSESSOR"]), meaning participants and personnel (nutritionists, geriatricians) were aware of group assignment. As stated: "Half of the patients received additionally nutritional counseling in groups conducted by nutritionists... " – participants clearly knew whether they received the extra counseling.

Regarding deviations arising from trial context: the authors note "Because the process of recruiting volunteers included information on the risks of obesity, it is also possible that this may have increased motivation to lose weight in both groups." This is an explicit acknowledgment of contamination/co-intervention effects arising from the trial context (informed consent process), affecting both arms similarly. The unexpected minimal difference in weight loss between groups ("BMI decrease... intensive group −2.1... conventional group −1.3... P=.428") supports that deviations occurred (control group participants also lost weight, likely due to trial-context-driven motivation), and this was a key driver of the exploratory re-analysis pooling groups.

However, because these deviations affected both groups in a similar direction (both lost weight), and the *outcome being assessed here is neuropsychological performance* analyzed via the BMI-change model (an exploratory, non-randomized comparison) rather than a clean between-group ITT analysis, there is a fundamental issue: **the reported result for the outcome of interest is derived from a secondary, non-randomized (BMI-as-continuous-predictor) analysis, not from the primary intention-to-treat between-group comparison.** The primary ITT analysis (group × time interaction) showed no significant difference between groups for neuropsychological tests, which is an appropriate ITT-consistent analysis. But the key result the authors emphasize and report as their main finding relies on pooling groups and using observed ΔBMI as a continuous exposure — this is more akin to an "as observed"/naturalistic analysis rather than a strict test of assignment effect, introducing risk of confounding by adherence-related factors.

Given: (i) lack of blinding of participants/personnel, (ii) evidence of deviations from intended intervention arising from trial context (contamination of motivation across both arms), and (iii) an analytic approach for the outcome of primary interest that deviates from a clean ITT comparison — but no clear indication these deviations differentially biased the assignment effect in one direction — this is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "High risk," since the ITT group-comparison itself (as reported in Supplemental Table 2) followed appropriate ITT principles.

## 3. Missing outcome data

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:** Of 80 randomized participants, 75 completed follow-up (37/40 conventional; 38/40 intensive), a 6.25% attrition rate. The flow chart states "one died of pneumonia" and "4 dropped out." This is described as: "Five women did not complete the follow-up: 4 dropped out and one died of pneumonia."

The proportion of missing data is small (5/80, 6.25%) and relatively balanced between groups (3 vs 2, per the flow chart: 37 and 38 analyzed). This proportion is close to the commonly used 5% threshold for "small enough" missingness, though slightly above it.

Reasons for dropout (unspecified reasons for 4, death from pneumonia for 1) do not obviously relate to the true value of the neuropsychological outcome — pneumonia-related death is unlikely to be informatively related to cognitive test trajectories, and generic "dropout" reasons are not detailed enough to suggest outcome-dependent missingness. However, no sensitivity analyses were reported to confirm robustness to missing data assumptions, and there is no explicit statement confirming reasons for the 4 dropouts were unrelated to cognitive status (e.g., mobility difficulties, illness, disengagement could plausibly correlate with cognitive/functional decline).

Because missingness is small but reasons are incompletely detailed and no sensitivity analysis was performed, this is judged "Some concerns" (could depend on true value, but not clearly likely to).

## 4. Measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:** The neuropsychological test battery used is comprised of well-established, validated instruments (RAVLT, Trail Making Test, Digit Span, Verbal Fluency, Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, CAMcog, IQCODE), which are appropriate methods for measuring cognitive domains — the method of measurement was not inappropriate.

Measurement/ascertainment methods did not differ between intervention groups: the same standardized battery was administered to all participants at baseline and 12 months regardless of group assignment, by (implicitly) the same assessment protocol.

Critically, the trial protocol explicitly specifies masking of "OUTCOMES_ASSESSOR," indicating outcome assessors were blinded to intervention assignment. This is a key protective feature. While these are observer-reported outcomes involving some judgement (neuropsychological test administration/scoring), blinding of the assessors substantially reduces the risk that assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received.

Since outcome assessors were blinded, this domain defaults to Low risk of bias per the tool's algorithm (blinded assessors → assessment could not have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received).

## 5. Selective reporting

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:** The trial registry (NCT01286389) lists "Neuropsychological test performance" as a primary outcome, consistent with reporting in the article. However, several concerns arise regarding selection among multiple eligible analyses:

1. The primary pre-specified comparison (per protocol design) was a between-group ITT comparison; however, the headline result reported and emphasized throughout the discussion/conclusion is a *secondary, exploratory* analysis using ΔBMI as a continuous predictor pooling both groups — a deviation from the primary analytic strategy. The authors state: "we decided to a posteriori pool the longitudinal analysis between groups and to test for the effects of change of BMI as a continuous variable" — this is an explicit post-hoc change in analytic approach, made **after** observing that between-group weight loss differences were smaller than expected, i.e., after seeing (unblinded) outcome-related data.

2. Multiple cognitive domains/subtests were assessed (verbal memory, executive function, language, attention, global cognition), analyzed via multiple approaches (individual tests in Table 2, domain composite scores in Table 3, correlations with biomarkers in Table 4). Selective emphasis on significant findings (memory, language, executive function, global cognition) versus non-significant ones (attention: "did not correlate with BMI, age, or APOE4 status (data not shown)") suggests results for the attention domain were not fully reported, consistent with selective reporting based on statistical significance.

3. No trial protocol or statistical analysis plan (SAP) was available/referenced to confirm whether the domain-grouping strategy, principal-components analysis approach, and choice of covariates (age, APOE4 interactions) were pre-specified before unblinded data were available, or were determined post-hoc based on the results.

Given the explicit post-hoc analytic change (pooling groups and using ΔBMI as continuous predictor) and incomplete reporting of the attention domain results, combined with lack of a verifiable pre-specified SAP, this domain is judged as "Some concerns," bordering on high risk — the post-hoc analytic strategy change is a significant methodological concern, but because the primary outcome domain itself (neuropsychological performance) matches the registered outcome, and full data are reported for most domains, "Some concerns" rather than "High risk" is judged.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

Per Table 1 (RoB 2.0 overall judgement criteria), since no domain was rated "High risk of bias" but multiple domains (randomization process, deviations from intervention, missing data, selective reporting) were rated "Some concerns," the overall judgement for this outcome is **Some concerns**. The cumulative effect of concerns across four of five domains — particularly the post-hoc shift from a between-group ITT analysis to an exploratory continuous-exposure analysis as the primary reported result — substantially lowers confidence in the reported effect of intentional weight loss on neuropsychological test performance.
