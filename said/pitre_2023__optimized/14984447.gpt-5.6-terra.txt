## Outcome: SBGM frequency

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because random assignment is reported, but neither the method used to generate the random sequence nor allocation concealment is described.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants “were stratified in three categories of income … and they were randomly assigned in a patient-blinded fashion to two groups.” However, the report does not state how the random sequence was generated (e.g. computer-generated sequence, random-number table) or how allocation was concealed from personnel enrolling participants. “Patient-blinded” does not establish concealment at allocation. Baseline characteristics appear broadly balanced (including baseline weekly SBGM frequency: 8.4 versus 8.5; HbA1c: 9.1% versus 9.2%), with no clear imbalance suggesting a failure of randomization. Under RoB 2, absent information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns when baseline differences do not suggest a randomization problem.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to free strips versus requiring participants to purchase strips.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants could realistically become aware of their allocation because one group received “a supply of reagent strips, 100 strips per month,” whereas the control group received only “a one-time single package … (50 strips)” and was instructed to purchase additional strips. The research nurse was also likely aware of allocation. However, the report provides no evidence that awareness led to non-protocol deviations arising specifically from the trial context that would affect SBGM frequency and differ between groups. The intervention itself was intended to alter access to strips and, consequently, testing behaviour.  
   
   The selected analysis was appropriate for the effect of assignment: “Efficacy data from the intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward, were analysed.” Although last-observation-carried-forward is problematic for missing data, that issue is evaluated in the missing-outcome-data domain rather than this domain.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not available for nearly all randomized participants by the end of follow-up. The article reports that at 12 months “there were 25 patients remaining in the NC group and 16 patients in the C group,” corresponding to dropout rates of **19%** (6/31) in the no-cost group and **48%** (15/31) in the control group. This is substantial and markedly differential attrition.  
   
   The analysis used last observation carried forward (LOCF): “Efficacy data from the intention-to-treat population, using the last observation carried forward, were analysed.” Under RoB 2, imputed values remain missing outcome data for assessment purposes, and LOCF does not demonstrate that the result was protected from bias.  
   
   The reported reasons and circumstances also make it plausible, indeed likely, that missingness was related to the true SBGM outcome. The authors state that the higher dropout in the control group “underscored” the importance of strip cost and suggest that control participants who found strip cost problematic “may well have dropped out of the study.” Since affordability of strips is directly related to the outcome of testing frequency, the missing SBGM measurements are likely to differ systematically from observed measurements. No robust sensitivity analysis addressing plausible missing values was reported.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Follow-up SBGM frequency was measured objectively: “the frequency of SBGM was determined by downloading the glucometer memory.” The nurse downloaded the meter at scheduled visits using specified software (“WinGlucofacts”). This is an objective electronic record rather than a participant-reported estimate. The same glucometer-based method and follow-up schedule were used in both groups.  
   
   Although participants and the research nurse may have been aware of allocation, assessment of the number of tests stored in the device memory involved little or no subjective judgement and is unlikely to have been influenced by this knowledge. The self-reported baseline SBGM frequency is less objective, but the intervention-effect result was based on subsequent glucometer downloads.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies SBGM frequency as an endpoint measured at each two-month visit and reports an overall group effect from a General Linear Model, but no pre-specified statistical analysis plan finalized before outcome data were available is presented. The available material does not establish that the analysis reported for SBGM was selected according to a pre-specified plan.  
   
   There were multiple eligible time points over 12 months, and both an ITT/LOCF General Linear Model and a completer-only repeated-measures analysis are described. The report highlights particular findings—for example, a significant difference in SBGM frequency “during the first 4 months”—but does not provide sufficient pre-specified analytical detail to exclude selection among time points or analytical approaches. There is no direct evidence that the result was selected because it was favourable; therefore, this is judged as some concerns rather than high risk of bias.

## Outcome: HbA1c

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The same randomization-process limitations apply. The study states that participants were “randomly assigned” after stratification by income, but does not report a random sequence-generation method or allocation-concealment procedure. Baseline HbA1c was very similar between groups (9.1% in the no-cost group and 9.2% in the control group), and Table 1 shows no obvious important baseline imbalance. Thus, there is no indication of a failed randomization process, but insufficient information about concealment prevents a low-risk judgement.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention.  
   b. **Rationale:** Allocation was likely apparent to participants and possibly the research nurse because of the differing strip provision. Nevertheless, the report does not identify deviations from the intended intervention arising from the trial context that were both unbalanced and likely to affect HbA1c. Participants were instructed to continue visiting their regular physician/endocrinologist at their usual frequency: “The frequency of visits to their physician was not altered.”  
   
   The research nurse “did not provide any feedback to the patient concerning glucometer readings, random blood glucose readings or HbA1c determinations,” reducing the possibility of differential trial-delivered management based on knowledge of interim study measurements. The analysis is described as ITT with LOCF and therefore retains participants in their randomized groups, which is appropriate for the effect of assignment; limitations of LOCF are considered under missing outcome data.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Attrition was substantial and strongly imbalanced: 19% in the no-cost group compared with 48% in the control group by study end. HbA1c was measured at scheduled follow-up visits, so participants who withdrew did not provide later HbA1c measurements. LOCF was used, but this approach does not establish that the estimated intervention effect is unbiased.  
   
   The reasons and circumstances of dropout plausibly relate to glycaemic control and to the intervention. The investigators acknowledge: “We cannot exclude the possibility that patients with intrinsically poorer glycaemic control became discouraged and therefore did less testing or dropped out of the study altogether.” They also suggest that cost-related dropout was more common in the control group. Thus, missingness could depend on unobserved HbA1c values and differs substantially between groups. No sensitivity analysis was reported to show that the HbA1c result was robust to plausible values among participants with missing data.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** HbA1c was assessed using a specified objective laboratory device: “HbA1c was measured using the DCA 2000 Analyser.” Measurements were made “initially, and at each subsequent visit” in both groups. The outcome is an instrument-based biochemical measurement with little scope for assessor judgement, and there is no indication that measurement methods or thresholds differed by group.  
   
   Knowledge of intervention assignment is therefore unlikely to have influenced the HbA1c result. The authors also state that “no analysis of the data occurred until all the patients had completed the study,” which further reduces concern that interim outcome assessment influenced measurement or management by the study nurse.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** HbA1c was explicitly identified as an endpoint measured at each visit, and the paper reports both an overall model-based comparison and the temporal pattern. However, no publicly available pre-specified analysis plan is provided in the supplied report, and no sufficiently detailed prospectively finalized plan is available here to verify the chosen analysis, time points, covariate adjustment, or handling of missing data.  
   
   Multiple time points and analytical approaches were possible. The article reports that the HbA1c difference was “most apparent at the 6th month,” while also presenting an overall significance result. This does not prove selective reporting, but it leaves uncertainty about whether the reported numerical result and highlighted time point were chosen after examining the data. Therefore, RoB 2 indicates some concerns rather than low risk of bias.
