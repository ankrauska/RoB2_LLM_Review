1. **Bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The study reports an adequately random allocation sequence and a reasonably protected allocation-concealment process. Participants were allocated using an online randomization tool with permuted blocks of randomly varying sizes, and allocation information was transmitted by an independent person in serially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes. Group sizes were equal after the two exclusions (24 participants per group), and there is no reported indication of important baseline imbalance suggesting a failure of randomization.

   Relevant report text:  
   > “Participants were allocated randomly to bright light (BRIGHT), monochromatic blue light (BLUE), or a control condition (CONTROL) using an online tool … which applies permuted block randomization with randomly varying block sizes of 3, 6, and 9.”  
   > “The list was transmitted by a non-involved person using serially numbered sealed opaque envelopes.”

   Although the report does not state explicitly that envelopes were opened sequentially only after irrevocable enrolment, the use of opaque sealed, serially numbered envelopes prepared/transmitted by a non-involved person provides reasonable assurance of concealment. There is no evidence that baseline differences reflected a problem with randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of assignment to the light-exposure conditions at baseline. Participants were aware of the light condition they received, whereas the test supervisor was blinded:

   > “The test supervisor (RK) was blinded to the light condition each participant was exposed to.”  
   > “Participants only knew the light condition they were exposed to, but not the other two light conditions.”

   Participants’ awareness could in principle have influenced effort or behaviour during the reaction-time task. However, there is no evidence that deviations from protocol arose because of the trial context, that such deviations were unbalanced between groups, or that they materially affected acoustic reaction time. Variation in actual light reaching the eye, including overlap between conditions, represents imperfect intervention implementation but does not by itself cause bias in the effect of assignment unless it arose through the trial context in a way that was unbalanced and outcome-relevant.

   The principal concern is analysis of 72 rather than all 74 randomized participants:

   > “Two subjects in BRIGHT and CONTROL had to be excluded due to invalid measurements of peak oxygen uptake in the cardiorespiratory fitness test.”

   The reported analysis was therefore not a fully inclusive intention-to-treat analysis. The exclusions were few (2/74), occurred equally across two groups, and were reported as related to invalid eligibility-test measurements rather than reaction-time outcomes. Thus, a substantial impact on the result is unlikely, but the post-randomization exclusions prevent a fully low-risk judgement.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:** Acoustic reaction-time data appear to have been available for all 72 participants included in the analyses. The two exclusions from the randomized cohort represent only 2/74 participants (approximately 3%), which is sufficiently small for this continuous outcome that their unobserved reaction-time results would be unlikely to make an important difference to the estimated between-group effects.

   > “The remaining 72 participants had a median age of 23 years.”  
   > “Reaction time before and after the light exposure in the three groups is shown in Table 2.”

   The reasons for exclusion were reported as invalid measurements in a cardiorespiratory fitness test, rather than loss to follow-up, withdrawal related to treatment response, adverse effects, or missing reaction-time measurements. There is no evidence that missingness in acoustic reaction time depended on its true value or differed importantly by intervention group.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Acoustic reaction time was measured using a standardized computer-based task under the same room-light conditions immediately before and after the allocated exposure. The method is appropriate for measuring simple acoustic reaction time, and measurement procedures appear comparable across groups.

   > “Immediately before, as well as immediately after the light exposure, participants performed a computer-based simple acoustic reaction time task…”  
   > “The acoustic signal in the reaction time task appeared randomly in intervals of 3–7 s over the duration of 5 min and subjects had to respond as fast as possible by pressing the space bar on a computer keyboard.”  
   > “The median of all reaction times was calculated for each subject.”

   Although participants knew their assigned light condition, the outcome was based on computer-recorded response times rather than subjective judgement by participants or study personnel. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment differed across groups or that the computer-based measurement itself could have been influenced by knowledge of assignment. Awareness could potentially affect actual performance through expectancy or effort, but this is more appropriately considered under deviations from intended interventions rather than outcome measurement.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial registry identifies reaction time as a secondary outcome:

   > Registry: “Reaction time in milliseconds (ms)”

   The publication also explicitly states that reaction time was a pre-specified secondary outcome and that the reported methods were pre-specified and registered:

   > “Secondary aims were to assess the influence of bright and blue light on reaction time and handgrip strength which were pre-specified secondary outcomes.”  
   > “All statistical methods used to compare the groups for reaction time and handgrip strength in the present report were pre-specified and registered.”

   However, the available registry record does not specify the reaction-time measurement instrument, the time point, the precise comparison(s), the analysis model, covariate adjustment, or handling of the pre- and post-exposure measurements. It therefore cannot confirm that the reported adjusted post-exposure analysis was specified in a detailed analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected from multiple reaction-time measures or analyses because it was favourable. Indeed, the primary adjusted analyses and the additional intensity-based regression analyses are reported and are consistently null. Nevertheless, because the available registration information is insufficiently detailed to exclude selection among possible time points or analytical approaches, the appropriate RoB 2 judgement is **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.
