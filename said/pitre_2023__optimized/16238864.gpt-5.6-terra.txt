1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence was genuinely random and there is no clear evidence of baseline imbalance, but concealment of the allocation sequence before allocation was not adequately described.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors state that “*intervention and control booklets were placed in a single pile in an order determined by a sequence of random integers generated by a true random number generator*.” This supports a **random allocation sequence**.

   However, the report does not state that the booklets were opaque, indistinguishable externally, sequentially numbered, or distributed by someone unable to foresee the next assignment. Since the intervention and control booklets necessarily contained different material, it is unclear whether the person distributing them could identify forthcoming allocations. Thus, allocation concealment is **not established**.

   The reported baseline comparisons did not identify differences between groups in pre-intervention behaviour or cognitions: “*MANOVA performed on pre-intervention behaviour … and cognition measures … revealed no significant differences between the control and experimental groups*.” Age, gender, and dropout rate also did not differ statistically by condition. These findings do not suggest that randomization failed, although the reporting does not fully clarify whether all baseline comparisons were based on every randomized participant.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants were aware of their assigned condition, because they received either the persuasive/implementation-intention intervention or a control questionnaire task. Personnel distributing the booklets may also have been aware of assignment. Nevertheless, for the assignment-effect estimand, awareness creates bias only if it produces deviations from the intended protocol that arise because of the trial context and affect the outcome.

   There is no evidence of such deviations. Both conditions received a booklet and completed it under the same conditions; the control task was designed to take “*approximately the same time to complete as the intervention*.” Participants were specifically asked not to discuss booklet contents during the following week, reducing potential contamination. The immediate post-intervention intention assessment was part of the intended study procedure; the intervention-related difference in intention is the outcome of interest, rather than a deviation from protocol.

   The reported analysis for intentions was consistent with analysis according to randomized assignment. The authors used an ANCOVA comparing post-intervention intention between groups while controlling for pre-intervention intention: “*Univariate ANCOVAs were used to test the difference between post-intervention means between conditions, controlling for pre-intervention scores.*” The degrees of freedom for the intention analysis, *F*(1, 215), are consistent with analysis of the 218 participants who completed the initial booklet. There is no indication that participants were analysed according to an intervention other than that assigned or were excluded post-randomization for reasons other than missing data.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome assessed here is **immediate post-intervention intention to consume the RDIFV**, not the one-week behavioural follow-up. The article reports that “*in total, 218 people completed the booklet*,” and the booklet included “*(a) pre-intervention measures, (b) the intervention or control task, and (c) immediate post-intervention measures*.” This strongly indicates that immediate post-intervention intention data were obtained for all, or nearly all, randomized participants.

   The reported intention ANCOVA has denominator degrees of freedom of 215, consistent with approximately 218 analysed participants, further supporting minimal or no missing immediate intention data. The 33% attrition reported by the study applies to the one-week follow-up behavioural measures, not to the immediate post-intervention intention outcome. Therefore, the substantial follow-up attrition does not create missing-data bias for this specific result.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Intention was measured using a self-reported five-item scale, including items such as “*I intend to eat the RDIFV every day over the next week*” and “*I am going to eat the RDIFV every day over the next week*.” This is a participant-reported, subjective outcome; therefore, the participant is the outcome assessor.

   Participants were necessarily aware of their assigned intervention. The experimental booklet explicitly included persuasive statements intended to change intentions, including: “*You can do it, so do it this week. Make a firm decision now that you will eat the recommended daily intake of fruit and vegetables each day this week.*” The post-intervention questions were completed immediately after this material. Consequently, knowledge of receiving the intervention was highly likely to influence how participants reported their intentions, including through demand characteristics, social desirability, or a desire to respond consistently with the persuasive message.

   Although the authors instructed participants to answer according to how they currently felt and not to look back at their prior answers, this does not blind participants or prevent intervention knowledge from affecting a subjective self-report outcome. Under RoB 2, the combination of an unblinded participant-reported outcome and a strong reason to expect reporting to be influenced by allocation supports **high risk of bias**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication identifies the intended cognitive outcomes in advance within its stated hypotheses. In particular, Hypothesis 2 specified that, compared with controls, intervention participants would have higher “*intention, perceived behavioural control, self-efficacy and anticipated regret*.” The paper reports the significant effects for intention and anticipated regret and states that “*no other significant differences in cognitions were observed*.” This reduces concern that only favourable cognitive outcomes were reported.

   However, there is no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan available in the supplied material that specifies the exact intention outcome score, time point, analysis model, covariate adjustment, handling of any missing immediate data, and hierarchy among possible analyses before unblinded data were available. The study included multiple cognition constructs, pre- and post-intervention measurements, and potential analytical choices (for example, post-intervention values versus baseline-adjusted analyses). The use of ANCOVA is reasonable, but cannot be confirmed as pre-specified from the available information.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported intention result was selected because it was statistically significant; therefore, high risk of bias is not justified. Nonetheless, absence of a demonstrably pre-specified analysis plan leaves **some concerns** about selective reporting.
