1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence was computer-generated but allocation concealment before enrolment/assignment was not adequately described.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that “**a computer-based blocking method (four blocks) was used for the randomization and no stratification was made**,” which supports a random allocation sequence. It also states that “**separate physicians were assigned to randomly allocate patients**,” but does not explain whether the allocation list was concealed from staff determining eligibility and enrolling participants (e.g., central allocation, opaque sequential envelopes, or an independent pharmacy system).  
   
   Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable, with no statistically significant differences reported in sex, age, disease duration, relapse history, prior IFNβ-1a exposure, baseline EDSS, or baseline MRI lesions. These data do not themselves suggest a serious failure of randomization. Nevertheless, under RoB 2, absent information on concealment produces **some concerns**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to simvastatin versus placebo at baseline, because the reported analysis excluded randomized participants after randomization and there was potential for this to materially affect the attack-number result.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and personnel appear to have been blinded: patients “**were unaware of their group assignment and all medications were given in similar packages**.” The registry also describes quadruple masking of participants, care providers, investigators, and outcome assessors. There is no evidence that trial-context deviations from the assigned interventions occurred or were unbalanced.

   However, the analysis was not a full intention-to-treat analysis despite being described as such. The authors state that analyses “**were performed according to intention-to-treat methods**,” but subsequently specify that only dropout cases who had received treatment for at least 6 months were included in the final analysis: “**those of our dropout cases who did use simvastatin or placebo for 6 months (nine patients) were included in the final analysis**.” Thus, participants withdrawing before 6 months were excluded from the main analysis.

   This is apparent in the reported attack-number analysis, which included only 40 simvastatin and 36 placebo participants, although 43 and 42 participants, respectively, were randomized. Excluding 9/85 randomized participants (including proportionally more in the placebo group) is incompatible with estimating the effect of assignment at baseline. The exclusions could plausibly have a substantial impact because withdrawals included disease exacerbation/two relapses and because the primary outcome was attack number. Therefore, the failure to analyse all randomized participants in their assigned groups creates a high risk of bias in this domain.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because outcome data for attack number were unavailable or excluded for an appreciable and unequal proportion of randomized participants, and missingness was likely related to the true outcome value.

   b. **Rationale:** Eighty-five participants were randomized (43 simvastatin; 42 placebo), whereas the primary attack-number comparison used 40 and 36 participants, respectively. Thus, attack-number data were not included for 3/43 (7.0%) in the simvastatin group and 6/42 (14.3%) in the placebo group.

   Fourteen participants reportedly withdrew. Reasons included refusal to cooperate, personal reasons, “**two relapses**,” and chronic exacerbation. In particular, six participants withdrew because they experienced two attacks (two simvastatin and four placebo), and one placebo participant withdrew owing to chronic exacerbation. These reasons are directly related to disease activity and therefore to the outcome being assessed (attack number). Missingness/exclusion was also more frequent in the placebo group.

   The report does not present a sensitivity analysis demonstrating that the primary result was robust to plausible outcomes among missing/excluded participants. Although the authors mention per-protocol and last-observation-carried-forward analyses, these do not establish that the principal result is unaffected by outcome-related missing data. Consequently, it is likely that missingness depended on the true attack number and could have biased the treatment comparison.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Attack/relapse was operationally defined as “**any new or worsening neurological deficit in the absence of high body temperature that lasted for more than 24 h after 1-month stability**.” This is an appropriate, clinically relevant definition for relapse in relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis.

   Participants underwent follow-up every 3 months, and “**a neurologist evaluated each patient for possible complications, relapses, disease activity, neurological status and EDSS every 3 months**.” The article further states that four neurologists assessing eligibility and conducting follow-up examinations “**were blinded to the patients’ assignment to treatment group**.” The trial registry likewise records masking of outcome assessors. There is no indication that ascertainment procedures or relapse definitions differed between groups. Thus, outcome measurement was appropriate, comparable across groups, and performed by blinded assessors.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The registered primary outcome was “**attack number**” at “**1 year**,” which is consistent with the article’s stated main outcome: “**the number of the relapses during the 1-year follow-up**.” The article reports the primary comparison for total relapses and additionally reports alternative per-protocol and LOCF analyses.

   However, the trial began in April 2005, whereas the registry was first submitted/postdated in April 2008, near or after the reported primary-completion period (March 2008). Therefore, the registry cannot provide assurance that the outcome measurement, analysis population, statistical analysis, and handling of withdrawals were specified before unblinded outcome data became available. No dated protocol or statistical analysis plan is available that prespecifies the Mann–Whitney analysis, the exclusion of early withdrawals, or the choice among the reported ITT-labelled, per-protocol, and LOCF analyses.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported attack-number result was selected because it was statistically significant. Nonetheless, because a prespecified analysis plan finalized before unblinded data were available is lacking, and multiple eligible analytic approaches were reported, RoB 2 indicates **some concerns**.
