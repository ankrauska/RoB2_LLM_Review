# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Motor Assessment Scale Item 6 (MAS6, Upper Arm Function)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The trial used adequate methods for both sequence generation and allocation concealment. The publication states: "An offsite investigator prepared the concealed randomization using a computer generated random number sequence (1:1)... Consecutively numbered opaque envelopes containing group allocation were collected from the offsite investigator by the tRNS intervener after initial assessment." This satisfies signalling questions 1.1 (Yes) and 1.2 (Yes).

However, baseline characteristics raise concern regarding comparability of the groups on a key prognostic factor: corticospinal tract (CST) structural integrity, which the authors themselves identify as central to potential for motor recovery. Table 3 shows the two active-tRNS participants (P1, P3) had markedly different asymmetry indices than expected balance—P1 had mild AI (0.02) while P3 had severe AI (unable to reconstruct streamlines); similarly the two sham participants (P2, P4) differed (P2 moderate AI 0.11, P4 severe AI, no streamlines). Baseline MAS6 scores also differed within groups (P1=1, P3=0 in active group; P2=2, P4=1 in sham group). With only n=4 (2 per group), no statistical inference regarding imbalance is meaningful, but the very small sample size means baseline differences in known prognostic factors (CST integrity, baseline severity) could bias the assignment-effect estimate, and it is impossible to determine whether these differences are compatible with chance given the tiny sample. Given the extremely small sample size (n=2/arm), any imbalance in prognostic factors is highly likely to influence the result, and there is no information to reassure us this reflects chance alone rather than a problem with randomization consistency of effect. This leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk," per Table 4 (adequate concealment + random sequence + potential baseline imbalance/uncertainty = Some concerns).

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** This was a triple-blind trial: "Study personnel involved in assessment and training, along with all participants were blinded to group allocation for the duration of the study." Only the "unblinded tRNS intervener" knew allocation, and this person was not involved in training delivery, assessment or analysis. Thus participants, carers/trainers delivering the reaching training were blinded (2.1 = No, 2.2 = No for training personnel).

However, tRNS delivery itself was administered by an unblinded intervener, and the paper notes "The tRNS intervener informed participants that they may or may not feel the stimulation irrespective of whether they were receiving active or sham stimulation," suggesting an attempt to preserve blinding despite potential detectable sensory cues from active tRNS (2 mA) versus sham (0 mA). There is no formal check of blinding success reported (e.g., participant guesses of allocation), so it is not possible to fully rule out unblinding effects on training engagement or the trial context influencing motor practice/effort — a possible source of deviation from intended intervention arising from trial context (e.g., differential motivation/effort if a participant perceived their allocation).

Regarding analysis: the protocol states "Analysis will be performed on an intention-to-treat basis," and this is a case series in which all four participants' data (as randomized) were reported and analysed — consistent with ITT principles for this small pilot (signalling question 2.6 = Yes).

Given blinding of participants/trainers was likely successful and analysis was ITT-consistent, but the unblinded status of the tRNS administrator combined with no formal assessment of blinding integrity leaves some uncertainty as to whether deviations arose from trial context (e.g., differential engagement effects related to tactile sensation of active stimulation), the judgement is "Some concerns" rather than "Low."

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** All four randomized participants completed the full course of training and had outcome data recorded at all three time points (baseline, post-training, follow-up). The publication explicitly states: "All four participants completed the 12 training sessions over 4-weeks, with no training sessions missed," and Table 3 presents complete MAS6 data (T0, T1, T2) for all four participants (P01–P04). There is no indication of any missing outcome data for MAS6 at any time point. This satisfies signalling question 3.1 (Yes – data available for all participants), leading to a "Low risk" judgement without need to consider further signalling questions in this domain.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The MAS6 is an observer-reported outcome involving clinical judgement (a performance-based scale scored by a clinician observing upper arm function), rather than an outcome with no room for judgement (e.g., mortality). The method of measurement was appropriate and standard (published, validated scale), and there is no indication that measurement/ascertainment procedures differed between intervention groups (4.1 = No; 4.2 = No).

However, the paper does not explicitly report that the assessors administering the MAS6 were blinded to group allocation, although the triple-blind design states that "people involved in assessment... were blinded to group allocation for the duration of the study" per the trial registration ("Who is/are masked/blinded? ...The people assessing the outcomes"). Taking this at face value, outcome assessors were blinded (4.3 = No), which would normally support a "Low risk" judgement.

Nonetheless, some uncertainty remains: with such a small, resource-intensive trial run within a single research team/department, and given that MAS6 requires subjective judgement in scoring movement quality/completion, there is a residual possibility that inadvertent unblinding could occur (e.g., through interactions with participants who may reveal information, or through subtle cues at follow-up assessments) that is not addressed empirically (e.g., no blinding-success check for assessors was reported). Because MAS6 is a judgement-based outcome where knowledge of allocation could plausibly influence scoring if blinding failed, but there is no direct evidence that unblinding occurred or influenced the outcome, this results in a judgement of "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** MAS6 was pre-specified as the primary outcome measure of the published trial report ("The primary clinical outcome measure was Motor Assessment Scale item 6 (Upper Arm Function, MAS6)"), and it was measured at all three pre-specified time points (baseline, post-training, follow-up) as stated in both the article methods and the trial registration record, which pre-specifies MAS6 (in composite form with items 7–8) as the primary outcome measured at "pre training (week 0), post training (week 4), follow-up (week 12)."

Comparing the registry with the publication: the registry's primary outcome was a composite of MAS items 6–8, whereas the publication designates MAS6 alone as primary and items 7–8 as secondary. This is a minor discrepancy in outcome composition, but all component data (MAS6, item 7, item 8 — implicitly, though only MAS6 numeric values are tabulated in Table 3) appear reported, and there is no evidence that MAS6 was selected from multiple alternative measurements or analyses on the basis of favorable results. There is only one plausible way MAS6 could be analysed (raw score at each time point, as a single-item ordinal scale), and results for all four individual participants are reported transparently for all time points, consistent with the case-series design and stated analysis plan. No evidence of selective outcome/analysis reporting was identified (signalling questions 5.2 = No, 5.3 = No), supporting "Low risk of bias" for this domain.
