# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: HAM-D score (Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression)
## Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The allocation sequence was generated randomly using a computer, and allocation was concealed via an independent pharmacy using presealed envelopes with a block design.

- *Sequence generation:* "Random assignment to treatment conditions (sertraline or placebo) was balanced by using a block design (blocks of 8) with computer-generated random number sequences in individual presealed envelopes." → clearly random.
- *Allocation concealment:* "The pharmacy implemented masking and random assignment of participants to sertraline or placebo conditions and was responsible for dispensing medications with identical markings to the project physicians for distribution... both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received." This central pharmacy-based concealment process is adequate; it is independent of enrolling researchers/physicians.
- *Baseline imbalance:* Baseline HAM-D scores were similar (27.5±7.1 vs 25.2±8.0) and no significant differences were found for age, sex, education, race, etiology, time since injury, or injury severity. The only statistically significant baseline difference was income ("The income level of those receiving placebo was significantly lower than those participants receiving medication"). Given the large number of baseline comparisons made (age, sex, education, race, income, etiology, severity, time since injury, and 3 baseline outcome measures), a single significant difference is compatible with chance (~1 in 20 expected) rather than indicative of a randomization failure. No extreme p-values (e.g., <0.001) were reported for this imbalance.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Blinding of participants/personnel:* This was a "double-blind, randomized controlled trial." Medications had "identical markings," and dosage titration was pill-count based so as to be indistinguishable between arms. "Both study staff and participants were blinded to the type of intervention received."
- *Deviations from trial context:* There is no evidence that non-protocol interventions or trial-context-driven deviations occurred differentially between groups. Participants were required to "abstain from seeking new psychosocial or pharmacologic treatments during the course of the intervention," reducing risk of non-protocol co-intervention.
- *Appropriate analysis:* The effect of assignment to intervention was analyzed via an intention-to-treat approach; a "last-observation-carried-forward procedure combined the 2 participants who completed 8 weeks with those who completed all 10 weeks," and "random-effects models for repeated measures with 6 time points were conducted to assess within- and between-group differences," with participants analyzed according to their randomized group. This is consistent with an appropriate (m)ITT approach for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Extent of missing data:* Of 52 randomized participants, only 41 (79%) were included in the analyses reported in Table 2 and used for the HAM-D group comparisons (22/27 sertraline = 81%; 19/25 placebo = 76%). This is a non-trivial proportion (~21%) of missing outcome data, not "nearly all."
- *No evidence result was unbiased:* While an LOCF approach was used for 2 late dropouts, there was no formal sensitivity analysis addressing the impact of the remaining 9 participants with no post-baseline outcome data. The authors' claim that "the findings are robust despite the small sample" is not substantiated by a quantitative sensitivity analysis.
- *Likely dependence of missingness on true outcome value:* The authors explicitly report: "The intent-to-treat analyses found differences between the last assessment HAM-D score for those who dropped out of treatment as compared with the assessment HAM-D score of those participants who remained in treatment (F=4.0, P=.052), with the dropouts tending to have higher HAM-D scores." This directly indicates that missingness in the HAM-D outcome was related to its (worse) true value.
- *Differential dropout:* Dropout was somewhat higher in the placebo group (6/25=24%) than sertraline (5/27=18.5%), and reasons for withdrawal differed by group (e.g., "a second [participant withdrew] because of increased depression after a death in the family," "a third because of a mild allergic reaction," both in placebo)—suggesting reasons for missingness may relate to clinical status and could differ subtly across arms.

Given that missingness plausibly depended on the true HAM-D value, and there was no adequate correction/sensitivity analysis, this domain is judged as high risk.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- *Appropriateness of method:* HAM-D is a standard, validated clinician-rated instrument ("high reliability and validity") appropriate for measuring depression severity.
- *Comparability of measurement across groups:* The same rating protocol and time points were used identically for both intervention arms; "research staff followed a standard protocol, and the duration of each contact was kept constant."
- *Blinding of outcome assessors:* The HAM-D was administered by "a postdoctoral fellow or licensed psychologist" who rated symptoms "independently from the physician," and "participants were not necessarily seen by the same staff member (i.e., physician or psychologist) at each visit." Given identical-appearing medication and the double-blind design, outcome assessors were plausibly unaware of allocation. The HAM-D is an observer-reported outcome involving some judgement, so awareness of group assignment could in principle influence scoring; however, there is no indication that blinding of assessors was compromised (e.g., no report of high rates of side-effect-driven unblinding — only 1 sertraline participant discontinued due to side effects, and adverse event rates were low and similar between groups: 1/22 sertraline vs 2/19 placebo for "other" events).

Because blinding of assessors was maintained and there is no evidence that measurement/ascertainment differed systematically between groups, risk of bias for this domain is low.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registry (NCT00233103) prospectively specifies "Depression at Baseline" and "Depression at End of Treatment," both explicitly operationalized via the HAM-D score, as primary outcomes, with the analysis approach (group comparison, responder definition as "≥50% reduction in HAM-D or HAM-D<10") matching what is reported in the publication.
- The publication reports the pre-specified HAM-D total score result completely, using both the group-level comparisons (ANCOVA, random-effects modeling) that were planned a priori (power calculations described in detail based on the HAM-D, citing a pre-specified analytic approach from Diggle et al.).
- While the article additionally reports several post hoc "alternate HAM-D" subscales (Beck Melancholia, Maier & Philipp, Gibbons, 5 most sensitive) that were not part of the original registered outcome, these are reported in addition to, not instead of, the primary total HAM-D score, and the paper transparently frames these as exploratory ("some researchers have indicated that unidimensional subscales may be more effective..."). There is no indication that the total HAM-D result itself was selected from multiple analyses on the basis of significance; the full null-result for the primary total HAM-D comparison is reported "the group effect was NS" despite it being an unfavorable/non-significant finding for the sponsor's product (sertraline manufacturer, Pfizer, co-funded the study), arguing against selective suppression.

Given consistency between the registered outcome definition/analysis plan and what is reported for the HAM-D score, this domain is judged as low risk.
