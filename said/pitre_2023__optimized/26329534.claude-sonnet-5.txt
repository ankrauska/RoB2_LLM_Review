# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Response Rate Outcome

**Preliminary note:** This trial randomizes pharmacists to different survey delivery methods (postal mail, email, hybrid), questionnaire lengths, item placements, and examines gender, with "response rate" as the outcome. This is somewhat atypical for RoB 2, but I will apply the tool as directed, treating "assignment to delivery method/design condition" as the intervention, and the effect of assignment to intervention (ITT) as the estimand of interest.

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise primarily from lack of information about allocation concealment and sequence generation methods, despite baseline characteristics appearing plausible given random allocation.

**b. Rationale:** The article states: "A random sample of 7200 pharmacists practicing throughout the U.S...was used... The sample was stratified by delivery method, questionnaire length, item placement, and gender of respondent for a total of 300 observations within each subgroup." No further detail is provided on the specific method used to generate the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers) or on concealment of allocation prior to assignment. There is no explicit statement of a randomization procedure beyond "random sample" and "stratified," which conflates sampling with allocation. Given the observational Table 1 data, there are notable differences in baseline characteristics between delivery-method arms (e.g., ethnic composition: 86.3% Non-Hispanic White in postal mail vs. 68.7% in email vs. 74.0% in hybrid; hospital practice site 19.0% postal vs 35.0% email vs 13.7% hybrid), which could reflect either chance or differences arising from the fact that different sampling vendors (MMS vs. Statlistics) were used for different arms ("provided by the Medical Marketing Service (for the postal mail and hybrid groups) and Statlistics (for the email group)"). This is a substantive concern: using different sampling frames/vendors for different intervention arms could introduce a systematic imbalance not attributable to chance, raising doubt about whether the randomization process (to the extent one existed) was successful in creating comparable groups. However, because no formal randomization method is described in detail, and there's no evidence of manipulation, this raises "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as this is an unblinded but structurally-enforced intervention (delivery method) where deviation from assignment was essentially impossible, and results were analyzed according to assigned groups.

**b. Rationale:** Participants and personnel could not be “blinded” to delivery method (participants necessarily know whether they received postal mail, email, or hybrid), so the response to signalling questions 2.1/2.2 is "Yes." However, deviations from intended interventions arising because of trial context are not plausible here: pharmacists assigned to receive a postal survey could not somehow receive and respond via a different delivery method assigned to another arm, and there is no mechanism by which trial context (e.g., informed consent process) would cause participants to switch delivery mode. The methods indicate consistent, protocol-driven implementation: "Pharmacists who were randomized to the postal mail group were sent an envelope containing a cover letter..."; "Pharmacists who were randomized to the email group were sent an email message..."; "Pharmacists who were randomized to the hybrid group were mailed a postcard..." These procedures were implemented as intended for all participants in each arm, and the analysis (response rate calculated as returned/net delivered per assigned group) reflects an appropriate ITT-consistent analysis by assigned group. There is no indication of cross-over between delivery methods. Therefore, there is no plausible deviation from intended intervention affecting the response-rate outcome.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, due to the substantial number of non-returned surveys and the potential for informative "missingness" of the response-rate outcome relative to true underlying propensity to respond, though the outcome itself (response/non-response) is directly observed for essentially all randomized participants (i.e., non-response IS the outcome, not missing data in the traditional sense) — this attenuates most concern, but incomplete/partial returns dropped from the "completed" numerator raise concern.

**b. Rationale:** The response rate outcome is defined as: "the percentage of eligible respondents that returned completed questionnaires, with no missing data for any questions." Critically, the study explicitly excludes returned-but-incomplete surveys from the numerator: "suffered from a relatively high prevalence (5.8%) of undeliverable questionnaires and a high prevalence (9.9%) of responses dropped because of incomplete data" (postal mail), and "a similar prevalence (9.4%) of dropped responses because of incomplete data" (email). This means the outcome as analyzed is not simply "did the pharmacist respond" (which would be observed for all randomized/allocated individuals with certainty), but rather a more restrictive complete-response definition, from which some returned surveys were excluded. Because the exclusion of "incomplete" returns could plausibly differ in nature or frequency between delivery arms (different formats may lead to different patterns of partial completion — e.g., online surveys allow proceeding without answering every question: "A response to each question was not required in order for the survey to proceed to the next question"), this could differentially affect the numerator across arms in ways related to the true value of the underlying outcome (willingness/ability to fully complete). Additionally, "undeliverable" surveys (233 total; disproportionately postal mail and hybrid, per Figure 1: 139 postal and 94 hybrid undeliverable vs 0 email) are removed from the denominator rather than treated as non-response, which could bias comparative response rate estimates between arms if undeliverable status correlates with prognostic factors. No sensitivity analysis addressing these exclusions was reported. This leads to "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk," since the amount of excluded data is non-trivial and could plausibly relate to the outcome, but there is not clear evidence that it was likely to bias the specific comparison (as it plausibly reflects real differential characteristics of delivery methods rather than a flaw introduced by trial conduct).

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias — the outcome (response rate) is an objective, observer-recorded count (returned/completed questionnaires) with no subjective judgement or scope for differential assessment based on knowledge of the intervention assignment in a way that would bias measurement.

**b. Rationale:** Response rate is calculated administratively: "the net number was then divided into the number of returned questionnaires completed, with no missing data for any question." This is an observer-reported outcome not involving judgement (akin to counting mortality events) — the method of ascertaining whether a questionnaire was "returned" and "complete" was consistent and mechanical (received or not received; missing data or not), applied identically to all groups via the same objective counting process regardless of assigned delivery method. There is no meaningful risk that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced the counting of returned/completed surveys, since receipt/completion status is objectively verifiable (a returned physical envelope, a submitted database entry) and not subject to assessor interpretation. The method of measurement did not differ inappropriately between intervention groups in a way likely to bias comparisons of response rate itself (differences in delivery mechanics are the exposure being tested, not a measurement artifact for this particular outcome).

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, due to the absence of a pre-registered/pre-specified analysis plan and an inability to confirm that the reported outcome definition and analyses were not selected based on results.

**b. Rationale:** No trial registration record was identified/available for this study (the assessment prompt indicates a registration doc_id was provided, but no registry content is reflected in the given article text, and the article itself contains no reference to a registered protocol, trial registry number, or pre-specified statistical analysis plan). The article defines the response rate outcome post hoc in the Methods with a specific restrictive definition (completed surveys with "no missing data for any questions"), and separately reports a "combined response rate of questionnaires with no missing data plus some data missing (23.3%)" as an alternate figure for postal mail, comparing it to a prior survey's 25.5%. The presence of multiple plausible outcome definitions (completed-only vs. combined completed+incomplete) that are both reported, with the primary analysis emphasizing the completed-only definition, combined with no evidence of a prior analysis plan specifying this choice, raises the possibility that outcome definition/analysis approach could have been influenced by the results, although there is no direct evidence of selective suppression of unfavorable results. Given the lack of a protocol/registration to confirm pre-specification, but no clear indication of selection based on results, "Some concerns" is appropriate rather than "High risk."

---

## Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

Per Table 1 criteria, since the study is judged to raise "Some concerns" in multiple domains (randomization, missing outcome data, selective reporting) but is not judged "High risk" in any single domain, the overall judgement is **Some concerns**.
