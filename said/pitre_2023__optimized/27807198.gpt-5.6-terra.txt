1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** The trial was described as randomized, placebo-controlled and double-blinded, with 1:1 allocation. Importantly, the randomization list and study medication were managed by an organization separate from the recruiting study centre: “*The randomisation list and the study medication were administered by the Charité, Universitätsmedizin, Berlin, Germany.*” This provides reasonable assurance that allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants.  
   
   The report does not specify how the random sequence itself was generated; however, management by an external academic institution in a registered, placebo-controlled phase II trial makes a genuinely random sequence probable. Initial allocation was close to the intended 1:1 ratio (7 doxycycline, 6 placebo). Although the analysed groups had differences in age and baseline MMSE (median age 58 versus 72 years; MMSE 21 versus 16), the very small sample means these differences can plausibly have arisen by chance. There is no direct evidence that the randomization process was manipulated or failed.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, for the effect of assignment to doxycycline versus placebo at baseline.  
   b. **Rationale:** This was a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial: “*Patients were randomly assigned (ratio 1:1) to receive either doxycycline or placebo*,” and the trial is described as “*double-blinded*.” Therefore, participants, carers and intervention providers were unlikely to know assigned treatment, reducing the likelihood of trial-context-related differential co-interventions or departures from protocol.  
   
   Four participants discontinued in the doxycycline group and one placebo participant was lost; however, for the effect of assignment, treatment discontinuation itself does not cause bias unless it arose through the trial context and affected outcomes differentially. The report provides no indication of such deviations or differential non-protocol care. The survival analysis retained all seven randomized doxycycline participants, and excluded only the participant with unavailable outcome data in the placebo group. This is consistent with a modified intention-to-treat analysis; the consequence of the unavailable outcome is considered in the missing-outcome-data domain.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** Of 13 randomized participants, one participant assigned to placebo dropped out and was excluded from the reported survival analysis: “*One patient who was randomised to the placebo group dropped out, so that 12 patients with sCJD were included in the reported survival analysis.*” The flowchart likewise shows that five of six placebo participants were analysed, whereas all seven doxycycline participants were analysed.  
   
   Although only one participant was missing, this represents approximately 8% of randomized participants and 17% of the placebo group. In a trial of only 13 patients, the survival experience of a single placebo participant could materially affect the hazard-ratio estimate. The reason for dropout, survival status, and censoring time of this participant are not reported. For time-to-event outcomes, loss to follow-up may be informative if it is related to subsequent mortality. There is no sensitivity analysis or other evidence demonstrating that exclusion of this participant did not affect the result. However, the available information does not establish that missingness was likely related to the participant’s true survival time; therefore, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Survival time/death is an objective outcome that is not meaningfully susceptible to differential assessment based on knowledge of treatment allocation. The study defined follow-up in the randomized trial as beginning with randomization and ending at “*date of death or end of the study, whichever occurs first*.”  
   
   The trial was double-blinded, providing further protection against differential outcome ascertainment. The investigators also state that the investigator determining disease-onset date was blinded to treatment; however, the randomized-trial survival analysis was based on time from randomization, rather than from onset. There is no indication that deaths were measured differently in the doxycycline and placebo groups or that the ascertainment method was inappropriate.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The EudraCT registration, entered before the reported trial was completed, prespecified the primary endpoint as survival time “*from symptom onset*” until death (“*Überlebenszeit der Patienten von Symptombeginn an*”). In contrast, the publication specifies that, for the randomized study, “*follow-up for survival starts with the randomisation (first application of study medication)*.” The reported randomized-trial result therefore uses a different survival-time origin from that prespecified in the registry.  
   
   This discrepancy is potentially important because time from symptom onset to treatment/placebo initiation differed between the randomized groups (median 95 days in the doxycycline group versus 152 days in controls). The publication does not provide the registered primary analysis of survival from symptom onset for the randomized comparison, nor does it explain the change in outcome definition/time origin.  
   
   Nevertheless, the registry clearly identifies survival as the primary outcome and there is no direct evidence that the reported analysis was selected because it was statistically favourable. The registry does not provide a detailed, date-stamped statistical analysis plan specifying the survival-analysis method, censoring rules, or alternative analyses. Thus, there are some concerns about selective reporting, but insufficient evidence for a high-risk judgement.
