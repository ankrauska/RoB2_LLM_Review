# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Pain VAS Scale (Post-treatment)
## Effect of Interest: Assignment to intervention (ITT effect)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The allocation sequence was adequately generated using random permuted blocks by an independent statistician (low risk for sequence generation). However, there is no information reported about allocation concealment, and baseline characteristics show a numerically higher baseline VAS in the iontophoresis group (6.3) compared with ultrasound and phonophoresis groups (5.7 each), though this was not statistically significant.

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: *"Each participant was assigned to one treatment group, at random, using a numbered list generated through the random permuted blocks method, applied by a statistician. The statistician was not involved in data collection or analysis."* This supports "Yes" for random sequence generation.
- Allocation concealment: No information is provided on whether the allocation sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants (e.g., no mention of sealed envelopes, central allocation, etc.). This leads to "No information" on the concealment signalling question.
- Baseline imbalance: Table 1 shows baseline characteristics were reported as homogeneous (all P>0.05), and the paper explicitly states *"At baseline, there were no differences between the groups"* and *"the groups were homogeneous not only for these variables but also for sex, age, affected shoulder and intake of analgesics."* The differences appear compatible with chance.
- Per the RoB2 algorithm: Random sequence = Yes/Probably yes, Concealment = No information, Baseline imbalance = No → this maps to "Some concerns."

---

## 2. Bias due to deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** This was a single-blind trial in which only participants (not necessarily fully, given the nature of interventions) were blinded, and it is very likely that personnel/therapists delivering treatment were aware of intervention assignment (the interventions are physically distinguishable - ultrasound alone vs. phonophoresis with gel vs. iontophoresis with electrical current and solution). There is no discussion of deviations from intended intervention arising from trial context, but critically, the outcome assessor (VAS pain, self-reported) is the participant, and blinding of participants to a drug-containing vs. non-drug physical modality is difficult to guarantee, especially as side effects/sensations from dexketoprofen (e.g., skin reactions, sensations from electrical current in iontophoresis vs ultrasound) could unblind participants.

**b. Rationale:**
- Design description: *"A randomised, single-blind experimental clinical trial"* - only participants are noted as masked per protocol, but the treating physiotherapists (personnel delivering intervention) were clearly aware of group assignment, as they administered physically distinct treatments (ultrasound machine only vs. gel-based phonophoresis vs. electrical current iontophoresis).
- *"The treatments were provided by physiotherapists following standardized protocols. The physiotherapists did not collect data and did not evaluate the results."* This suggests attempt to isolate assessment from intervention delivery, but the outcome (VAS) is self-reported by the participant, not by an independent blinded assessor.
- There is no explicit statement confirming that participants were successfully blinded to which modality they received — the three modalities (ultrasound gel/probe, dexketoprofen gel with ultrasound, electrical current iontophoresis) are sensorially distinguishable (e.g., iontophoresis involves electrical current sensation different from ultrasound), making true blinding of participants questionable despite the "single-blind" label.
- No information is provided about whether deviations from intended intervention occurred due to trial context, and no appropriate sensitivity analysis or discussion addresses this.
- Given the strong likelihood that participants could distinguish interventions (differential sensory experience) and that the primary outcome is self-reported pain (highly susceptible to expectation effects), and no evidence blinding was verified or maintained, this raises the risk to "High" for this domain, since deviations affecting a subjective, participant-reported outcome are plausible and unaddressed.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Outcome data appear to be available for all or nearly all randomized participants at the post-treatment timepoint, based on the reported sample sizes matching the original allocation.

**b. Rationale:**
- *"Ninety-nine participants with SIS...were assigned at random to three intervention groups"* with n=32, n=33, n=34 respectively, and Table 1 and Table 2 report data consistent with these full group sizes.
- *"No adverse events were documented in any of the treatment groups"* suggesting no withdrawals due to safety issues.
- The results tables (Table 2 and 3) present data for all three time points without indication of participant dropout or exclusion for the post-treatment VAS outcome.
- No CONSORT flow diagram data is provided in the extracted text to confirm exact dropout numbers, but the consistent reporting of full baseline sample sizes in the results with no mention of missing data or exclusions for this outcome supports a "Low risk" judgement at this timepoint.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** VAS is a participant-reported outcome. The trial was only single-blind (participants), but the treating personnel (and by extension, potentially the participants themselves through sensory cues) were aware of intervention assignment. Since VAS pain is self-reported by the participant (who is the "outcome assessor" for this measure), and the interventions are sensorially distinguishable (electrical current in iontophoresis vs. ultrasound probe/gel), it is likely that knowledge of intervention assignment could have influenced the participant's pain reporting, particularly given the trial's hypothesis-driven expectation that dexketoprofen-based treatments would be superior.

**b. Rationale:**
- The outcome assessor for VAS is the participant themselves (per RoB2 guidance on participant-reported outcomes).
- The trial is described as single-blind, blinding only participants nominally, but the physical differences between ultrasound (no current), phonophoresis (gel + ultrasound), and iontophoresis (electrical current, different device, longer duration - 20 min vs 5 min) make true participant blinding highly implausible. Different session durations alone (5 min vs 5 min vs 20 min) would reveal group assignment to participants.
- There is no description of any sham/placebo procedures (e.g., sham current, matched session durations) to preserve blinding, and no formal assessment of blinding success (e.g., asking participants to guess their allocation) is reported.
- Because participants likely knew their allocation (due to differing session durations/sensations) and the outcome is a subjective, self-reported pain score, and given that the trial's hypothesis favored the drug-augmented treatments, it is likely that assessment of pain (VAS) was influenced by participants' awareness of their treatment allocation — leading to "High risk of bias."

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The trial registry (NCT01748188) pre-specifies VAS pain as the primary outcome, and this is reported in the publication at all three timepoints (baseline, post-treatment, 1 month) with full data presented in tables. There is no evidence of selective outcome reporting for the VAS measurement itself. However, no statistical analysis plan (SAP) or full protocol with pre-specified analysis methods was available to confirm whether the specific analyses reported (e.g., pairwise comparisons via Scheffé post-hoc test) were pre-specified before unblinded data were available.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registration confirms: *"Pain measure by the visual analogic scale pain score"* as the primary outcome, matching the outcome assessed in the publication at multiple time points as described in Table 2 and Table 3.
- VAS was measured only once per timepoint (no evidence of multiple scales/instruments for pain from which to selectively choose), reducing risk of selective outcome measurement.
- However, the analysis approach (repeated measures ANOVA with post-hoc Scheffé test, and separate baseline-difference analyses in Table 3) is not confirmed against a pre-registered/pre-specified statistical analysis plan; no SAP was available for review, and the protocol registry entry does not specify detailed analysis methods (e.g., adjustment sets, exact statistical tests) finalized prior to unblinding.
- Because there is no information available to confirm the analysis was pre-specified before unblinded data was accessed, but there is no strong indication of results-based selection (multiple outcome measurements/analyses being selectively reported) either, this leads to a judgement of "Some concerns" rather than "Low" or "High."

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **High risk of bias**

**Rationale:** Per RoB 2.0 guidance, a judgement of "High risk of bias" in any single domain results in an overall "High risk of bias" judgement. This trial was judged high risk for two domains: (2) deviations from intended intervention, due to inadequate blinding of personnel/participants given the sensorially distinguishable interventions, and (4) measurement of the outcome, given the self-reported, subjective nature of the VAS pain outcome combined with probable unblinding due to differences in intervention delivery (e.g., different session durations, presence/absence of electrical current). These two "High risk" domains compound to substantially lower confidence in the reported treatment effect on the primary outcome (VAS pain, post-treatment).
