1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants “were stratified” according to recent self-reported drug/alcohol use and duration of negative urine drug screens, and “were then randomized into one of the three study conditions.” This supports that random allocation was intended. However, no method for generating the allocation sequence (e.g., computer-generated sequence, random-number table, blocking) is reported, and there is no information on allocation concealment before enrolment/assignment.

   The numbers initially randomized (routine care 25, clinic-based MMM 27, office-based MMM 26) are not markedly inconsistent with intended approximately equal allocation. However, baseline characteristics are reported only for the overall sample, not separately by randomized group, so group comparability cannot be assessed. The subsequent imbalance in the analysed sample—particularly four withdrawals immediately after assignment to routine care—is a post-randomization issue and does not itself demonstrate a defective randomization process. Thus, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**, rather than low risk of bias.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** This assessment concerns the effect of assignment at baseline. The interventions were necessarily open-label: patients knew whether they were assigned to routine clinic care, clinic-based monthly MMM, or physician-office MMM; carers and intervention providers would also have known the assigned setting and dispensing schedule.

   More importantly, the reported retention analysis did not include all randomized participants. Although 78 patients were randomized, the authors state: “A total of five patients withdrew from the study upon assignment to study condition; four following assignment to the routine treatment condition and one following assignment to the MMM physician office-based condition.” The retention analysis was then conducted in an “intent-to-treat sample (n=73),” excluding these five randomized patients.

   This is not a full intention-to-treat analysis of the effect of assignment, because post-randomization withdrawals were excluded. The exclusions were strongly imbalanced: four of the five occurred after allocation to routine care. Since treatment retention is the outcome under assessment, excluding patients who withdrew immediately after allocation is especially likely to materially affect the result. The analysis therefore has substantial potential to bias the estimated effect of assignment, likely making retention in routine care appear better than it would have been had all randomized participants been included.

   The stepped-care intensification procedures appear protocol-defined and applied across groups in response to positive urine tests or failed medication recalls. These procedures do not by themselves establish bias in the effect of assignment. However, the inappropriate exclusion of randomized participants from the retention analysis warrants **high risk of bias** in this domain.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:** Among the 73 participants included in the reported analysis, retention status at 6 months appears available for all participants. The study reports that 19/21 routine-care participants, 24/27 clinic-based MMM participants, and 23/25 office-based MMM participants completed 6 months of randomized care, and it describes reasons for the seven participants who dropped out of the randomized treatment arm.

   For a retention outcome, leaving treatment/randomized care is itself the event of interest rather than missing outcome information. The authors used survival analysis to compare retention, which is appropriate for incorporating observed timing of treatment discontinuation. The five participants excluded immediately after randomization are an analysis-population problem addressed under deviations from intended interventions, rather than missing outcome data among the analysed 73 participants. There is no indication of additional unascertained retention status or informative loss of follow-up among these 73 participants.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Treatment retention was operationalized as completion versus dropout from randomized care over 6 months, with the authors reporting completion counts and reasons for leaving the randomized treatment arm. This is an objective, routinely ascertainable outcome based on treatment participation/clinic records, rather than a subjective participant-reported outcome.

   Although participants and treatment personnel were aware of intervention assignment, awareness is unlikely to have biased the *measurement* or classification of whether a participant remained in randomized treatment. The groups were monitored under the same trial framework, and there is no evidence that different definitions, thresholds, or ascertainment procedures for retention were applied across groups. Therefore, the measurement of retention is at **low risk of bias**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article describes an ongoing 12-month trial but reports results for only the first 6 months: “Results are presented for the first 6 months of the 1-year trial.” The methods state prospectively that “no group differences were expected on measures of drug use, retention or medication recall problems,” and a retention analysis is reported, which is consistent with retention having been an intended outcome.

   However, no sufficiently detailed, date-stamped pre-specified statistical analysis plan is available in the provided material. In particular, the available report does not establish that the reported 6-month retention analysis, survival-analysis method, handling of the five immediately post-randomization withdrawals, and choice of reported follow-up time were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The referenced trial registration was not available in the material supplied here to verify these points.

   There is no direct evidence that the reported retention result was selected from multiple analyses or time points because of its findings. Nevertheless, the absence of a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan and the interim 6-month reporting from a planned 12-month study result in **some concerns** about selective reporting.
