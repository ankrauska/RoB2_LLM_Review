1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The report describes an adequately random allocation process and allocation concealment. Participants were assigned “using a **randomization automated system on a one-to-one basis**.” The authors further state that “the **randomization list was held securely, and released only after study completion**,” which supports concealment of the allocation sequence until assignment.

   The groups were equal in size at baseline (20 participants each), and the reported baseline SOWS mean values (gabapentin 12.6; placebo 14.9) do not indicate an implausible imbalance. The authors also state that there were no significant baseline differences in age or methadone dosage. Although detailed baseline characteristics are limited, there is no evidence that baseline differences reflect a failure of randomization.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This was described as a “**double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial**.” Gabapentin and placebo were “**dispensed in identical appearing capsules**,” and placebo participants took the same number of capsules as participants allocated to gabapentin. These procedures make it unlikely that participants, carers, or staff delivering the intervention were aware of assigned treatment.

   There is no reported evidence of deviations from intended intervention that arose specifically because of the trial context and that were unbalanced between groups. Both groups received the same methadone-assisted detoxification programme and could receive symptomatic medication if required. The authors report no significant difference in methadone or symptomatic-medication doses between groups.

   Three placebo-group participants were excluded for non-compliance and illicit/over-the-counter drug use. However, for the assignment-effect assessment, these exclusions primarily result in missing outcome data and are considered in the missing-outcome-data domain. The reported repeated-measures analysis retained participants in their randomized groups while outcome data were available; it was not an as-treated analysis.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data were not available for all randomized participants at the later follow-up assessments. All 20 gabapentin participants remained in the analysis, whereas the placebo group fell from 20 participants at baseline to 19 at day 14 and 17 at day 18. Thus, at the final reported assessment, outcome data were missing for 3/20 (15%) participants in the placebo group and none in the gabapentin group.

   The reasons for missing data are plausibly related to the true value of the outcome. The authors state that one placebo participant was excluded at day 14 and two at day 17 because of “**non-compliance with the scheduled treatment programme and self-medication with illicit and/or over-the-counter (OTC) drugs**.” They further suggest that this “**may be due to the greater severity of withdrawal symptoms**.” Since the outcome is subjective opioid-withdrawal severity, discontinuation/non-compliance due to withdrawal severity is directly related to the likely unobserved SOWS scores.

   Missingness was also differential between groups, occurring only in the placebo group. No sensitivity analysis or appropriately justified method for addressing the potential bias from missing SOWS data was reported. The combination of appreciable, differential missingness and reasons plausibly associated with withdrawal severity supports a high-risk judgement.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was measured using the **Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS)**, a participant-reported scale comprising 16 withdrawal symptoms rated from 0 to 4. The report states that the scale “**has been shown to be a valid and reliable indicator of the severity of the opiate withdrawal syndrome**.” Thus, the measurement method appears appropriate for subjective withdrawal symptoms.

   Because this was a double-blind placebo-controlled trial using identical-appearing capsules, participants—the outcome assessors for this self-reported outcome—were unlikely to know their assigned intervention. The same SOWS measurement approach and scheduled assessment time points were used for both groups. There is no evidence that ascertainment or measurement of SOWS differed systematically between intervention groups.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication states that SOWS was administered at six time points and reports a repeated-measures analysis of withdrawal-symptom severity across follow-up. However, no sufficiently detailed, date-stamped statistical analysis plan is available in the provided trial report to establish that the specific SOWS analysis and reported result were prespecified before unblinded outcome data became available.

   There are also internal inconsistencies in the reporting of assessment timing. The methods describe assessments on days 0, 4, 7, 10, 18 and 21, whereas Table 1 presents results for baseline, day 3, day 7, day 10, day 14 and day 18. This discrepancy makes it difficult to verify which time points were prespecified and whether all intended measurements were reported consistently.

   The report presents several possible analyses of the SOWS data, including total symptom scores at individual time points, a repeated-measures analysis adjusted for age and methadone dose, a Mann–Whitney U-test, and analyses of individual withdrawal symptoms. In the absence of a prespecified analysis plan or sufficiently detailed registration information confirming the planned outcome time point and analytic approach, it cannot be excluded that the reported analysis was selected from multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses. There is no direct evidence that selective reporting occurred; therefore, the appropriate judgement is some concerns rather than high risk.
