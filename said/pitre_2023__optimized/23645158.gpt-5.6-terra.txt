1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence appears genuinely random, and reported baseline characteristics were similar between groups, but allocation concealment was not described.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors state that patients “were allocated into one of the two groups based on a **computer random numbers table**,” which supports a random sequence-generation process. The two groups contained 44 participants each, and Table 1 shows no important baseline imbalances in measured prognostic variables, including age, Euroscore, ejection fraction, creatinine, haematocrit, and preoperative NT-pro-BNP.

   However, the report does not state who generated the sequence, who enrolled participants, or how the allocation was concealed before enrolment and assignment. There is no description of central randomization, pharmacy-controlled allocation, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. Under RoB 2, absence of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns** when baseline data do not suggest a problem with randomization.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to magnesium sulfate versus placebo at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** This was described as a “**double-blind clinical trial**” using normal saline placebo. The intervention procedures were standardized: the magnesium group received MgSO4 15 mg/kg/h from induction of anaesthesia until skin closure, while the control group received the same volume of saline through a 50-mL syringe. The authors state that, apart from MgSO4 or placebo, “**the anesthesia methods and protocols for the two patient groups were exactly the same**,” and that the same surgical, cardiopulmonary bypass, and postoperative-care protocols were used.

   These features make deviations caused by awareness of treatment allocation or by the trial context unlikely. The analysis compared the randomized groups, with 44 participants reported in each group in the outcome table. Although the authors do not explicitly label the analysis as intention-to-treat, there is no clear evidence that participants were analysed according to treatment received or that eligible randomized participants were excluded from the NT-pro-BNP analysis. Therefore, the analysis is considered appropriate for the effect of assignment to intervention.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:** Eighty-eight participants were enrolled and randomized, with 44 participants in each group. Table 3 reports NT-pro-BNP results for the MgSO4 group (*n* = 44) and placebo group (*n* = 44), corresponding to all 88 enrolled participants. Thus, data for the postoperative NT-pro-BNP outcome appear to have been available for all randomized participants.

   The report does mention that patients requiring triple inotropic therapy during weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass would be excluded and that the number excluded would be registered. However, no exclusions are reported in the results, and the reported group totals remain 44 per group. There is consequently no indication of missing 24-hour NT-pro-BNP data or differential missingness between groups.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** NT-pro-BNP was measured using an objective laboratory method: “**an electrochemical luminescence immunoassay in an Elecsys 2010**” analyser. The report provides assay performance details, including a coefficient of variation of 2.2–3.2% and detection range of 5–35,000 pg/mL. Blood collection, centrifugation, storage, and transfer conditions were specified and were reportedly “**kept consistent throughout the study**.”

   The same sampling schedule was used in both groups—before anaesthesia and 24 hours after ICU arrival—and the trial was double blind. NT-pro-BNP is an objectively measured biochemical outcome, for which outcome assessment is unlikely to be materially influenced by knowledge of the assigned intervention. There is no indication that laboratory measurement or ascertainment differed between groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies postoperative NT-pro-BNP as the “**main study end point**” and reports the prespecified-looking 24-hour postoperative result. It also reports baseline NT-pro-BNP values. However, no protocol or statistical analysis plan finalized before outcome data became available is provided in the report.

   The study is stated to have begun in January 2010 and lasted 12 months, whereas the reported IRCT registration identifier is “**IRCT201205069650N1**,” which indicates that registration occurred after study commencement and apparently after the stated recruitment period. Therefore, the registration cannot provide assurance that the NT-pro-BNP measurement time point, analytic approach, or reporting strategy was prospectively specified before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The authors acknowledge that “**we just assessed two points of the serum levels of NT pro-BNP; while the trend of the marker or the ‘area under curve’ (AUC) was not measured**.” Although this does not demonstrate selective reporting, multiple potentially eligible analytic approaches were possible (for example, postoperative value, change from baseline, or baseline-adjusted analysis), and the absence of a prospectively finalized analysis plan prevents exclusion of selective selection of the reported result. There is no direct evidence that the reported 24-hour result was selected because of its statistical significance; therefore, this is judged as **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.
