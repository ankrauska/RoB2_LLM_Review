**Scope note:** The randomized comparisons are the Phase 2 allocations within each faculty (Trento: intensive clinical tutoring versus routine tutoring; Udine: weekly tutorials versus routine tutoring). Phase 1 compares the two faculties’ existing educational approaches (laboratory session in Trento versus no formal strategy in Udine) and is not a randomized between-group comparison. The judgements below therefore apply to the Phase 2 randomized result for the main outcome, *number of wrong diagnostic hypotheses*. A causal estimate for the Phase 1 between-faculty comparison would require assessment as a non-randomized study.

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**
   
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.
   
   b. **Rationale:** The report states that students were randomized using a randomization table: “*the students enrolled in the research project were consequently randomised with a randomisation table into two different groups*.” This supports use of a random allocation sequence.
   
   However, the report does not state who generated or held the allocation table, whether allocation was concealed from those recruiting/enrolling students, or whether group assignment could have been foreseen before enrolment. Thus, allocation concealment cannot be confirmed.
   
   Baseline comparability within the Phase 2 randomized groups is partly reassuring: Table 2 states that there was “*no significant difference between Trial and Control groups in each group*” for the reported characteristics. Nevertheless, arm-specific baseline data are not fully presented, and the information is insufficient to eliminate concern about the randomization process. Under RoB 2, a random sequence with no information about concealment leads to **some concerns**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
   
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.
   
   b. **Rationale:** Students and tutors were necessarily aware of the educational strategy received/delivered. For example, intensive tutoring involved daily tutor-led debriefing, weekly tutorials involved weekly debriefing, and routine tutoring had no systematic formal reflective strategy. Thus, blinding of participants and intervention providers was not feasible.
   
   However, for the assignment-effect estimand, awareness alone does not establish bias. The relevant concern is whether deviations from the planned strategy occurred because of the trial context, were unbalanced between groups, and affected the outcome. The authors state: “*During the study, none of the teaching practices changed in either of the degree courses.*” The reported differences in intensity, tutor competence, standardization, and reflective activities were defining components of the assigned strategies, rather than unintended departures from them.
   
   All Phase 2 randomized students appear to have been analysed in their allocated groups: Trento intensive tutoring, \(n=41\), versus routine tutoring, \(n=41\); Udine weekly tutorials, \(n=39\), versus routine tutoring, \(n=23\). There is no indication of crossovers, post-randomization exclusions, or as-treated/per-protocol analyses. Therefore, the analysis appears consistent with estimating the effect of assignment.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
   
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
   
   b. **Rationale:** The participant flow indicates that all 144 eligible students contributed to the study: 82 in Trento and 62 in Udine. The Phase 2 group sizes reported in Table 3 total the full sample: 41 + 41 + 23 + 39 = 144. Outcome data on wrong diagnostic hypotheses are reported for every randomized group.
   
   The article describes the included group as stable during the six-month period: “*The group included was stable in the first six months*.” There is no reported loss to follow-up, missing outcome assessment, or differential exclusion from the main diagnostic-hypothesis outcome. Consequently, missing data could not plausibly have materially altered the reported Phase 2 comparisons.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**
   
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.
   
   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was assessed using the same standardized simulated case framework across groups and phases. The authors used “*the same ‘case history’ (‘Valentina’s case’)*,” with demographic/contextual details modified across phases to retain comparable complexity. Students had the same 20-minute assessment period.
   
   Importantly, outcome scoring was blinded: “*Two researchers did blind evaluation of the problems individualised by the students and these were compared to the ‘standard’.*” The standard was independently developed by 15 professional nurses, with agreement assessed using Cohen’s kappa and a specified threshold. Therefore, the persons deciding whether hypotheses were correct or mistaken were unaware of the faculty/group identities.
   
   Although students knew the educational strategy they had received, the outcome is a performance test scored against a pre-defined reference standard, not a self-rated outcome. Knowledge of allocation is part of the mechanism through which an educational intervention may affect diagnostic reasoning, rather than clear evidence of differential outcome ascertainment. No evidence suggests that scoring methods, thresholds, or timing differed by intervention group.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**
   
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.
   
   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies the main outcome in advance in its methods: “*The main outcome was the calculation of the number of mistakes made in diagnosing problems (diagnostic hypotheses) identified by the students in Phases 1 and 2.*” It reports outcome data for both phases and all Phase 2 groups in Table 3. This is reassuring.
   
   Nevertheless, the available materials do not provide a sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan for this specific result. The published analysis includes several possible analytic choices: continuous numbers of hypotheses/errors, dichotomization using phase-specific median cut-offs (three errors in Phase 1 and two errors in Phase 2), univariate odds ratios, and multivariable logistic-regression estimates. The authors state that the medians “*were regarded as reference ‘cut-off’ points*,” but it is not clear that these cut-offs and the choice of adjusted analyses were prespecified before outcome data were available.
   
   The trial-registration information cited in the request does not, in the material available here, establish a dated pre-specified analysis plan that fixes the precise outcome metric, cut-offs, time points, and analyses. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was favourable, but the potential for selection among multiple eligible measurements/analyses cannot be excluded. Therefore, **some concerns** is appropriate.
