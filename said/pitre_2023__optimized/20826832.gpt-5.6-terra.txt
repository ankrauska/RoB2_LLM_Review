**Outcome assessed:** compliance with mammogram screening at 6 months, principally ascertained from electronic radiology/clinical records.  
**Effect of interest:** effect of assignment to intervention at baseline (intention-to-treat effect).

1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants were individually randomized after baseline assessment using a centrally implemented computerized procedure. The report states that “a stratified randomization method blocking by age (50–59; 60–70) was used to allocate women to each group using a computerized random generator program.” The baseline database was sent to the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center “to proceed with a random allocation,” which provides reasonable assurance that allocation was centrally administered and concealed from local recruiters until allocation.  
   
   The three groups were nearly equal in size (167, 166, and 167), and the investigators report that “no significant differences were observed between groups in the demographic characteristics of the participants.” There is no indication of a baseline imbalance suggesting a failure of randomization.  
   
   Relevant RoB 2 considerations: sequence random—**Yes**; allocation concealment—**Probably yes**; baseline differences suggesting a problem—**No**.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the interventions.  
   b. **Rationale:** Participants and intervention personnel were necessarily aware of allocation: “Blinding participants to their intervention group was not possible in this study.” However, lack of blinding alone does not imply bias for the assignment effect. The relevant question is whether deviations from protocol arose because of the trial context, were unbalanced, and were likely to affect mammography screening.

   The reported non-study opportunistic advice was similar across groups: “Brief advice about having a mammogram was delivered by a primary care provider to 15% (73/500) of participants. This intervention had a very similar distribution across groups.” The clinic staff and radiology-unit staff were blinded to allocation group. The mail intervention was delivered to all women assigned to the low- and high-intensity groups, and the telephone/home-visit component in the high-intensity group was delivered according to the pre-specified intervention algorithm to women who had not obtained mammography. Thus, the reported differential contacts were intended components of the assigned interventions rather than problematic deviations.

   The outcome analysis followed the intention-to-treat principle. The authors state: “An intention to treat analysis was conducted using electronic clinical records,” which “were reviewed for all women and compliance was estimated using the baseline denominators of each group.” Therefore, women were analysed in their randomized groups and the analysis included all randomized women for the electronic-record outcome.

   Relevant RoB 2 considerations: participant/personnel awareness—**Yes**; trial-context deviations likely to affect outcome and unbalanced—**Probably no**; appropriate analysis for assignment effect—**Yes**.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Although 38/500 women (7.6%) did not complete the final self-report survey, the primary assessment considered here is mammography compliance based on electronic clinical/radiology records. These records were reviewed for every randomized participant: “They were reviewed for all women and compliance was estimated using the baseline denominators of each group.” Therefore, outcome data from electronic records appear available for all 500 randomized women.

   The losses to the final survey were also reasonably balanced—6.6% in control, 7.8% in low intensity, and 8.4% in high intensity—but these survey losses do not create missing data for the electronic-record screening outcome. The single death was attributed to cardiovascular disease and did not prevent ascertainment of whether a mammogram had been obtained during follow-up.

   Relevant RoB 2 consideration: outcome data available for all or nearly all randomized participants—**Yes**, for the electronic-record mammography outcome.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**  
   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.  
   b. **Rationale:** Mammography completion was measured using electronic reports from the affiliated radiology unit and the electronic clinical chart. This is an appropriate and relatively objective method for ascertaining whether screening occurred, and is less susceptible to recall or social-desirability bias than self-report. The investigators specify that the outcome was measured using “the electronic database containing the reports of all tests conducted during the study at the radiology unit affiliated with the university clinic” and “the electronic chart where the results of all tests are registered.”

   Outcome ascertainment was also protected from differential assessment by blinding: “Staff at the clinic and at the radiology unit where participants got their mammograms were blinded to the allocation group.” There is no indication that the definitions, data sources, or ascertainment procedures differed across randomized groups. Although access to mammography was deliberately promoted by the intervention, that is the intended causal pathway, not differential outcome measurement.

   Relevant RoB 2 considerations: inappropriate outcome-measurement method—**No**; ascertainment differing between groups—**No**; outcome assessors aware of allocation—**No**.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**  
   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.  
   b. **Rationale:** The article clearly identifies mammography-screening compliance as the main outcome and specifies the six-month assessment time point: “The main outcome of the study was compliance with mammogram screening.” It also reports outcome ascertainment through both self-report and electronic records, and identifies the electronic-record analysis as the intention-to-treat analysis. This reduces concern that the reported electronic-record screening result was selected from an entirely unreported outcome domain.

   However, the supplied materials do not include a dated protocol or statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. The trial-registration information referenced in the request is not available in the materials provided here for direct comparison with the publication. Moreover, mammography compliance was assessed using more than one measurement source (self-report and electronic records), and the report does not provide sufficient pre-specified analytical detail to exclude selection among possible outcome definitions, measurement sources, or analyses. There is no direct evidence that the reported result was selected because it was favourable, but the absence of a verifiable prospective analysis plan prevents a low-risk judgement.

   Relevant RoB 2 considerations: analysis according to a pre-specified plan finalized before unblinded data—**No information**; selection from multiple eligible outcome measurements—**No information**; selection from multiple eligible analyses—**No information**.
