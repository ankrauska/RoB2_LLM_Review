# Risk of Bias Assessment: KCSG-BR07-02 (Maintenance vs Observation in MBC after PG Chemotherapy)
## Outcome: Progression-Free Survival (PFS)
## Effect of Interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns due to lack of information on allocation concealment, despite adequate sequence generation and no evidence of baseline imbalance.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Sequence generation:** The article states "The random permutation method was used for random assignment," with stratification by visceral disease, prior adjuvant taxane, response to induction PG, and HR status. This indicates a random component was used → **Yes** to sequence randomness.
- **Allocation concealment:** The article states "Registration and random assignment were coordinated centrally at the KCSG data center." This suggests central randomization, which is a strong method of concealment. However, no explicit detail is given regarding the mechanism (e.g., whether personnel could predict upcoming allocations, particularly given the use of permuted blocks stratified by multiple factors across 10 centers). Given the plausible use of blocking, and no explicit description of concealment safeguards, I answer **Probably yes** to concealment given the central coordination, but with some residual uncertainty.
- **Baseline imbalance:** Table 1 shows generally similar baseline characteristics (age, PS, menopausal status, HR status, number of metastatic sites, prior therapy) between arms, with p-values >0.05 for nearly all comparisons except "Lung, hematogenous" metastases (31.9% vs 16.5%, P=.029), a single comparison among many that is compatible with chance (this is the only one of ~15 comparisons significant at conventional threshold, consistent with what might be expected). This does not suggest a problem with randomization.
- Because central randomization was used (reducing concern) but specific safeguard details (e.g., whether randomization office staff were blinded until irrevocable enrollment, block sizes) are not reported, and this was a multicenter open-label trial where knowledge of upcoming allocation before randomization could theoretically be relevant — I judge this domain as **Some concerns** rather than "Low," reflecting the incomplete reporting of concealment procedural detail.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, driven primarily by the absence of blinding (open-label trial), and evidence that some participants in the maintenance arm did not continue treatment per protocol, without clear evidence that this arose from trial context bias affecting the outcome in a differential/unbalanced way — but with an intention-to-treat analysis employed.

**b. Rationale:**
- **Blinding of participants/personnel:** The registry states "masking: NONE." This is an open-label trial (unsurprising given one arm is observation/no treatment vs continued chemotherapy) → **Yes** to signalling question 2.1 and 2.2 (participants and personnel were aware of assigned intervention).
- **Deviations arising from trial context:** In the maintenance group, "33 patients whose disease had not progressed stopped further chemotherapy at the physician's discretion" (Table 2), and "Patients who stopped the study drugs at the physician's discretion did not receive further anticancer treatment...until disease progression was documented." This represents a deviation from the intended maintenance-until-progression protocol, potentially influenced by clinician/patient preference for stopping chemotherapy (a trial-context-related deviation, since toxicity/QoL concerns influenced decisions in an unblinded setting). However, since PFS was determined by radiographic assessment/documented progression rather than treatment continuation itself, and events were assessed by investigators (unblinded) using RECIST — there is potential, but not definitive/strong, evidence that this deviation influenced the PFS outcome measurement itself.
- No non-protocol interventions (e.g., crossover) were reported: "PG combination chemotherapy was not given to any patient in the observation group after progression, which means that there was no crossover." This reduces concern about differential contamination.
- **Analysis:** "All randomly assigned patients were included in the efficacy analysis according to the intent-to-treat principle" — an appropriate ITT analysis was used (signalling question 2.6 = Yes).
- Because deviations (early discontinuation in maintenance arm) occurred but did not clearly bias the *assignment* effect estimate (ITT analysis was used, and stopping early would tend to bias towards the null, not away from it, weakening rather than exaggerating the observed benefit), and because assessment relies on unblinded investigator determination of progression (addressed more directly in Domain 4), I judge this domain as **Some concerns**.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk, as PFS data are available for essentially all randomized participants with complete accounting of reasons for censoring/discontinuation.

**b. Rationale:**
- The CONSORT flow diagram (Figure 1B) and Table 2 provide detailed accounting of all 231 randomized patients (116 maintenance, 115 observation), with documented reasons for discontinuation (disease progression, withdrawal, adverse events, physician's discretion, other).
- "Observations were censored at the last clinical contact if patients were lost to follow-up or at the cutoff date for the analysis" — indicating systematic handling of censoring.
- There is no indication of substantial differential loss to follow-up unrelated to documented events; nearly complete outcome ascertainment (via imaging every 6 weeks) is described ("Radiographic studies were performed every 6 weeks").
- No large or differential missingness that would bias the PFS estimate is apparent. All randomized participants appear accounted for in the survival analysis (median follow-up 33 months, with defined events/censoring).

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk, because PFS assessment (a judgment-based, observer-reported outcome) was performed by unblinded investigators in an open-label trial, and knowledge of intervention assignment (particularly continued active chemotherapy vs. no treatment) could plausibly influence timing/threshold for declaring progression.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial was unmasked ("masking: NONE"), and "PFS and the duration of response were assessed by investigators" — not by an independent, blinded central review committee.
- PFS assessment via RECIST involves radiologic and clinical judgment (an "observer-reported outcome involving some judgement"), which is susceptible to influence from knowledge of treatment assignment (signalling question 4.3 = Yes, assessors aware).
- Assessment could plausibly be influenced: e.g., investigators aware that a patient is in the observation arm (no active treatment) might be more vigilant or have a lower threshold to attribute symptoms/findings to progression, or conversely, investigators managing patients on active maintenance therapy might delay declaring progression, hoping continued treatment is working. Given the marked expected direction of benefit hypothesized by investigators (the trial's stated hypothesis was that maintenance would improve PFS), and no blinded central independent radiologic review is mentioned, this represents a substantial likelihood of biased assessment favoring the hypothesis (i.e., favouring the experimental/maintenance arm).
- No mention of an independent/blinded response evaluation committee to adjudicate progression status was found in the article.
- Therefore, per the algorithm: assessors aware (Yes) → could influence outcome (Yes, judgment-based RECIST assessment) → likely influenced (assessed as likely, given absence of blinded central review and directional hypothesis) → **High risk of bias**.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk, as the reported PFS result corresponds to the pre-specified primary outcome and analysis as registered in the trial protocol/registry, with no evidence of selective reporting from multiple outcome measures or analyses.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registry (NCT00561119) lists the primary outcome as "Progression free survival" with a timeframe of "approximately 4 years," matching the outcome and analysis reported in the publication.
- The statistical methods section pre-specifies: "The primary endpoint was PFS after random assignment" with a defined sample size calculation based on an anticipated 20% difference in 6-month PFS rate, matching the reported result ("The 6-month PFS rate after random assignment, which was the primary endpoint of this study, was 59.7%...").
- PFS was defined consistently: "PFS was calculated from the date of random assignment to the documented date of disease progression or the last visit date" — a single, clearly pre-specified definition, without evidence of multiple alternative definitions, timepoints, or scales being tested and selectively reported.
- The analysis method (Kaplan-Meier/log-rank, Cox model with pre-specified stratification covariates: "visceral disease, age, menopausal status, performance status, number of metastatic sites, prior adjuvant taxane therapy, disease response, and HR status") is consistent with a pre-specified plan; subgroup/forest plot analyses are also presented transparently as exploratory ("although this was the result of the subset analyses" — explicitly acknowledged as secondary/exploratory rather than selectively substituted for the primary result).
- No discrepancy is evident between the registered primary outcome and the outcome/analysis reported for PFS.

---

## Overall Risk of Bias for PFS: **High risk of bias**

**Overall rationale:** Per RoB 2 criteria, a "High" risk-of-bias judgement in any single domain drives the overall judgement to "High" risk of bias. The domain "measurement of the outcome" is judged high risk because PFS — a subjective, judgment-based endpoint — was assessed by unblinded investigators in an open-label trial without independent blinded adjudication, in a context where investigators had a directional hypothesis favoring the maintenance arm. This, combined with "Some concerns" in two other domains (randomization process and deviations from intended intervention), substantially lowers confidence in the reported PFS result, supporting an overall **High risk of bias** judgement.
