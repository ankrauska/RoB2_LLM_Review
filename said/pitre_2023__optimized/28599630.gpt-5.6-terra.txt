1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the trial is described as randomized and baseline characteristics were broadly comparable, but the report does not adequately describe either the random sequence-generation method or allocation concealment.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The authors state that women were “*randomly assigned*” and that “*randomization was performed by a researcher not involved in the data collection using the participant identification (subject ID) after stratification for EPDS score and previous history of depression*.” Stratification is reported, but the method used to generate a random sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers or random-number table) is not specified. Thus, the allocation sequence cannot be confirmed as random.  
   - There is also no explicit statement that the allocation sequence was concealed until women had been enrolled and irrevocably assigned. Independence of the researcher from data collection is reassuring but does not itself demonstrate adequate allocation concealment.  
   - Baseline characteristics were generally similar: baseline EPDS score was 10.0 in the fish-oil group and 10.5 in the placebo group; history of depression was 50.0% and 60.7%, respectively. The imbalance in skin colour approached but did not reach conventional statistical significance (non-white: 59.4% versus 82.1%; *P* = 0.055), and does not by itself indicate a problem with randomization.  
   - The group sizes (32 fish oil; 28 placebo) are compatible with chance. Therefore, there is no evidence that randomization was compromised, but insufficient reporting of sequence generation and concealment prevents a low-risk judgement.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to fish-oil versus placebo at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - This was described as a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. “*Participants and all research assistants and technicians responsible for running both the cohort study and the RCT were blinded to the study allocation.*” The fish-oil and placebo capsules were “*identical regarding color, shape, size and packing*” and were deodorized. Thus, participants, carers/research personnel, and those delivering the intervention were unlikely to know allocation.  
   - No evidence is presented of deviations from the assigned intervention that arose because of the trial context and were differential between groups. Reported adherence was similar among women receiving the intervention: 74.4% in the fish-oil group and 73.5% in controls (*P* = 0.903).  
   - The authors state that the ITT population “*included all women randomized, regardless of whether or not they dropped out or fully adhered to the intervention*.” They also report per-protocol analyses, but the ITT results are available and are the appropriate results for the effect of assignment.  
   - Although several randomized participants did not receive or complete supplementation, this does not itself cause bias in the effect of assignment, provided participants remain analysed by randomized group. The possible implications of unavailable outcome measurements are addressed under missing outcome data.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because outcome data at the postpartum endpoint were unavailable for a large proportion of randomized women, losses were somewhat greater in the fish-oil group, and the reasons for missingness could plausibly be related to depressive symptoms or pregnancy/health status.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - Sixty women were randomized, but only 32 completed the trial: 15/32 (46.9%) in the fish-oil group and 17/28 (60.7%) in the placebo group. Thus, outcome data at postpartum were unavailable for approximately 47% of randomized participants overall.  
   - The report describes numerous post-randomization losses. Before receiving intervention, six women in the fish-oil group and four in the placebo group were lost; one fish-oil participant declined RCT participation. After commencement, 7 fish-oil and 5 placebo participants dropped out; one participant in each group was excluded; one placebo participant had a stillbirth; and two fish-oil participants missed the postpartum appointment.  
   - Attrition was greater in the fish-oil group (17/32) than in the placebo group (11/28). This imbalance is not extreme, but is substantial in a very small trial.  
   - Reasons such as missed appointments, withdrawal/dropout, transfer of care, hypertensive complications, miscarriage/stillbirth, and other pregnancy-related events may plausibly be associated with mental health, health status, or the likelihood of reporting depressive symptoms. The statement that losses were “*apparently not related to the study*” does not establish that missingness was unrelated to the true EPDS outcome.  
   - No sensitivity analysis is reported to demonstrate that the prevalence result for EPDS ≥ 11 was robust to plausible assumptions about the missing data. The nominal ITT denominators in Table 3 do not resolve the concern, since outcome data could not have been observed at postpartum for all randomized women.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The outcome was prevalence of depressive symptoms defined using a pre-specified threshold on the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale: “*At the study end-point, for screening of moderate and severe cases of PPD, the cutoff point of ≥11 … was used.*”  
   - The EPDS is an established, validated self-report screening scale. The authors note that it “*has been previously validated for use in pregnancy*” and that the Portuguese version had been validated in Brazilian mothers. The use of EPDS is appropriate for measuring depressive symptoms, though it is not a diagnostic assessment of major depressive disorder.  
   - The same EPDS method and threshold were applied in both treatment groups at the same scheduled postpartum time point (4–6 weeks postpartum). There is no indication that outcome ascertainment differed systematically between groups.  
   - For this participant-reported outcome, participants are the outcome assessors. Participants were blinded to assignment, and placebo capsules were indistinguishable from fish-oil capsules. Therefore, knowledge of intervention received was unlikely to have influenced EPDS reporting.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the trial report identifies EPDS ≥ 11 as the primary outcome and reports the relevant postpartum result, but there was no prospectively registered, sufficiently detailed randomized-trial analysis plan to verify that the reported EPDS ≥ 11 result was selected from neither multiple measurements nor multiple analyses on the basis of the findings.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   - The article explicitly states: “*Prevalence of EPDS ≥11 was the primary outcome*,” and it reports prevalence at the postpartum endpoint as well as during pregnancy. This supports that the outcome was planned and fully reported in the publication.  
   - However, the registered record, NCT01660165, is for an **observational prospective cohort** of 299 women, not the nested randomized fish-oil versus placebo trial of 60 women. The registry identifies “Major depressive episode” as the primary outcome, but does not specify the randomized intervention, EPDS ≥ 11 threshold, postpartum endpoint, analysis population, or statistical analysis plan for the RCT.  
   - The publication states that the trial was “*retrospectively registered on 24 May 2012*.” Consequently, the registration cannot demonstrate that the outcome measurement, time point, threshold, or analysis plan were finalized before unblinded outcome data became available.  
   - Multiple eligible measurements and analyses existed: EPDS was measured at T0, T1, T2, and T3; results were reported as EPDS ≥ 11, continuous EPDS scores, change scores, longitudinal logistic-regression models, linear mixed-effects models, ITT analyses, per-protocol analyses, adjusted analyses, and subgroup analyses.  
   - There is no direct evidence that the postpartum prevalence result was selected because it was favourable or statistically significant. Therefore, “some concerns,” rather than high risk of bias, is appropriate.
