1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study is described as a randomized controlled trial and reports that participants were “**randomly selected (stratified by gender and age: 14–15, 16–18 years), and assigned to 1 of the 3 study conditions**.” Stratification by age and gender is compatible with an appropriate randomization process, and the reported baseline prevalence of severe peer aggression was broadly similar across arms (therapist 82.7%, computer 75.5%, control 77.9%); there is no clear evidence of a baseline imbalance indicating failure of randomization.

   However, neither the article nor the registry describes how the random allocation sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated random numbers or random permuted blocks) or, importantly, how allocation was concealed before assignment. There is no indication of central randomization, an independent allocation system, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. Under RoB 2, lack of information about allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**, in the absence of evidence that baseline imbalance suggests a more serious problem.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants receiving a therapist-assisted computer intervention, computer intervention, or a control brochure could not realistically have been blinded to their allocation. The interventions were conspicuously different: the control group “**received a trifold brochure with community resources**,” whereas the therapist intervention had a median duration of 37 minutes and the computer intervention a median duration of 29 minutes. Therapists and other personnel delivering the intervention would also necessarily have known the assigned condition.

   The report indicates good implementation and an analysis consistent with assignment: “**An intent-to-treat approach was used. All randomly assigned participants were included whether the intervention was received or not (.95% received their assigned intervention during the ED visit).**” The generalized estimating equation analyses used available observations, which is acceptable for this RoB 2 domain; implications of unavailable 12-month observations are considered separately under missing outcome data.

   There is no reported evidence that knowledge of allocation caused unbalanced, non-protocol co-interventions or other deviations arising specifically from the trial context that affected peer aggression. Nonetheless, because participants and intervention personnel were unblinded and the report provides insufficient information to exclude trial-context deviations, this domain raises **some concerns** rather than being at low risk.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Outcome data at 12 months were not available for nearly all randomized participants. Of 726 participants completing baseline assessment and randomized, only 607 completed the 12-month follow-up: “**the 12-month follow-up rate was 83.6% (n = 607/726)**.” Thus, approximately 16% of randomized participants lacked 12-month data. For severe peer aggression specifically, the Table 1 denominators imply data from approximately 201 therapist-arm participants, 199 computer-arm participants, and 200 control participants, compared with baseline/randomized group sizes of approximately 254, 237, and 235, respectively.

   Attrition also appears somewhat differential: approximately 21% in the therapist arm, 16% in the computer arm, and 15% in the control arm had no 12-month follow-up. No sensitivity analysis, inverse-probability weighting, multiple imputation with a justified missing-data model, or other analysis demonstrating robustness to plausible missing outcomes was reported.

   Missingness could plausibly depend on the unobserved value of self-reported peer aggression: adolescents with continued aggression, instability, disengagement, or other adverse circumstances may be less likely to complete a 12-month survey. The authors themselves acknowledge that “**although attrition was low (14%) and an intent to treat analysis was conducted, it is always possible that those lost to follow-up may have biased results**.” Given the appreciable missing data, somewhat differing follow-up between groups, and plausible relation between follow-up and aggression, missingness is likely to depend on the true outcome. This supports a **high risk of bias** judgement.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Peer aggression was measured through participant self-report using items from the conflict tactics scale. The article states: “**Items from the conflict tactic scale assessed past-year severe aggression toward peers**,” and severe peer aggression was formed as a binary outcome based on self-reported acts such as “**hit or punched, serious physical fighting, used a knife/gun**.”

   Although use of a computerized, self-administered follow-up survey and confidentiality protections may reduce reporting bias, this remains a participant-reported, subjective behavioural outcome. For participant-reported outcomes, the participant is the outcome assessor. Participants were necessarily aware of whether they had received a therapist-delivered motivational intervention, a computerized intervention, or only a brochure. Such knowledge could affect willingness to disclose aggression, especially where the intervention explicitly sought to reduce violence and promote behaviour change.

   The registry reports single masking of the “outcomes assessor,” and the article notes that self-report validity may be enhanced when “**staff are blind to condition assignment**.” However, blinding staff does not blind the participant, who is the relevant assessor for this outcome. Given the sensitive and socially desirable nature of reporting severe aggression, and the clear potential for intervention recipients to report less aggression because of awareness of assignment, it is likely that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received. Therefore, measurement of peer aggression at 12 months is at **high risk of bias**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was registered before recruitment (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00251212; first posted in 2005; recruitment began in September 2006). The registry prespecified that primary outcomes would include “**alcohol use/misuse [and] violent behaviors**,” assessed at “**3, 6 and 12-months**.” The publication reports the 12-month peer-aggression outcome and presents the planned comparison with control in Table 2. It also reports other violence outcomes—peer victimization and violence consequences—rather than reporting only the favourable peer-aggression result. This reduces concern that the outcome domain itself was selectively reported.

   However, the registration record does not specify the exact peer-aggression measure, the dichotomous definition of severe aggression, the preferred analysis metric, covariate adjustment, or a finalized statistical analysis plan. Therefore, it cannot establish that the reported 12-month GEE analysis was specified before unblinded outcome data were available.

   Further, the article states: “**At 3 and 6 months, the effects were noted in dichotomous treatment of the variables and not the continuous variables. The analyses focused on evaluating if effects noted at 6 months were sustained at 12 months; therefore, only dichotomous outcomes were examined.**” This indicates that the choice to focus on dichotomous analyses at 12 months was informed by results at earlier follow-up assessments. It does not conclusively demonstrate that the reported 12-month peer-aggression result was selected from multiple analyses because it was favourable, but it leaves a material possibility of result-based analytic selection. In the absence of a detailed, time-stamped analysis plan, this domain has **some concerns**.
