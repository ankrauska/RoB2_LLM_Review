1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because the article states that participants were randomly assigned, but does not describe the method used to generate the allocation sequence or how allocation was concealed. Baseline comparability by group is also not reported.

   b. **Rationale:** The study reports that “**28 first- and second-year medical students… then practiced**” and that, after the pre-test, “**they were randomly assigned to three experimental groups**.” However, it gives no information on whether the sequence was computer-generated or otherwise random, and no information on allocation concealment (e.g., central allocation or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes). No baseline table or group-specific baseline checklist scores is presented to assess whether baseline differences suggest a problem with randomization. Under RoB 2, lack of information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns when there is no evidence of important baseline imbalance.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the practice schedules at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and personnel were necessarily aware of the assigned practice schedule: whole/functional-order practice, random practice, or blocked practice. However, for the assignment effect, lack of blinding alone is not sufficient for bias. The relevant question is whether deviations from the intended practice schedule arose because of the trial context, affected the checklist outcome, and were unbalanced between groups.

   The interventions were clearly described and apparently implemented as planned. For example, the functional-order group practiced the entire task in three 20-minute sessions; the random-practice group practiced the five skills in random order; and the blocked-practice group completed three consecutive trials of each skill. The report further states that “**all participants in all groups practice[d] each skill 3 times for 2 minutes and 10 seconds**,” supporting comparable practice exposure. Feedback was also delivered systematically: “**an orthopedic faculty member circulated within the group systematically to provide feedback to the trainees**,” while no feedback was provided during testing.

   There is no reported evidence of non-protocol co-interventions, differential implementation failures, crossover between schedules, or exclusions from the analysis based on the intervention received. The analysis compared participants according to their randomized groups, using mixed ANOVA models with practice group as the between-participant factor. Thus, the reported analysis is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment, subject to the separate consideration of missing data.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The report identifies 28 participants who consented and describes outcome assessments at pre-test, immediate post-test, and delayed post-test. It reports the planned analyses of change scores and does not describe withdrawals, missing checklist assessments, or exclusion of participants from the analysis. There is no indication that outcome data were unavailable for an important proportion of randomized participants.

   Although a participant flow diagram and explicit group-specific denominators are not provided, the available report is consistent with all, or nearly all, enrolled participants contributing outcome data. There is no evidence that missingness occurred, differed between groups, or was related to the true operation-specific checklist score.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was measured with an operation-specific checklist developed by orthopedic surgeons: “**a panel of 3 orthopedic surgeons identified the actions necessary to perform the operative task effectively and thus selected these items for the checklist**.” This is a plausible and relevant measurement method for operative-task performance, and there is no indication that different checklist methods or thresholds were used across groups.

   However, outcome assessment was performed by “**two experienced orthopedic surgeons**,” and the report does not state that these assessors were blinded to participants’ assigned practice schedules. Checklist scoring of operative performance involves at least some observer judgement. Because the intervention concerns educational practice schedules and the study hypothesis favoured particular schedules, knowledge of group assignment could plausibly influence assessors’ ratings. No procedures are reported to prevent this, such as video-recording and blinded scoring, masking of participant identity/group, independent adjudication, or assessment of inter-rater reliability.

   Therefore, outcome assessors were likely aware of intervention status, and assessment of a performance checklist could have been influenced by that knowledge. This creates a high risk of bias in measurement of the operation-specific checklist outcome.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report does not provide a protocol, statistical analysis plan, or sufficiently detailed prospectively registered analysis plan demonstrating that the analysis of change in operation-specific checklist score was finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The supplied article predates widespread prospective trial-registration requirements, and no registration information or pre-specified analysis plan is available in the material provided.

   There were several potentially eligible outcome measurements and analyses: checklist scores, final-product analysis, global ratings, drilling times, immediate and delayed post-tests, and change scores calculated from pre-test to each post-test. For the checklist outcome specifically, the investigators analysed difference scores and incorporated both post-test phases in a mixed ANOVA. This approach may be reasonable, but without a pre-specified plan it cannot be established that the reported outcome metric, time-point handling, and analysis were selected independently of the results.

   There is no direct evidence that the checklist result was selectively chosen from multiple analyses or measurements because it was favourable; indeed, the article reports results for the other outcome measures. Nevertheless, absence of a pre-specified analysis plan and the availability of multiple measurement times and analytic choices result in some concerns under RoB 2.
