**Assessment context:** This assessment concerns the reported interhemispheric functional-connectivity result (EEG coherence), principally the comparison of random-frequency (1–5 Hz) tPCS with sham. The effect of interest is the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns arise because allocation concealment was not reported, and the description of blocked randomization does not explicitly confirm a random sequence within blocks.

   b. **Rationale:** The authors state: “*Volunteers completed a single-session experiment and were randomized into one of four study arms*” and “*Randomization was completed using blocks of four.*” Blocked allocation alone does not establish that the order of assignments within each block was random. There is also no description of who generated the sequence, whether allocation was centrally administered, or whether sealed opaque envelopes or another concealment method was used. Thus, both sequence generation and concealment are insufficiently described.

   Baseline characteristics do not indicate an evident failure of randomization. The four groups were of broadly similar size (9, 11, 9, and 9 participants), and the authors report that “*There were no significant differences in demographic characteristics between groups*.” However, absence of reported baseline imbalance does not resolve the lack of information about allocation concealment. Under RoB 2, no information about concealment, with no apparent problematic imbalance, leads to **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, primarily because blinding of all personnel delivering stimulation/care was not fully described and there is no direct information on whether any trial-context deviations occurred. The analysis itself was broadly appropriate for the effect of assignment.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and the outcome-assessing investigator were blinded: “*Both the investigator assessing the outcomes and the subjects were blinded to the intervention.*” The sham procedure was designed to support participant blinding: “*For the sham condition, stimulation was applied for 30 s and then turned off automatically.*” Moreover, the reported blinding check found that “*Subjects did not guess correctly the stimulation group to which they were assigned beyond chance (p = 0.679).*”

   However, the report does not clearly state whether the personnel setting up or delivering stimulation were blinded. It also does not report whether there were deviations from the protocol attributable to the trial context, such as differential co-interventions or differential stimulation implementation. This leaves uncertainty for this RoB 2 domain.

   For the assignment effect, the analysis retained participants in their randomized groups. One participant in the random-frequency group was excluded because of “*excessive artifacts in the EEG recording*,” which represents missing outcome data rather than switching groups or a per-protocol/as-treated analysis. The mixed-model comparison of randomized groups is therefore broadly appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment; the artifact-related exclusion is considered under missing outcome data.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Of 40 recruited individuals, 38 underwent/completed the stimulation session and were randomized across the four groups. The two individuals who did not participate “*decided not to participate before stimulation was given*,” and therefore do not appear to have contributed post-randomization missing outcome data.

   For the EEG coherence outcome, only one randomized participant was omitted: “*One subject in the Random Frequency group was removed from the analysis due to excessive artifacts in the EEG recording.*” Thus, coherence data were available for 37/38 randomized participants (97.4%). This is nearly complete outcome data for a continuous outcome, and the loss is small enough that it is unlikely, by itself, to have materially changed the effect estimate. The reason—excessive EEG artifact—is technical and does not inherently suggest dependence on the true coherence outcome. No differential loss was reported in the sham group or other active-treatment groups.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Interhemispheric functional connectivity was measured using a standardized, instrument-based EEG procedure. The authors used a “*64-channel Electrical Geodesic Incorporated (EGI) EEG device*” and calculated “*magnitude square coherence as a pair-wise connectivity measure*.” The same EEG procedures were performed before and after the experimental condition for all groups, including both eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions.

   The outcome is based on quantitative signal processing rather than a participant-reported or clinician-judgement outcome. Although manual artifact detection/rejection was part of preprocessing, the report states that “*the investigator assessing the outcomes*” was blinded to intervention. There is no indication that EEG acquisition, coherence definitions, timing, or analytic thresholds differed between intervention groups. Therefore, differential outcome measurement bias is unlikely.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The publication describes the coherence analyses as exploratory: “*We conducted an exploratory analysis to detect changes in mean power for theta, alpha and beta, and interhemispheric coherence for alpha and theta and four different sub-bands.*” Numerous possible results were available for selection, including multiple frequency bands and sub-bands, two electrode-pair configurations, eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions, group effects, interaction effects, and several post-hoc pairwise comparisons.

   The article states that some post-hoc comparisons were specified a priori—“*According to a-priori specifications, we used t-test for post-hoc comparison*”—but it does not provide a dated statistical analysis plan demonstrating that the specific coherence outcome, frequency band/sub-band, time-point/condition, and analysis reported were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available.

   The trial-registration record referenced in the request was not supplied in the material available here; therefore, it cannot be used to verify whether the reported interhemispheric-connectivity analyses, outcome measurements, and analyses were prospectively specified. There is no direct evidence that the reported theta-coherence result was selected because of its statistical significance, but the combination of exploratory analyses, multiplicity, and lack of an available pre-specified analysis plan supports **some concerns** rather than low risk of bias.
