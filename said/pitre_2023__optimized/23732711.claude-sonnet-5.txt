# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Change in Photoaging

---

### 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The allocation sequence was generated randomly by computer: "one of our study investigators, who had no knowledge of the participants, randomly assigned them by using a computer-generated randomized list to daily application of sunscreen... or discretionary sunscreen use... and to 30 mg of β-carotene or placebo supplements daily." This satisfies the criterion for random sequence generation.

Regarding allocation concealment, the description of an independent investigator with no knowledge of participants generating and implementing a computer-generated list suggests reasonable concealment, though explicit details of the concealment mechanism (e.g., sealed envelopes, central randomization) are not fully described (probably yes/yes).

Baseline characteristics (Table 1) were well balanced between both the sunscreen groups and the β-carotene groups across sex, age, country of birth, education, skin color, skin reaction to sun, occupation, sunburns, nevi, history of skin cancer, clinical photoaging of neck, BMI, and smoking status. The only notable imbalance was in group sizes for the β-carotene allocation crossed with sunscreen allocation (P = 0.06 for slightly more randomized to β-carotene than placebo), which is compatible with chance and explicitly noted by the authors as non-significant at conventional thresholds. No baseline imbalances suggest a problem with the randomization process.

---

### 2. Deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** This was an open-label trial; blinding of participants and personnel to sunscreen allocation was not feasible ("placebo sunscreen was considered unethical"), and daily sunscreen use is inherently apparent to participants. Therefore, participants and those delivering the sunscreen intervention were aware of intervention assignment (Y to signalling question 2.1/2.2 for sunscreen).

However, there is no strong evidence that deviations arose *because of the trial context* in a manner that biased the outcome. The authors specifically checked for and reported that "reported sun exposure was similar between the daily and discretionary sunscreen groups during the trial" and that "use of sun-protection measures other than sunscreen was also similar" between groups. This suggests behavioral co-interventions were balanced. Adherence differed as expected (77% vs 33% applying at least 3-4 days/week), but this reflects the nature of the intervention allocation itself rather than trial-context-driven contamination.

For β-carotene, the trial used a placebo, allowing blinding of participants and assessors to this arm, reducing concern for this component; adherence was similar between β-carotene (68%) and placebo (67%) groups, and photometric verification confirmed differential dermal carotene levels consistent with allocation, indicating the blinding/randomization for this arm functioned appropriately.

Analysis was based on intention-to-treat principles ("data were analyzed according to treatment as randomly allocated"), which is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention.

The overall judgement of "Some concerns" (rather than "Low") is driven primarily by the inherent lack of blinding of participants/personnel for the sunscreen comparison and the theoretical possibility that trial engagement/awareness of allocation could have influenced outdoor behavior, even though the reported balance in sun-exposure behavior mitigates this concern substantially.

---

### 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** Outcome data (microtopography grade) were not available for all participants: "886 participants (98% of 903)... had at least 1 good-quality skin replica. Of these, 604 contributed replicas in 1992 and 1996, 213 in 1992 only, and 69 in 1996 only." Thus, roughly one-third of participants (282/886) had only one time point, and complete paired data were available for only 604/886 (~68%) of those with any replica, and 604/903 (~67%) of all eligible participants.

The authors provide substantial reassurance that this missingness was unlikely to bias the intervention effect estimates: "Compared with those with 2 replicas, participants who contributed only 1 (n=282) were more likely to have severe photoaging of the neck and 2 to 5 total sunburns. Having a missing microtopography grade in 1996 was not associated with baseline microtopography grade," and, critically, "treatment allocation was not associated with missing grades." The authors performed sensitivity analyses: complete-case analysis (n=604) and multiple imputation, both of which "replicated the results presented in Table 4."

Because missingness was unrelated to treatment allocation and the trialists demonstrated through sensitivity analyses (complete-case and multiple imputation) that the results were robust to the missing data, there is evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data. However, the substantial proportion of missing data (32-33%) and reliance on assumptions in the sensitivity analyses (particularly multiple imputation, which cannot fully rule out MNAR mechanisms related to unmeasured factors) leave some residual concern, warranting "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The method of outcome measurement (Beagley and Gibson microtopography grading of silicone skin replicas) is an established, validated technique: "We have previously shown that severity of dermal elastosis is predicted by standard grading of the microtopography of the skin surface, which provides a valid measure of skin photoaging." This is not an inappropriate measurement method.

Measurement procedures were identical across groups (same replica collection method, same grading scale), so ascertainment did not differ between intervention groups.

Critically, outcome assessors were blinded to treatment allocation: "Experienced assessors who were unaware of treatment allocation graded replicas." Additionally, the reliability of assessors was demonstrated to be high: "Intra- and intergrader repeatability of assessors was high, with weighted κ statistics of 0.81 and 0.86, respectively." The authors explicitly note: "measurement error... seemed to be nondifferential with respect to treatment groups, particularly for microtopography, because assessors were blinded to allocations."

Because outcome assessors were blinded and the outcome (semi-objective grading based on physical skin replicas) is not a participant-reported outcome, knowledge of intervention assignment by participants themselves is unlikely to have influenced the objectively-graded silicone replica assessment.

---

### 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The trial was prospectively registered (Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry: ACTRN12610000086066), and the design and methods were pre-specified and published in detail prior to this results paper, referencing the original trial design paper (Green et al., Control Clin Trials 1994) and prior related outcome papers (skin cancer, actinic keratoses). The photoaging outcome (microtopography grading using the pre-validated Beagley and Gibson scale) was a pre-specified, single measurement method with no evidence of selection among multiple competing outcome measurements or scales.

The statistical analysis is fully pre-specified and clearly described (ordinal logistic regression via GEE, comparing change in grade from baseline to follow-up), and the paper reports the primary analysis (Model 1 and Model 2, both adjusted and unadjusted) transparently, along with sensitivity analyses (complete case, multiple imputation) and subgroup analyses—all of which are reported regardless of statistical significance (e.g., the null result for β-carotene overall is reported prominently, not suppressed). There is no indication that a result was selected from multiple candidate analyses on the basis of significance; rather, all planned analyses (main effects, adjusted models, subgroup analyses by baseline severity) are reported with full transparency, including non-significant and unexpected findings (e.g., borderline harmful signal for β-carotene in the more severely aged subgroup).
