**Outcome assessed:** Surgical time (minutes), comparing percutaneous microwave ablation (group A) with laparoscopic resection (group B): 96.7 ± 27.8 versus 134.2 ± 34.3 minutes, respectively (Table 1). The assessment addresses the **effect of assignment to intervention at baseline**.

1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that participants were allocated using a random-number table: “*Using the random number table method, 90 cases were divided into group A and group B (n=45).*” This supports a random allocation sequence.

   However, there is no information on allocation concealment—e.g., central randomization, an independent allocator, or sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. It is therefore unclear whether those enrolling participants could have foreseen upcoming allocations.

   The reported baseline characteristics are broadly similar for sex, age, and tumour size. However, the numbers reported for single and multiple nodules do not sum to the randomized total in either group (group A: 20 + 15 = 35; group B: 21 + 14 = 35), which limits confidence in the completeness and reliability of the baseline reporting. This discrepancy alone does not demonstrate a material baseline imbalance or a failed randomization process, but the absence of allocation-concealment information leads to **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Blinding of participants, surgeons, and other personnel was not feasible because the assigned interventions were materially different procedures: CT-guided percutaneous microwave ablation versus laparoscopic resection. Thus, participants and intervention providers were almost certainly aware of the allocated intervention.

   Nevertheless, for the effect of assignment to intervention, lack of blinding only creates bias in this domain if it leads to deviations from the protocol that arose because of the trial context, were unbalanced between groups, and could affect surgical time. The report describes delivery of the assigned procedures in each group—“*Cases in group A were treated by modified percutaneous microwave ablation*” and “*Laparoscopic resection was performed for group B*”—and provides no evidence of crossovers, treatment substitutions, or trial-context-related co-interventions that would affect surgical time.

   The surgical-time analysis includes all 45 randomized participants in each group (Table 1). Although the article does not explicitly label the analysis as intention-to-treat, there is no indication that eligible randomized participants were excluded or analysed according to a different procedure. Therefore, there is no evidence of a deviation or analytical approach likely to bias the effect of assignment on surgical time.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Surgical time is reported for all randomized participants: Table 1 includes 45 participants in group A and 45 in group B, corresponding to the full randomized sample of 90 participants. Surgical time is an intraoperative outcome and would ordinarily be available for participants who underwent their assigned procedure.

   There is no reported loss to follow-up, exclusion, or missing surgical-time measurement. Thus, outcome data appear available for all participants randomized, and missing data could not plausibly have materially affected the comparison.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Surgical time is an objective intraoperative outcome, generally measured from operative records or timekeeping and requiring little or no subjective judgement. Although blinding of surgeons and operative personnel was not possible, knowledge of the intervention is unlikely to alter the recorded duration of the procedure in a way that constitutes differential outcome-assessment bias.

   The study reports that “*Surgical time … [was] recorded for the two groups*.” There is no indication that different measurement methods, definitions, timekeeping procedures, or thresholds were used between the groups. While the exact start and end points for operative time were not defined, the outcome itself is appropriate and objective, and there is no evidence that measurement or ascertainment differed between intervention groups.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article reports surgical time in Table 1 and identifies it in the methods as one of the planned “*criteria for efficacy assessment*”: “*Surgical time, intraoperative blood loss and incidence of complications were recorded for the two groups.*”

   However, no dated protocol or statistical analysis plan finalized before access to unblinded outcome data is available in the supplied material. Although a trial-registration document was referenced in the request, its contents were not provided here; therefore, it cannot be used to verify prospective specification of the surgical-time outcome, the relevant time point/definition, or the planned analysis. There is also no information establishing that the reported two-sample t-test analysis was selected from no other eligible analyses (e.g., alternative definitions of operative time, transformations, adjusted analyses, or handling of potential exclusions).

   There is no direct evidence that the reported surgical-time result was selected because of its statistical significance or direction. Nevertheless, without a verifiable pre-specified analysis plan, RoB 2 indicates **some concerns** for selection of the reported result.
