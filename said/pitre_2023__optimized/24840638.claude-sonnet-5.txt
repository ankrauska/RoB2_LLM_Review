# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Smoking abstinence by breath CO sample < 3 ppm
## Effect of Interest: Effect of assignment to intervention

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** 
- Sequence generation had a random component: "Random assignment to one of the three groups was accomplished by randomly assigning 2 participants to the percentile group, 2 to the fixed criterion group and 1 to the random incentives group from each group of 5 participants completing intake" and "Randomization was stratified by intent to use medication to help them stop smoking, and by order of study entry." This supports "Yes" to sequence being random.
- No information is provided about allocation concealment. There is no description of how the randomization sequence was concealed from those enrolling participants (e.g., no mention of central randomization, sealed envelopes, etc.). This warrants "No information" on allocation concealment.
- There is evidence of baseline imbalance: "A higher proportion of percentile incentive participants achieved this level of education [Vocational/Technical School/Community College], relative to fixed criterion participants (χ2 = 8.46, p < 0.01)." This is a single significant difference among many baseline characteristics tested (see Table 1), which is compatible with a modest excess given multiple comparisons, but it is noted by the authors themselves as a discrepancy. Because the randomization process itself was described as random and the block/stratified procedure was appropriate, and only one baseline variable (of many compared) was significantly different, this is judged as compatible with chance in the context of multiple testing, though it contributes to some uncertainty.
- Given random sequence generation was adequately described but no information on allocation concealment, combined with a baseline imbalance that raises some (but not high) concern, this domain is judged as **"Some concerns"** rather than "Low risk."

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- Participants and study personnel could not be blinded to their intervention group assignment, as the intervention itself (type of incentive schedule) was necessarily known to participants: "Percentile incentive participants were told that the value of the breath CO changed to help them smoke less. Fixed criterion incentive participants were told that they could only earn incentives for not smoking. Random incentive participants were told that they would receive incentives only for attendance." This confirms participants were aware of their assigned intervention (answer "Yes" to signalling question 2.1).
- Personnel delivering the intervention (those administering incentives and calculating percentile criteria) were also aware of group assignment by necessity, as they had to apply different contingency rules (answer "Yes" to 2.2).
- However, there is no clear evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose *because of the trial context* (i.e., non-protocol interventions or trial-specific recruitment/engagement effects) that would differentially bias the assignment effect. The primary "deviations" described (e.g., participants adjusting their smoking behavior in response to incentive schedules) are the intended behavioral responses to the interventions themselves, not deviations from protocol. There is no mention of contamination, co-interventions, or non-protocol interventions differentially affecting groups.
- The analysis used appears to reflect all randomized participants who were classified as HTT and remained in their assigned group; there is no indication of switching between arms or exclusion of eligible participants from the assigned-group analysis. The event records (Figure 1) appear to include all participants' visit patterns by assigned group.
- Because participants and personnel were unblinded (necessarily, given the nature of the intervention), and this is a common and often unavoidable feature of behavioral CM trials, but there is no specific evidence that trial-context-driven deviations (rather than the intervention's mechanism of action itself) biased the outcome, this domain is judged as **"Some concerns"** based on the lack of blinding combined with no information confirming absence of trial-context deviations affecting the outcome.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:**
- Outcome data (breath CO samples) were not available for all randomized participants across all 60 visits. Attendance was incomplete: "The average number of delivered breath CO samples per participant (out of 60) was 42 (SD = 22), 40 (24), and 52 (19) for the percentile, fixed criterion and random incentive groups, respectively." This indicates substantial missing data (representing roughly 30-33% missing visits for percentile and fixed criterion groups on average).
- Figure 1 event records show "White areas represent missed visits," confirming missingness was common and visually substantial across all three groups.
- The number of delivered breath CO samples differed significantly between groups: "The number of delivered breath CO samples was different between groups (H = 8.57, df (2), p < 0.02)," though there was no significant difference specifically between percentile and fixed criterion groups (z = −0.10, p = 0.92).
- There is no clear evidence/sensitivity analysis presented that confirms the primary result (time to first abstinence, ability to maintain abstinence) is robust to the missing data, nor is there strong reason to rule out that missingness in breath CO status depends on smoking status itself (e.g., participants who resumed smoking heavily may be more likely to drop out or miss visits, a plausible mechanism in smoking cessation trials).
- Because missingness could plausibly depend on the true (unmeasured) smoking/abstinence status, and there is a difference in overall attendance between groups (notably higher in the random group), but no analytic correction (e.g., imputation, sensitivity analysis) was reported, this leads to a judgment of **"Some concerns"** rather than high risk, given the group-level comparison of primary interest (percentile vs. fixed criterion) showed no significant attendance difference.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- The outcome (breath CO < 3 ppm) is an objective biochemical measure, not a judgment-based or participant-reported outcome: "Vitalograph CO monitors (Vitalograph Inc. Lenexa, KS) were used to take breath CO samples... The peak breath CO reading was taken as the participant's breath CO level." This is an observer-reported outcome not involving judgement (i.e., an automated/objective test).
- The same measurement method and threshold (< 3 ppm) was applied identically across all three groups and throughout the study: "We used an abstinence breath CO criterion of < 3 ppm based on our previous smoking cessation studies." There is no indication that measurement or ascertainment of the outcome differed between intervention groups.
- Because this is an objective test using standardized equipment and criteria applied identically to all groups, awareness of intervention assignment by participants or staff administering the CO test is very unlikely to influence the actual measured CO reading (unlike a subjective/judgment-based outcome). Therefore, even though blinding of participants/staff was not possible, the outcome measurement itself is not susceptible to bias from this awareness.
- This supports a judgement of **"Low risk of bias"** for this domain.

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registration (referenced doc_id: 4dab5cab-2b44-42f8-8559-2dd9073b18b5) and the publication align in terms of the primary outcome of breath CO abstinence (< 3 ppm), which is consistently defined and used as the primary metric of interest throughout the Methods, Results, and Discussion sections of the article.
- The outcome measurement (breath CO < 3 ppm) is reported using a single, pre-specified definition and threshold that is consistent throughout the paper: "We used an abstinence breath CO criterion of < 3 ppm based on our previous smoking cessation studies (Javors, Hatch & Lamb, 2005)." There is no indication of multiple competing thresholds or definitions from which the authors selectively chose favorable results.
- Multiple related outcome analyses are reported comprehensively and transparently (e.g., proportion achieving at least one abstinent sample, time to first abstinent sample, total number of abstinent samples, ability to maintain consecutive abstinence, earnings), with both statistically significant and non-significant results reported (e.g., "the total number of criterion breath CO samples submitted was not statistically different between the three groups (H = 5.89, df (2), p = 0.053)" and "Fixed criterion incentive participants generally earned more... although this difference was not statistically significant (z = −1.92, p = 0.055)"). This transparency in reporting both significant and non-significant/borderline findings suggests low risk of outcome-selective reporting.
- There is no evidence of switching primary outcome measures, changing analysis methods post-hoc based on results, or omission of pre-specified analyses related to the < 3 ppm abstinence outcome.
- Based on consistency between the registered outcome and the reported outcome, and comprehensive reporting of all relevant analyses (favorable and unfavorable), this domain is judged as **"Low risk of bias."**

---

# Overall Risk of Bias Judgement: **Some concerns**

**Rationale:** The trial is judged to raise some concerns in multiple domains (randomization process, deviations from intended intervention, and missing outcome data) but is not judged to be at high risk of bias in any single domain. Per RoB 2.0 guidance (Table 1), "Some concerns in multiple domains may lead the review authors to decide on an overall judgement of 'High' risk of bias." However, given that the concerns in each domain are modest (a single baseline imbalance amid multiple comparisons, an inherent and commonly unavoidable lack of blinding for behavioral interventions without clear evidence of trial-context-driven bias, and comparable attendance/missingness between the two primary comparison groups), the overall judgement is **"Some concerns"** rather than "High risk of bias."
