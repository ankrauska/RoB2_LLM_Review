**1. bias due to randomization: some concerns**
a. Judgement: There is insufficient information provided in the manuscript regarding how the random allocation sequence was concealed, which raises some concerns about the randomization process. 
b. Rationale: The study states, "After assent/consent for the RCT, participants who completed a computerized baseline assessment were randomly selected (stratified by gender and age: 14–15, 16–18 years), and assigned to 1 of the 3 study conditions..." While the use of a computerized assessment and random selection suggests sequence generation was adequate, there is no explicit mention of allocation concealment methods (e.g., whether the sequence was concealed from the enrolling personnel until assignment). 

**2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: Although participants and personnel were not blinded to the intervention assignment, there is no evidence that deviations from the intended intervention arose due to the trial context, and an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis was conducted.
b. Rationale: The trial report states, "An intent-to-treat approach was used. All randomly assigned participants were included whether the intervention was received or not (>95% received their assigned intervention during the ED visit)." Because an appropriate ITT analysis was used and the vast majority received their assigned intervention, the risk of bias due to deviations from the intended intervention is low.

**3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The follow-up rate was reasonably high and the statistical analysis approach appropriately accounted for participants with missing data.
b. Rationale: The study reports that "the 12-month follow-up rate was 83.6% (n = 607/726)." Furthermore, the analysis handled missing data properly: "models predicting 12-month outcomes were estimated by using generalized estimating equations (GEEs). GEE analyses use all data available for participants, including those lost to attrition..." There is no strong evidence suggesting that missingness was related to the true outcome in a way that would heavily bias the results.

**4. measurement of the outcome: some concerns**
a. Judgement: The outcome is self-reported, and participants were aware of their intervention assignment, meaning the assessment could potentially have been influenced by knowledge of the intervention received.
b. Rationale: Peer aggression was assessed using self-report surveys ("Items from the conflict tactic scale assessed past-year severe aggression toward peers..."). Because the participants were not blinded to whether they received the brief intervention or the control brochure, their self-reported answers could theoretically be influenced by their knowledge of having received an intervention targeting these behaviors.

**5. selective reporting: low risk of bias**
a. Judgement: The reported outcomes match the pre-specified outcomes in the trial registration, and the analyses are consistent with what would be expected for these measures.
b. Rationale: The trial was registered (NCT00251212). The registration lists the primary outcomes as "alcohol misuse and violent behaviors" at 12 months. The manuscript appropriately reports on these pre-specified outcomes (including peer aggression) using standard time frames and measurements (e.g., GEE models for 12-month data). There is no indication that the reported results were selected from multiple analyses based on statistical significance.
