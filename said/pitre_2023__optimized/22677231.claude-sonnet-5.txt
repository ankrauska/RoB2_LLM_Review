# RoB 2.0 Assessment: Beck Depression Inventory (Total Score) Post-Intervention

**Study:** Moritz et al. — RCT of internet-based therapy (Deprexis) vs. wait-list control for depression (NCT01401296)

**Effect of interest:** Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat)

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:**
The paper states only: *"Participants who left their email addresses at the end of the baseline survey were randomly allocated to the treatment or wait-list group in consecutive order. No stratification was applied."* No further detail is provided about the actual mechanism used to generate the random sequence (e.g., computer-generated random numbers), and the phrase "in consecutive order" introduces ambiguity as to whether a truly random process or a systematic/sequential process was used. No explicit description of allocation concealment procedures is given, although the fact that allocation occurred automatically via the online survey software after baseline data were collected suggests a reasonably low risk of foreknowledge or manipulation.

Baseline characteristics were well balanced: *"Randomization was successful: significant differences emerged neither for any of the background nor psychopathological variables"* (Table 1: all p > .1), which is reassuring, but the lack of methodological detail regarding sequence generation and concealment prevents a "Low risk" judgement. There is no information suggesting problems with randomization based on baseline data, but the absence of detail on the two key procedural signalling questions (1.1 and 1.2) results in a "Some concerns" judgement rather than "Low risk."

---

## 2. Deviations from the intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention)

**Judgement: Some concerns**

**Rationale:**
Participants and (given the self-help/online nature of the intervention) any personnel involved were necessarily aware of group assignment, as this was an open-label, unblinded wait-list-controlled design: *"participants received program access or were allocated to a wait-list control condition."* There is no placebo or sham condition.

There is plausible risk of deviations arising from the trial context: the wait-list group showed a much larger-than-expected improvement over time (*"the wait-list control group also improved substantially across time (Cohen's d = .58)"*), which the authors attribute possibly to *"patients' use of treatments other than Deprexis, ... spontaneous remission ... or other, unknown factors."* This raises concern that awareness of allocation to a no-treatment arm may have led some wait-list participants to seek alternative (non-protocol) interventions, although the study does report that concurrent treatment/medication status did not differ significantly between groups at baseline (Table 1), and there is no explicit report on whether use of concurrent treatments changed differentially over the 8-week period between arms. This lack of information about differential non-protocol intervention use during follow-up prevents a "Low risk" judgement but does not provide clear evidence of a substantial biasing effect either, hence "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

For the analysis: the ITT analysis was performed appropriately using multiple imputation for missing data, following ITT principles (*"ITT analyses considered data from all subjects with available baseline data. Multiple imputation was adopted to estimate post-scores for non-completers"*), satisfying the analysis component of this domain (Part 2 = Low risk).

Combining Part 1 (Some concerns) and Part 2 (Low risk) yields an overall domain judgement of **Some concerns**.

---

## 3. Missing outcome data

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
Overall completion was good (*"the completion rate was high (i.e., 81.9%)"*), though this falls short of "nearly all" participants. Missing data rates were not statistically significantly different between groups (Table 1: completer rates 85.7% wait-list vs. 78.1% Deprexis, χ²(1)=2.06, p>.1), and non-completers were largely similar to completers on measured baseline variables except two QoL subscales, where completers had *"slightly higher baseline scores."*

Critically, the authors used an appropriate approach to handle missing data for the ITT analysis: *"ITT analyses were performed with multiple imputation using SAS PROC MI ... 100 datasets were imputed, stratified for treatment."* Results after imputation were highly consistent with the complete-case (per-protocol) analysis for the primary outcome (BDI total score: PP: F(1,167)=5.51, p=.02, d=.36; ITT: p=.03, d=.36), indicating that the result is robust to the missing data and unlikely to be materially biased by the missingness pattern. This constitutes evidence that the result was not biased by missing outcome data (signalling question 3.2 = Probably yes), supporting a low risk of bias judgement for this domain.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome

**Judgement: High risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
The BDI is a self-report (participant-reported) outcome: *"The primary outcome of the study was depression symptomatology as assessed with the BDI."* As a participant-reported outcome, the outcome assessor is the participant themselves, and blinding of participants to intervention assignment was not possible in this open-label, wait-list-controlled design (participants clearly knew whether they had immediate access to Deprexis or were waiting).

Because the comparator was an inactive wait-list condition (not an active or placebo comparator), and the outcome is a subjective, participant-rated measure of mood/depressive symptoms, there is a strong plausible mechanism by which participants' self-reported symptom ratings could be influenced by their knowledge of allocation (e.g., disappointment/demoralization in the wait-list group, or expectancy/hope effects in the intervention group). The authors themselves acknowledge that, in this specific self-help/online context, *"the primary outcome rested exclusively on self-report,"* with no external validation of the specific total score used for analysis (only 29% of participants had externally confirmed baseline diagnoses, and no independent post-treatment severity rating was obtained). Additionally, the unexpectedly large wait-list improvement itself raises concern that non-blinded, self-report assessment may not have been a "clean" measure of the underlying trajectory.

Given that (i) outcome assessors (participants) were not blinded, (ii) the outcome is inherently subjective and could plausibly be influenced by knowledge of allocation, and (iii) the comparator is an inactive control (heightening risk of expectation-driven reporting), it is judged likely that assessment of the outcome was influenced by knowledge of the intervention received, warranting a **High risk of bias** judgement per RoB 2 guidance for unblinded participant-reported outcomes compared against a no-treatment/wait-list control.

---

## 5. Selective reporting

**Judgement: Low risk of bias**

**Rationale:**
The trial was prospectively registered (NCT01401296), with the BDI explicitly specified as the primary outcome (*"Severity of depressive symptoms as assessed with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)"*), matching the outcome measure and instrument reported in the publication. The WHOQOL-BREF secondary outcome specified in the registry was also reported in the article, along with additional outcomes (DAS, RSE, SBQ-R) that appear to be reported comprehensively rather than selectively.

For the BDI specifically, results are reported in full for both the total score and its three subscales, using both per-protocol and intention-to-treat analyses, with results presented regardless of statistical significance (e.g., the somatic symptoms subscale was non-significant and was still reported: *"F(1,167) = 2.33, p > .1, d = .24"*). This comprehensive and consistent reporting across pre-specified outcome measurements and both intended analysis approaches (PP and ITT) provides no indication that the reported BDI total score result was selected, on the basis of the results, from multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses. The minor discrepancy in the registered outcome timeframe ("7 Days" vs. the actual 8-week endpoint) appears to be a registry data-entry issue rather than evidence of outcome-reporting bias.
