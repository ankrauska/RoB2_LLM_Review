Based on the provided trial publication and the RoB 2.0 guidance, here is the assessment of the risk of bias for the outcome of delayed diagnostic performance:

    1. bias due to randomization: some concerns
           a. Judgement: The trial states participants were "randomly allocated" to one of four conditions, but it does not provide information on the method used to generate the random sequence or whether the allocation sequence was concealed.
           b. Rationale: The publication mentions that "Participants were randomly allocated to one of four study conditions" and "Participants completed this study individually using a custom-designed computer program." However, it lacks explicit details regarding random sequence generation (e.g., random number generator) and allocation concealment mechanisms. There were no obvious baseline imbalances (e.g., student types were well distributed), but the lack of methodological transparency raises some concerns.

    2. deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias
           a. Judgement: The intervention was administered via a computer program, eliminating the risk of personnel influencing the delivery. While two participants were excluded post-randomization based on their engagement, the numbers are too small to have a substantial impact on the result.
           b. Rationale: Interventions were standardized and delivered via a "custom-designed computer program," which acts as a blinded intervention provider. Although the authors excluded two participants from the analysis because they "spent less than five minutes on the delayed diagnostic test" (a post-randomization exclusion that violates strict Intention-To-Treat principles), the exclusion of only 2 out of nearly 100 participants does not have the potential for a substantial impact on the estimated effect of assignment to the interventions.

    3. missing outcome data: low risk of bias
           a. Judgement: The proportion of missing outcome data is small and unlikely to depend on the true value of the outcome.
           b. Rationale: Out of the initial enrolled participants, only five "failed to complete the delayed session of the study" and two were excluded for spending insufficient time on the test. This leaves a final sample size of 94 out of 101 (~7% missing data). This low attrition rate across the delayed testing period is sufficiently small that the missing outcomes could not have made a substantial, systematic difference to the estimated intervention effect.

    4. measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias
           a. Judgement: The outcome was measured objectively using a standardized, computer-administered diagnostic test, removing the risk of assessor bias. 
           b. Rationale: The outcome was a "diagnostic test consisted of 12 clinical vignettes for which students had to identify the correct diagnosis from a list of four possible disorders." Because the test consisted of multiple-choice diagnostic questions administered and graded via a custom computer program, the outcome assessors (the computer system) were completely blinded to the intervention received, and the assessment could not have been influenced by knowledge of the assigned group. 

    5. selective reporting: some concerns
           a. Judgement: The reported outcomes align with the clearly stated methods, but without an available pre-specified protocol or trial registration (common for educational trials), it cannot be fully confirmed that the analysis plan was finalized before unblinded data were available.
           b. Rationale: The study fully reports the outcomes described in its methods section (memory testing and diagnostic testing at immediate and delayed timepoints). However, there is no mention of a date-stamped, pre-specified statistical analysis plan or trial registry entry. Under RoB 2.0 guidance, the absence of a pre-specified protocol to compare against the final reported analyses typically warrants a judgement of "some concerns."
