1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The study reports use of a random allocation method: “a Microsoft Excel random number generator was used to randomize participants into the two conditions.” This supports that the allocation sequence probably included a random component.

   However, the report gives no information on allocation concealment—e.g., whether the investigator enrolling participants could foresee or influence the upcoming allocation. Thus, it is unclear whether allocation was concealed until participants were enrolled.

   Baseline comparability is reassuring: the authors report that the FI and control groups had no significant baseline differences in age, ethnicity, sex distribution, insomnia, fatigue, depression, dysfunctional sleep beliefs, or the LAT measures. Nevertheless, absence of information on allocation concealment leads to **some concerns** under RoB 2.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and the study investigator were necessarily aware of the allocated information condition, because the interventions were distinct information sheets read with the investigator individually: fatigue-cause information versus generic sleep information. Thus, blinding of participants and personnel was not feasible.

   However, for the effect of assignment, awareness only raises concern if it resulted in deviations from the intended intervention arising because of the trial context, which were likely to affect the outcome and differed between groups. There is no indication of such deviations. The difference in information content was the intended intervention, rather than a departure from protocol. The intervention was delivered in a standardized one-hour session, and the report does not describe contamination, differential co-interventions, switching, or implementation failures.

   The analysis retained the two randomized groups for comparison using mixed group-by-time ANOVAs. Although five participants were excluded before analysis, exclusions related to age, missing demographic data, or inability to calculate outcome scores; the issue of missing outcome data is considered separately. There is no evidence of an as-treated or naïve per-protocol analysis. Therefore, the analysis was appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to the fatigue-information versus control-information condition.

3. **Missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that five participants’ data were excluded, leaving 88 analysed participants, 44 per group. Reasons were: two participants were younger than 18 years, one did not complete demographic information, and two did not complete the outcome measures in a way that permitted calculation of a total score.

   The principal LAT outcome appears to have been available for nearly all randomized participants. In particular, only two exclusions were explicitly due to inability to score outcome measures. Even if all five exclusions are considered potentially relevant, this is approximately 5% of the initially enrolled sample and is unlikely to have materially changed the continuous LAT group comparison. The report does not suggest differential outcome-data loss between groups or reasons for missing LAT data that were clearly related to the unobserved post-intervention attribution scores.

   The reporting would have been stronger with a participant flow diagram and group-specific numbers and reasons for exclusions. Nonetheless, the amount of missing outcome data appears sufficiently small for the LAT result that it is unlikely to have caused material bias.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** LAT is a participant-reported outcome developed specifically for this study. Participants generated their own explanations for fatigue, ranked them, and assigned perceived likelihoods. Since the outcome is based directly on participants’ reported cognitions, the participants themselves were the outcome assessors.

   Participants were aware of whether they had just received information about multiple non-sleep causes of fatigue or generic sleep information. This knowledge was likely to influence their immediate post-intervention responses. The intervention was expressly designed to change the range of causes participants considered, and the post-intervention LAT was administered immediately after the information sheet. The paper itself acknowledges this concern:

   > “it is also possible that demand characteristics or priming played a role in FI participants’ post-intervention responses on the LAT.”

   The authors argue that the control sleep-information condition might also have primed sleep-related attributions, but this does not eliminate the substantial possibility that FI participants consciously or unconsciously reported the newly presented fatigue causes. The LAT was also a newly developed, unvalidated measure. Although the coding of written attributions was partly undertaken by a volunteer blinded to the hypotheses and checked by an investigator blinded to assignment, this does not resolve bias in the participants’ self-generated and self-rated responses.

   Therefore, because knowledge of the assigned intervention was likely to influence the participant-reported outcome assessment, this domain is at **high risk of bias**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article states the principal hypotheses in advance within the publication and reports results for the three stated LAT-derived outcomes: NumberAttributionScore, FrequencyScore, and ImportanceScore. It also provides group-specific pre- and post-intervention means, standard deviations, and interaction-test results for each outcome. This is reassuring.

   However, there is no available prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan or sufficiently detailed pre-results trial-registration information demonstrating that the specific LAT outcomes, their scoring rules, chosen time point, and ANOVA analyses were specified before unblinded outcome data were available. The LAT generated several possible analytic choices, including classification of open-ended responses as sleep-related versus non-sleep-related, handling of ambiguous responses, calculation of aggregate scores, ranking transformations, and analysis of pre/post data. The authors describe some coding decisions, including a post hoc decision that environmental sleep factors such as an “uncomfortable bed” or “loud roommate during the night” would be classified as sleep-related.

   In the absence of an accessible pre-specified analysis plan or detailed registry record allowing comparison with the published analyses, it cannot be ruled out that the reported numerical LAT results were selected from multiple possible outcome measurements or analyses. There is no direct evidence of selective reporting, but the lack of verifiable pre-specification results in **some concerns**.
