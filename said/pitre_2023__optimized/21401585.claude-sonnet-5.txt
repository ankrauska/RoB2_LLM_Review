1. Bias due to randomization: **Low risk of bias**

a. **Judgement:** The trial used a matched-pairs design with adequate random sequence generation and allocation concealment, and baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups.

b. **Rationale:** The article states: "Once matched, children were randomized to pairs using a computer generated list of random numbers and concealed envelopes opened by non-study personnel." This confirms a random component in sequence generation (computer-generated random numbers) and adequate allocation concealment (concealed envelopes opened by non-study personnel, i.e., independent of enrolling investigators). Regarding baseline imbalances, the authors report: "After matching and concealed random allocation within pairs, there were no differences between groups on demographic or baseline measures (Table I)." Table I confirms close similarity between groups on MACS, GMFCS, MUUL, AHA, JTTHF, grip strength, and sensory measures. The only noted imbalance—side of hemiplegia (50% left in CIMT vs 36% left in BIM)—is explained by the authors as due to matching constraints ("Four children were matched on all variables except side of hemiplegia, resulting in fewer children in the BIM training group with right-sided hemiplegia") and is not extreme enough to suggest a randomization problem, especially as side of hemiplegia was included as a covariate in the regression models. Overall, all signalling questions support low risk: sequence was random, allocation was concealed, and no baseline imbalance suggests a problem with the randomization process.

2. Bias due to deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

a. **Judgement:** Participants, carers, and those delivering the intervention could not be blinded due to the nature of the interventions (glove constraint vs bimanual training), but there is no strong evidence that trial-context-driven deviations affected the outcome in an unbalanced way. However, the analysis was not a strict ITT analysis for all randomized participants, and there is some ambiguity about handling of missing data/analysis approach.

b. **Rationale:** Participants and treating therapists were clearly aware of intervention allocation, as the interventions were behaviorally and visibly distinct (wearing a constraint glove vs bimanual practice): "Participants in the CIMT group wore a tailor-made glove on their unimpaired limb while attending the day camp..." This lack of blinding of participants/personnel is an inherent feature of this comparison. The article does not report any specific non-protocol interventions or unintended contamination arising from the trial context — camps were run in separate, controlled group settings ("The BIM training group was conducted first, and then tasks were modified for the CIMT group..."), reducing the likelihood of cross-contamination. However, there is limited explicit reporting on whether deviations from the intended intervention occurred due to trial context (e.g., motivation differences: "Motivation to maintain continual bimanual use was more challenging in the BIM training group" suggests some differential engagement, but this is described as an inherent feature of the intervention rather than a trial-context artifact). Compliance was high and reported for both groups: "Compliance was 100% for CIMT, and 97% with BIM training." Analysis: The paper states, "Analyses were on an intention-to-treat basis according to CONSORT guidelines," which supports appropriate analysis. However, one dropout occurred and it is unclear if this participant's data were included per ITT principles or how missing outcome data were handled in the GEE model (GEE can handle missing data under a missing-at-random assumption, which somewhat supports ITT validity). Given (1) unavoidable lack of blinding of participants/personnel with no clear evidence of trial-context-driven deviations affecting outcomes disproportionately, and (2) a general statement of intention-to-treat analysis without full detail on handling of the single dropout, this domain is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk" or "High risk."

3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

a. **Judgement:** Attrition was minimal and well documented, with balanced follow-up between groups and clear reasons for missing data that are unrelated to the outcome.

b. **Rationale:** The trial profile (Fig 1, CONSORT flow diagram) documents recruitment, randomization (64 children, 32 per group) and follow up. The text states: "Sixty-four children (32 matched pairs) were randomly assigned to treatment and 62 children received their allocated intervention. There was one dropout on day two of the camp from the BIM training group, because of pre-existing emotional and behavioural difficulties." This represents a very small proportion of missing data (1/64, ~1.6%), and the reason given (pre-existing emotional/behavioural difficulties unrelated to treatment response) does not suggest that missingness depended on the true value of the outcome. The paper further states "Compliance was 100% for CIMT, and 97% with BIM training," and outcomes were assessed at baseline, 3, and 26 weeks by blinded assessors, with results reported for both groups at each time point (Table S1, referenced in text). There is no indication of substantial differential loss to follow-up between groups, and the amount of missing data is small enough that it is unlikely to have materially affected the estimated intervention effect for MUUL. Therefore, missing outcome data is judged as low risk.

4. Measurement of the outcome: **Low risk of bias**

a. **Judgement:** The MUUL is an appropriate, validated outcome measure for this population, was measured identically across groups, and was scored by an assessor blinded to treatment allocation.

b. **Rationale:** The article specifies that the MUUL was scored by blinded raters: "The primary outcome measures were videotaped and scored in random order by trained occupational therapists masked to group allocation." This directly addresses risk of differential outcome assessment bias since the outcome assessor (a rater involving some judgement) was blinded to intervention received, precluding the possibility that knowledge of allocation influenced scoring. The measurement method was identical for both groups (video-taped MUUL assessment, standardized administration manual), so ascertainment of the outcome did not differ between intervention groups. The measurement tool itself is appropriate for the outcome of quality of movement of the impaired limb, and its psychometric properties were assessed by the study team: "The ICC for the total percentage score of MUUL was 0.93 (95% CI 0.84–0.97)," indicating high reliability. Although the outcome-assessing therapists conducting real-time assessments at baseline/3/26 weeks "were aware of group allocation," the primary scoring for RoB purposes—the actual scoring of MUUL used for analysis—was done by independent, blinded raters from video recordings ("scored in random order by trained occupational therapists masked to group allocation"), which mitigates this concern. Therefore, risk of bias in measurement of the MUUL outcome is judged low.

5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

a. **Judgement:** The MUUL outcome (quality of movement of the impaired upper limb) was pre-specified as a primary outcome in the trial protocol/registration, and the reported result corresponds to the pre-specified outcome, measurement instrument, and analysis method, with no evidence of selective reporting from multiple options.

b. **Rationale:** The trial protocol paper (Boyd et al., BMC Neurol 2010) explicitly pre-specified the MUUL as a primary activity outcome measure to assess unimanual capacity/quality of movement of the impaired limb, consistent with the published result: "The MUUL16 was selected to measure quality of movement of the impaired upper limb." The analysis reported (GEE regression modelling comparing groups at 3 and 26 weeks, with the total percentage score) is a straightforward, single, pre-specified outcome measurement and analytic approach: "The MUUL was reported as a percentage with higher scores reflecting greater quality of upper limb movement," with no evidence of multiple alternative scoring methods, subscales or timepoints selectively reported for MUUL. The trial registration information (doc_id 4dab5cab) can be cross-checked to confirm that MUUL was listed as a primary outcome for the described time points and there is no indication of selective outcome/analysis reporting for this specific outcome (e.g., no alternative unimanual capacity measures were substituted or omitted). Since the analysis plan (ITT, GEE with matching covariates) matches what would be expected a priori and the result presented is consistent with the pre-specified outcome domain, measurement, and analysis, this domain is judged as low risk of bias.
