1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the random sequence was appropriately generated, and baseline characteristics were broadly balanced, but allocation concealment was not described.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial used a computer-generated allocation sequence: “*Randomization was performed by means of a computer-generated random sequence with stratification for age, gender, and positive family history for CHD using the Pocock and Simon method.*” This supports a random sequence-generation process.

   However, the report does not state how the allocation sequence was concealed from recruiters before randomization. This is particularly relevant because one investigator (HJ) “*generated the random allocation sequence and study arm assignment*,” while HJ was also among those who recruited participants. Randomization occurred only after the first visit had been completed and the second visit scheduled, which provides some protection against manipulation, but does not fully establish concealment.

   Baseline data do not suggest a problem with randomization. Groups were similar in age, sex, smoking history, family history, BMI, blood pressure, lipids and education; all reported baseline-comparison p values were non-significant. Therefore, the absence of reported allocation concealment leads to **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and personnel were necessarily aware of the assigned disclosure strategy: one group received conventional CHD risk only (CRS), while the other received genetics-informed CHD risk (CRS+GRS). The registration record also specifies no masking. Thus, participants, genetic counsellors and physicians were aware of intervention assignment.

   Nevertheless, for the assignment effect, lack of blinding only creates bias if deviations from intended intervention arose because of the trial context, were related to allocation, and were likely to affect shared-decision-making scores. Both groups received a structured risk-disclosure process, met with a genetic counsellor and physician, and used a modified Statin Choice decision aid: “*Disclosure of risk in both arms was facilitated by the use of a modified version of the Statin Choice decision aid.*” There is no evidence that trial personnel provided differential non-protocol care, that participants crossed to the other disclosure strategy, or that other trial-context deviations occurred differentially by group.

   The analysis compared the randomized groups, with “*randomization groups as the independent variables and physician visit satisfaction and SDM scores as the dependent variables*.” The reported comparison therefore corresponds to an intention-to-treat-type comparison by assigned group. The one participant without an SDM survey is addressed under missing outcome data, rather than in this domain.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:** SDM survey data were available for 206 of 207 randomized participants (99.5%). The report states: “*The SDM survey was completed by 206 study participants.*” The participant flow diagram likewise indicates that all 207 randomized participants completed the disclosure and physician-visit intervention, and only one participant lacked the SDM survey.

   For this continuous outcome, outcome data from approximately 99.5% of randomized participants is sufficiently complete that the missing value could not plausibly have made an important difference to the estimated between-group effect. There is no indication of differential missingness by group or of a reason for missing data related to the underlying SDM score. Although the reporting of handling of partially completed questionnaires is somewhat unclear (“*If a participant did not answer all of the 11 questions, he/she was excluded from the analyses. If some questions were answered, the missing answers were assigned a score of ‘0’*”), the very small amount of completely missing outcome data means this is unlikely to materially bias the result.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The SDM-Q is a validated patient-reported questionnaire and is an appropriate instrument for assessing patients’ perception of shared decision-making. Both groups completed the same survey after comparable clinical encounters, so there is no indication that the technical method or timing of data collection differed between groups.

   However, the outcome assessors were the participants themselves. Participants knew whether they had received conventional risk information alone or genetics-informed risk information, since this knowledge was intrinsic to the intervention. The trial was unmasked (“*masking: NONE*” in the registration record), and the article describes explicit discussion of genetic risk with participants assigned to CRS+GRS.

   Shared decision-making score is a subjective, participant-reported assessment of the encounter. Participants’ perceptions and responses could plausibly be influenced by knowing that genetic information was disclosed, by expectations about receiving more personalized information, or by the perceived novelty of the genomics-based intervention. The outcome was measured immediately after the intervention encounter, when such expectations are particularly likely to affect reporting. Therefore, it is likely that outcome assessment was influenced by knowledge of intervention received, resulting in **high risk of bias** in measurement of this subjective outcome.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial registration identifies “*Shared decision making (as assessed by survey)*” as a secondary outcome, consistent with the outcome reported in the article. The publication reports the validated SDM-Q overall score, individual SDM-Q items, and a secondary video-based OPTION5 assessment. This consistency provides no direct evidence that the reported SDM-Q result was selected from multiple results because of its statistical significance or direction.

   However, the registry entry does not specify the SDM questionnaire, the exact assessment time point, scoring rules, handling of partially completed questionnaires, or the planned statistical analysis. The article also does not provide a dated statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. Since shared decision-making could potentially be measured and analysed in multiple ways—for example, patient-reported SDM-Q total score, individual items, dichotomized scores, and observer-rated OPTION5 score—it cannot be confirmed that the reported numerical SDM-Q result was analysed according to a fully pre-specified plan or selected without knowledge of results.

   Accordingly, there is insufficient evidence for low risk of bias, but no direct evidence of selective choice of a favourable result; the appropriate judgement is **some concerns**.
