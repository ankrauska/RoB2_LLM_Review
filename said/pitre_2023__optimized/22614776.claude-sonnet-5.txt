# Risk of Bias Assessment (RoB 2.0)
## Outcome: Peer Aggression at 12 Months
## Comparison: Therapist-assisted brief intervention (TBI) vs. Control
## Effect of interest: Effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat)

---

### 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** The trial reports random allocation stratified by age and gender, but does not describe the actual method of sequence generation (e.g., computer-generated numbers) or the method used to conceal allocation from those enrolling participants. There is also no explicit statistical comparison of baseline peer-aggression rates between groups, although raw percentages in Table 1 show a notable difference (Therapist 82.7% vs. Computer 75.5% vs. Control 77.9%), and the text notes "initial differences in dropping out of school by condition" that required statistical adjustment. Because allocation concealment is not described (No information) and there is no clear evidence that the observed baseline differences reflect problems with randomization (though not fully excluded), this domain reaches "Some concerns" rather than "Low."

**b. Rationale:** 
- "participants who completed a computerized baseline assessment were randomly selected (stratified by gender and age…), and assigned to 1 of the 3 study conditions (TBI, CBI, control) during the ED visit."
- No description of the sequence generation mechanism or concealment procedure (e.g., sealed envelopes, central randomization) is provided in the manuscript.
- "Because of initial differences in dropping out of school by condition, this variable was controlled for in analysis" – indicating an identified baseline imbalance requiring adjustment, though not judged severe enough to reflect randomization failure.
- Table 1 baseline aggression rates differ by up to 7 percentage points across arms, but no formal significance testing is reported, and imbalance could be compatible with chance in a sample of this size.

---

### 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Participants and intervention deliverers (therapists) could not be blinded to group assignment, given the nature of the intervention (in-person motivational interviewing vs. brochure). There is no explicit information about whether trial-context-related deviations (e.g., control participants seeking similar counseling services, or contamination) occurred. However, an appropriate intention-to-treat analysis was used. The lack of information on potential deviations arising from trial context in an unblinded psychosocial trial results in "Some concerns" rather than "Low."

**b. Rationale:**
- Blinding was not possible: "Participants assigned to the control received a trifold brochure with community resources," clearly distinguishable from the TBI's 37-minute in-person motivational interviewing session — participants and personnel necessarily knew their allocation.
- No information is provided about whether control or CBI participants sought out additional violence-prevention resources or counseling because of trial engagement (i.e., no data on non-protocol co-interventions).
- Appropriate analysis: "An intent-to-treat approach was used. All randomly assigned participants were included whether the intervention was received or not (>95% received their assigned intervention during the ED visit)." This satisfies the criterion for appropriate ITT analysis for the assignment effect.
- Given appropriate ITT analysis but lack of information regarding trial-context-driven deviations in an unblinded design, "Some concerns" is appropriate for this domain.

---

### 3. Missing outcome data: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Outcome data were not available for all or nearly all participants — the 12-month follow-up rate was 83.6% (607/726), meaning ~16% of participants had missing data. This proportion, combined with a binary/dichotomous outcome subject to differential dropout risk, is large enough that missingness could plausibly relate to the true outcome value (e.g., participants doing poorly, or well, may have been differentially likely to be lost to follow-up). However, there is no clear evidence (e.g., differential dropout rates by arm, or differing reasons for dropout related to the outcome) that missingness was related to true outcome status, nor were sensitivity analyses reported to confirm robustness to missing data assumptions. This leads to "Some concerns" rather than "High."

**b. Rationale:**
- "the 12-month follow-up rate was 83.6% (n = 607/726)."
- GEE analysis was used ("GEE analyses use all data available for participants, including those lost to attrition"), which relies on a missing-at-random assumption but was not accompanied by sensitivity analyses testing robustness to plausible non-random missingness scenarios.
- No explicit reporting of differential attrition rates or reasons for dropout by intervention arm is provided, so it cannot be excluded that missingness was related to the true value of peer aggression (e.g., differentially engaged/high-risk youths being lost to follow up), but there is also no strong signal that this occurred differentially between arms.
- The authors acknowledge this limitation generally: "although attrition was low (14%)… it is always possible that those lost to follow-up may have biased results" (note discrepancy between stated 14% and calculated ~16.4%, but both indicate meaningful missingness).

### 4. Measurement of the outcome: **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Peer aggression was measured via self-report (participant-reported outcome) using the conflict tactics scale administered as a computerized survey. The outcome assessor is therefore the participant, who was necessarily aware of intervention assignment (particularly stark for the TBI arm, which involved sustained, personal interaction with a therapist for ~37 minutes, versus the control's simple brochure). This creates strong potential for socially desirable reporting/demand characteristics favoring the TBI arm at follow-up, particularly given the personal rapport built with a therapist. Given the strong theoretical and empirically supported mechanism (participant-reported outcomes in unblinded psychosocial interventions are highly susceptible to differential reporting bias), it is judged likely that assessment of peer aggression was influenced by knowledge of intervention received in the therapist arm, resulting in high risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:**
- "TBI participants were less likely to report severe peer aggression at 12 months in comparison with controls" — this is a self-reported measure, not an objective clinical or administrative record.
- Method of measurement: "Items from the conflict tactic scale assessed past-year severe aggression toward peers." Assessment relies entirely on the participant's own report.
- No blinding of participants was possible or attempted—the TBI arm received a substantive relational intervention ("brief intervention... delivered by a therapist with computer assistance") versus a simple brochure for controls, creating asymmetric expectancy/demand effects that are particularly likely to bias self-reported behavioral outcomes like aggression.
- The authors themselves speculate about mechanisms consistent with reporting bias: "It may be that key components of BI, such as empathy, are not easily transferrable to computerized platforms… a therapist may be able to provide more complex reflections and elicit change talk" — suggesting an interpersonal/relational mechanism plausibly affecting self-report rather than only genuine behavior change.
- Because CBI (also unblinded but without personal contact) showed no significant effect while TBI (with strong personal/therapeutic rapport) did, this pattern is also consistent with differential reporting bias specific to the interpersonal component rather than a "pure" behavioral effect.

---

### 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** The trial was prospectively registered (NCT00251212, registered 2005, prior to trial start in September 2006), specifying primary outcomes as "alcohol misuse and violent behaviors" assessed at 12 months. Peer aggression is a core component of the pre-specified "violent behaviors" outcome domain and was consistently reported across the trial's published time points (3, 6, and 12 months) using the same measurement instrument (conflict tactics scale) and analytic approach (GEE models for dichotomous outcomes). All three violence-related outcomes (peer aggression, peer victimization, violence consequences) and three alcohol-related outcomes are reported in full in Tables 2 and 3, with no evidence of selective omission of unfavorable results.

**b. Rationale:**
- Registry: "This trial has been registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (identifier NCT00251212)." Primary outcome specified: "Primary outcome variables include alcohol misuse and violent behaviors," timeframe "12-months" — consistent with what is reported.
- The methods explicitly state pre-specified outcome measures consistent with earlier publications from the same trial (3- and 6-month follow-ups by the same measures): "Items from the conflict tactic scale... assessed past-year severe aggression toward peers... Past-year peer victimization... was assessed."
- All pre-specified violence and alcohol outcomes are fully reported in Tables 1–3 (severe peer aggression, peer victimization, violence consequences, binge drinking, alcohol misuse, alcohol consequences), with both significant and non-significant results presented transparently (e.g., "BI and control groups did not differ on alcohol-related variables at 12 months" is reported despite being a null/unfavorable finding).
- No evidence of selective outcome-measurement or selective analysis reporting (e.g., no indication of multiple unreported scales, alternative cut-points, or unreported models) was found.
