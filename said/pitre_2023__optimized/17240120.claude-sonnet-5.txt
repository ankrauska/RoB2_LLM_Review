# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## Outcome: Time to relapse from start date of double-blind treatment

---

## 1. Bias arising from the randomization process: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The allocation sequence was randomly generated: "eligible patients were assigned to escitalopram or placebo treatment according to a computer-generated randomisation list." Allocation concealment was adequate: "The details of the randomisation series were unknown to any of the investigators and were contained in a set of sealed opaque envelopes. At each study centre, sequentially enrolled patients were assigned the lowest randomisation number available in blocks of four." This meets the extended criteria for adequate concealment (sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes with independent control of the sequence). Baseline characteristics at randomization appear balanced between groups: "There were no differences in demographics and clinical parameters between the two treatment groups in the randomisation period." Table 1 confirms similar age, sex distribution, race, age at onset, and duration of illness between placebo (n=157) and escitalopram (n=163) groups at randomization. No evidence of problems with the randomization process.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Participants, carers, and personnel were blinded during the double-blind period: "Study medications were tablets for oral administration, of identical appearance, taste or smell... All study personnel and participants were blinded to treatment assignment for the entire duration of the study." A taper strategy was used to minimize discontinuation-related unblinding: patients switching to placebo received a step-down dose (10mg for one week) rather than an abrupt switch, reducing the likelihood that side-effect-related unblinding drove deviations from intended intervention arising from trial context. There is no evidence of non-protocol interventions or contamination arising because of trial context. The analysis was appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment to intervention: "All efficacy analyses in the double-blind treatment period were conducted using the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, which consisted of all randomised patients who took at least one dose of trial medication and had at least one post-randomisation efficacy assessment" — this is consistent with an appropriate ITT/mITT approach (2 patients were excluded for not meeting the open-label response criterion, which is a legitimate eligibility-based exclusion unrelated to post-randomization deviations, not a violation of ITT principles for this domain). No evidence that any deviations that did occur (e.g., dose adjustments, discontinuation symptoms) were unbalanced between arms in a way that would bias the assignment effect; sensitivity analyses censoring early relapses (7/14 days) to account for discontinuation symptoms confirmed the primary result was robust: "In analyses using the ITT and a 7- or 14-day relapse cut-off period after randomisation, during which relapses were censored to discount the possible effect of discontinuation symptoms, escitalopram was significantly superior to placebo."

---

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** Outcome data (time-to-event) were available for essentially all randomized participants, and dropout for reasons other than relapse was low and balanced between groups: "the overall withdrawal rate, excluding relapses, was comparable for both treatments: 7.9% for patients treated with escitalopram (13 patients), and 8.9% for patients treated with placebo (14 patients)." For time-to-event analysis, patients who withdrew for reasons other than relapse would be censored; because these rates are low and similar between groups, informative censoring is unlikely to have biased the hazard ratio. The primary analysis included the full ITT population (320 patients, with only 2 excluded due to not meeting eligibility criteria for randomization, unrelated to missingness in the outcome). The CONSORT-style flow (Figure 2) documents patient disposition and reasons for withdrawal transparently. There is no indication that missingness in the relapse outcome depended on its true, unobserved value in a manner that differed by group beyond the low and balanced discontinuation rates reported.

---

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns.

**b. Rationale:** The outcome (relapse) was measured using the Y-BOCS (an observer-reported outcome involving some judgement) and additionally included "an unsatisfactory treatment effect (lack of efficacy) as judged by the investigator" as an alternative relapse criterion — this latter component is a subjective, judgement-based clinical assessment. Although raters were trained ("Only those investigators who had actively participated in rater training sessions prior to inclusion of patients into the study were allowed to rate patients... Rater training was undertaken to increase inter-rater reliability"), and the study was double-blind, discontinuation symptoms (e.g., dizziness, nausea, which were significantly more common in the placebo group shortly after randomization: "there were statistically significantly higher incidences of dizziness and nausea in the placebo group") could have unblinded raters or patients in a minority of cases, potentially influencing the "lack of efficacy" judgement component or Y-BOCS scoring in a manner correlated with treatment assignment. The article does not explicitly confirm that outcome assessors remained unaware of intervention assignment in all cases, and reliance on investigator judgement for the "lack of efficacy" relapse criterion introduces a risk that is not fully mitigated, although the Y-BOCS-based criterion accounted for the vast majority of relapses (97%) and sensitivity analyses excluding early relapses (which would capture most discontinuation-related unblinding) confirmed a robust effect. Because the outcome assessment could plausibly have been influenced by knowledge of intervention received (via unblinding due to discontinuation symptoms) but there is no strong evidence this actually occurred to a meaningful degree, this is judged as "Some concerns" rather than "High risk."

---

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

**b. Rationale:** The primary outcome (time to relapse from start of double-blind treatment) and its definition were pre-specified: "The prospectively defined primary analysis of efficacy was the time to relapse from the start date of double-blind treatment." This matches the outcome as reported in the results, using the pre-specified analysis (log-rank test, Cox proportional hazards, ITT population), consistent with the sample size/power calculation section which described the same primary analysis in advance ("The sample size and power calculations were based on analysis of time to relapse in the double-blind period... using a two-tailed, log-rank test at the 5% level of significance"). Secondary outcomes (proportion relapsed, Y-BOCS change, NIMH-OCS, CGI, response/remission rates) were also pre-specified ("The prospectively defined secondary efficacy parameters included...") and are reported consistently with these plans. There is no evidence of selective reporting from multiple eligible outcome measurements or analyses for the specific time-to-relapse outcome; additional post-hoc analyses (e.g., using only the Y-BOCS relapse criterion, or censoring at 7/14 days) are clearly labeled as post-hoc/sensitivity analyses rather than substituted as the primary result. No trial registry entry was provided among the source documents, but the consistency between the stated a priori primary analysis, sample-size justification, and reported result supports a low risk judgement for this specific outcome.
