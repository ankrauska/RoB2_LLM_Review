1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the trial is described as randomized with equal-sized groups and baseline characteristics appear comparable, but the method used to generate and conceal the allocation sequence was not reported.

   b. **Rationale:** The article describes a “prospective, 1:1, randomized study” with “random allocation” of 80 participants to conventional care (n=40) or conventional care plus nutritional counselling (n=40). However, it does not state how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence) or how allocation was concealed from those enrolling participants. Under RoB 2, a statement that a study was randomized, without these details, does not establish adequate sequence generation or allocation concealment.

   Baseline characteristics were generally similar between groups, including age, sex, education, cognitive screening score, comorbidities, physical activity, APOE genotype, and MCI type. There is no obvious baseline imbalance strongly suggesting a failed randomization process. Nevertheless, absence of allocation-concealment information leads to **some concerns**.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and personnel delivering the intervention were necessarily aware of allocation: one group attended “26 to 28 1-hour meetings” of nutritional counselling whereas the control group received conventional medical care only. Thus, participants and care providers were not blinded.

   All participants received conventional geriatric care and physical-activity advice, while the intervention group additionally received group nutritional counselling. The authors reported that both groups lost weight and that the between-group difference in BMI change was not statistically significant: “mean ΔBMI intensive group −2.1 … kg/m², conventional group −1.3 … kg/m², P=.428.” They also acknowledged that recruitment itself may have altered behaviour in both groups: “Because the process of recruiting volunteers included information on the risks of obesity, it is also possible that this may have increased motivation to lose weight in both groups.”

   This provides a plausible trial-context mechanism by which control participants may have modified diet or weight-related behaviours. However, the report does not provide sufficient information on adherence to counselling, dietary behaviour by randomized group, or other non-protocol weight-loss interventions to determine whether consequential deviations occurred and whether they were balanced between groups. The longitudinal randomized comparison used generalized estimating equations with group, time, and group-by-time terms; this is broadly appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment among participants with observed follow-up data. Missing data are considered separately. Therefore, the domain is judged **some concerns**, rather than high risk.

3. **Missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** Neuropsychological follow-up data at 12 months were available for 75 of 80 randomized participants (93.8%). The flow diagram indicates that 37/40 conventional-care participants and 38/40 intensive-intervention participants were analysed at 12 months. Thus, five participants (6.3%) did not contribute follow-up outcome data.

   The report states: “Five women did not complete the follow-up: 4 dropped out and one died of pneumonia.” The reasons for the four dropouts, their randomized groups, and their likely cognitive outcomes were not reported. Although missingness was limited and numerically similar across groups (3 versus 2 participants), cognitive status or health deterioration could plausibly be associated with failure to complete neuropsychological testing in an elderly population with MCI.

   There was no reported sensitivity analysis addressing plausible outcomes among missing participants, nor an analysis specifically designed to account for informative missingness. The amount and balance of missing data do not establish that bias is likely to be substantial, but the available information cannot rule it out. This supports **some concerns**.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** The outcome was assessed using a specified neuropsychological test battery, including tests of memory, attention, executive function, fluency/language, and global cognition. These are established, structured outcome measures, and the same assessments were conducted at baseline and 12 months. There is no indication that the measurement method or timing differed systematically between intervention groups.

   Importantly, the trial registration states that the study used single masking and specifies that the **outcomes assessor** was masked. This is particularly relevant because several neuropsychological outcomes require administration and scoring by an assessor and may involve judgement. Blinded outcome assessment reduces the risk that knowledge of treatment allocation influenced test administration, scoring, or interpretation.

   Although participants knew whether they attended nutritional-counselling sessions, and effort on cognitive testing could theoretically be influenced by such knowledge, the primary assessment procedures were standardized neuropsychological tests and outcome assessors were registered as blinded. There is no evidence that outcome assessment differed between groups or was influenced by allocation knowledge. Therefore, this domain is judged at **low risk of bias**.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The registration record identifies “Neuropsychological test performance” as a primary outcome but does not specify the neuropsychological instruments, individual cognitive domains, time point, scoring approach, primary contrast, or statistical analysis plan. No dated statistical analysis plan was available. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that the reported neuropsychological analyses were specified before unblinded outcome data became available.

   The publication reports numerous cognitive measures and multiple analytic approaches. The randomized longitudinal comparison was based on generalized estimating equations, but the authors also state that, because the between-group BMI difference was smaller than expected, they “decided to a posteriori pool the longitudinal analysis between groups and to test for the effects of change of BMI as a continuous variable.” The principal positive conclusions are consequently based substantially on post hoc observational associations between change in BMI and changes in cognition, rather than on the randomized group-by-time comparison, which showed “no significant time-group interaction for most of the tests after 12 months.”

   The authors further acknowledge that “due to the exploratory nature of the analyses and the relatively small sample, it was not possible to carry a multiple comparisons correction.” There is no direct evidence that a particular cognitive result was selected because it was statistically significant or favourable, but the combination of broad registration, many eligible cognitive outcomes and analyses, and explicitly post hoc pooled analyses creates **some concerns** about selection of the reported result.
