1. **bias due to randomization: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The trial used a randomized split-mouth, cross-quadrant design. Allocation of the etch/primer system was made using block randomization: “*Randomization for allocation of either of the two etch and primer systems to the upper right quadrant was undertaken using a block randomization method*.” Although the precise method used to generate the random order within blocks is not fully detailed, the stated use of block randomization supports a *probably yes* response for random sequence generation.

   Allocation concealment appears adequate. The report states that “*The operators were unaware of the assignment until the patient was entered into the trial*.” Thus, treatment allocation was not known to the operators before participant entry, limiting the opportunity for selective recruitment or manipulation of allocation.

   Baseline characteristics were presented for the overall sample rather than by allocated etching system. However, this was a within-patient split-mouth comparison, so patient-level characteristics such as age and sex were inherently balanced between the two etching-system conditions. There is no indication of an important imbalance in the numbers of brackets assigned to each system or of another problem with randomization.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, for the effect of assignment to interventions at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   Participants were unaware of which system was used on each side of the mouth: “*The patients were unaware which system had been used on each side of the mouth*.” Operators could not be blinded because the systems required different application procedures: “*it was not possible to blind the operators to the type of system being used*.”

   Lack of operator blinding does not itself imply bias for the assignment effect. The relevant question is whether knowledge of allocation caused deviations from the protocol that were related to the trial context and likely to affect bracket failure. The report describes standardized bonding procedures, including specified etching/priming procedures, the same adhesive, defined curing procedures, and monitoring of curing-light output. It reports no evidence of differential co-interventions, failure to implement the allocated bonding procedures, or other deviations arising because operators knew the assigned system.

   The analysis retained the allocated treatment comparison. All 661 bonded brackets were included, and the analysis accounted for clustering of brackets within mouths: “*Binary outcomes were tested with logistic regression … adjusted for the clustering within mouths*,” while survival analyses used Cox regression “*adjusted for the clustering within mouths*.” The one participant who moved away after seven months was retained in the survival analysis up to the time of censoring. These are appropriate approaches for estimating the effect of assignment.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:**  
   Outcome follow-up was essentially complete. The authors report: “*All patients were observed for the entire 12-month period apart from one 13-year-old male who moved to another city after 7 months of observation, during which time no bracket failures occurred.*” This participant’s observed follow-up was retained: “*His data was still included in the analysis, as it was considered that it still provided useful information on bracket survival rates.*”

   Therefore, only 1 of 39 participants had incomplete follow-up, and he contributed seven months of event-free follow-up data to the time-to-event analysis. Given the small amount of censoring, the lack of reported bracket failures before censoring, and the substantially larger number of observed bracket failures overall, it is unlikely that the missing later follow-up could have materially changed the estimated comparative failure rate. There is no indication that missingness differed systematically by allocated etching system or was related to unobserved bracket failure.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The outcome—bracket bond failure—is a relatively objective clinical event. The protocol states that when a bond failed, “*the tooth on which the failure occurred [and] the date of failure*” were recorded. The measurement method was the same for both allocated etching/priming systems, with the same follow-up period and clinical monitoring procedures.

   Operators were aware of the assigned system, but the article provides no indication that ascertainment of whether a bracket had failed differed between groups. A bracket detaching from a tooth is not a highly subjective outcome requiring interpretation of symptom severity or clinical improvement. Knowledge of the system could theoretically influence the timing of recognition or recording of a failure, but it is unlikely to have materially influenced whether an actual bracket failure occurred or was identified. The adhesive remnant index assessment involved more judgement, but that is separate from the failure-rate outcome assessed here.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   The publication clearly reports the principal failure-rate comparison and associated survival analysis: failure was 11.2% with the self-etching primer versus 3.9% with conventional etching/priming, and a Cox model reported a hazard ratio of 0.4 (95% CI 0.2–0.8). It also reports results for several prespecified-looking explanatory factors, including age, sex, operator, tooth position, and number of bracket manipulations.

   However, there is no accessible prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan in the information provided, and the publication does not state that the reported numerical result for failure rate was analysed according to an analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data became available. The article reports several possible analyses of the outcome—crude failure rates, logistic regression, Kaplan–Meier curves, and Cox proportional-hazards regression—but does not clearly identify one analysis as pre-specified primary analysis or explain the hierarchy among them.

   The available trial information does not provide clear evidence that the reported failure-rate result was selected from multiple analyses or outcome measurements based on its statistical significance. Nevertheless, absence of a verifiable prospective, detailed analysis plan means selective selection among eligible analyses or time-to-event summaries cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, this domain raises **some concerns**, rather than high risk of bias.
