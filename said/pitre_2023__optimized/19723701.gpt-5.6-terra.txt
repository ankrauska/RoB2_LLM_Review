**Outcome assessed:** first event of **all-cause death or nonfatal heart-failure event**, comparing CRT–ICD versus ICD alone (published ITT hazard ratio 0.66, 95% CI 0.52–0.84). The assessment of deviations from intended intervention addresses the **effect of assignment** to CRT–ICD versus ICD at baseline.

1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the trial was described as randomized and baseline groups were well balanced, but the available report does not adequately describe either the random sequence generation or concealment of allocation before enrolment.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   * The trial reports that patients “were randomly assigned in a 3:2 ratio” and “were stratified according to clinical center and ischemic status with the use of an algorithm that ensured near balance in each stratum.” The registration record also identifies the allocation as randomized and specifies stratification by clinical center and ischemic status.  
   * However, neither source specifies that the algorithm incorporated a random component (e.g., computer-generated random sequence or randomized minimization), nor how allocation was concealed from personnel enrolling patients. Thus, sequence generation and allocation concealment cannot be confirmed.  
   * Baseline characteristics appear balanced and do not suggest a failure of randomization. For example, mean age was 64 versus 65 years; male sex was 75.6% versus 74.7%; mean ejection fraction was 0.24 in both groups; and key medication use was similar.  
   * Under RoB 2, where baseline balance is compatible with chance but allocation concealment is not reported, the appropriate judgement is **some concerns** rather than low risk.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention. Although this was an unblinded device trial and crossovers occurred, the primary analysis was intention-to-treat and there is no evidence that deviations caused by the trial context were differentially introduced in a way likely to bias the effect of assignment.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   * The trial was necessarily open label: “The treating physicians were aware of study-group assignments.” Participants and treating personnel were therefore also likely aware of assignment.  
   * There were deviations from assigned treatment. Among CRT–ICD-assigned participants, 82/1089 (7.5%) received an ICD-only device because of technical difficulty placing the coronary-vein lead. In the ICD-only group, 91/731 (12.4%) received a CRT–ICD device; 61 occurred after a heart-failure event, while 30 occurred before an endpoint at physician discretion.  
   * These departures do not, by themselves, cause bias in an assessment of the effect of assignment. The technical implantation failures and clinically driven treatment changes are not clearly shown to have arisen because of the trial context in the RoB 2 sense (i.e., differential departures induced by participation in the trial or by personnel undermining the protocol).  
   * Importantly, “Data analysis was performed according to the intention-to-treat principle,” and the trial registry likewise states that “Data from all patients enrolled were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis.” The reported primary analysis therefore retained participants in their randomized groups.  
   * For the assignment effect, the use of ITT analysis is appropriate. The crossovers would tend to dilute the contrast between assigned groups rather than invalidate the ITT estimate.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because outcome follow-up was incomplete and differed materially between groups, while the handling and consequences of censoring/missing primary-outcome data were not adequately reported.

   f. **Rationale:**  
   * The publication reports that “44 patients in the CRT–ICD group (4.0%) and 55 in the ICD-only group (7.5%) declined to continue participating in the study, were withdrawn by a physician, or were lost to follow-up.” Thus, follow-up relevant to this time-to-first-event composite was incomplete, with a notable between-group difference (7.5% versus 4.0%).  
   * The reasons are aggregated, so it is unclear how many participants were lost, withdrew because of health status, or were withdrawn for clinical reasons. Such reasons could be related to the subsequent risk of death or heart-failure events.  
   * The article does not provide a sensitivity analysis demonstrating that plausible outcomes among those with incomplete follow-up could not alter the estimated effect. Nor does it provide sufficient detail on the censoring mechanism by randomized group.  
   * Although the registry says that all 1820 randomized participants were analysed on an ITT basis and lists all as “completed,” ITT analysis does not eliminate bias from informative censoring or unavailable outcome data. This statement also does not resolve the publication’s report of differential withdrawal/loss to follow-up.  
   * Given the nontrivial amount of incomplete follow-up, the imbalance between groups, and the plausible relation between withdrawal/loss and a serious clinical composite outcome, missingness is likely to have depended on the true outcome value. This supports a **high-risk** judgement under RoB 2.

4. **measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because the heart-failure component of the composite depended on unblinded clinicians’ diagnostic and treatment/hospital-admission decisions. Blinded adjudication reduces but does not eliminate this concern.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   * All-cause death is objective and unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of assignment. However, the composite result was primarily driven by nonfatal heart-failure events: 151 versus 167 first heart-failure events, whereas only 36 versus 18 first events were deaths. Therefore, the risk of bias in measuring/ascertaining heart-failure events is highly influential for the composite outcome.  
   * Heart-failure events were initially identified by treating physicians who knew treatment assignment. The article states: “The diagnosis of heart failure, which was made by physicians who were aware of study-group assignments,” required signs/symptoms and treatment escalation, including intravenous decongestive therapy or an augmented regimen during hospitalization.  
   * This outcome therefore incorporates clinician judgement and management decisions, including decisions regarding treatment intensification and hospital admission. Such decisions could plausibly differ between an unblinded CRT–ICD group and an ICD-only group.  
   * The investigators acknowledge this limitation: “Members of the heart-failure adjudication committee were unaware of study-group assignments, but the investigators who decided on therapy or hospital admission for patients with heart failure were aware of such assignments. It is possible that the investigators’ knowledge of study-group assignment contributed in some way to the lower frequency of heart failure in the CRT–ICD group.”  
   * Although the independent mortality and heart-failure committees were blinded and used prespecified criteria, blinded adjudication cannot fully correct bias if the initial occurrence, clinical investigation, treatment escalation, or admission decision was influenced by assignment. Accordingly, ascertainment could have differed between groups, which gives **high risk of bias** under RoB 2.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the outcome and broad analysis approach were pre-specified in the registry and correspond to the publication, but there is no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan available to exclude selection among possible analyses.

   b. **Rationale:**  
   * The trial was registered before completion (NCT00180271; first posted September 2005; primary completion June 2009). The registry prospectively specifies the primary outcome as “Mortality From Any Cause or First Heart Failure (HF) Event,” with a detailed heart-failure definition and an average follow-up of 2.4 years.  
   * The publication reports the same primary outcome: “death from any cause or a nonfatal heart-failure event (whichever came first).” This consistency reduces concern that the outcome domain or broad outcome definition was selected after examining results.  
   * The publication also describes a prespecified group-sequential monitoring design, stratified log-rank test, and stratified Cox model, and states that the trial stopped after crossing a “prespecified efficacy boundary.”  
   * Nevertheless, the available registry record does not contain a detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. It therefore cannot fully establish that the reported hazard-ratio analysis, handling of censoring/missing data, final analysis timing, and other analytic choices were selected without knowledge of results.  
   * There is no clear evidence that the reported result was selected from multiple outcome measurements or analyses because it was favourable; therefore, high risk is not warranted. However, the absence of a sufficiently detailed prospective analysis plan leads to **some concerns**.
