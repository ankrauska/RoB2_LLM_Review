1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because the report describes an intended random allocation ratio and stratification, but does not report a genuinely random sequence-generation method or allocation concealment.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states: “*Random assignment … was accomplished by randomly assigning 2 participants to the percentile group, 2 to the fixed criterion group and 1 to the random incentives group from each group of 5 participants*,” and that randomization was stratified by medication intention and order of study entry. This suggests restricted/block allocation, but does not state how the ordering within each set of five was randomized (e.g., computer-generated sequence or random-number table). It also provides no information that allocation was concealed from staff enrolling participants before assignment.  
   
   Baseline characteristics were broadly similar, with the exception of vocational/technical/community-college education, which was more frequent in the percentile than fixed-criterion group (*χ²* = 8.46, *p* < 0.01). This isolated imbalance, among numerous baseline comparisons and in a modest-sized study, does not by itself demonstrate a failure of randomization. However, absent information on allocation concealment leads to some concerns under RoB 2.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to the incentive schedule at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and intervention personnel were necessarily aware of the assigned incentive contingency: participants were told the nature of their assigned schedule immediately after randomization. For example, fixed-criterion participants “*were told that they could only earn incentives for not smoking*,” while percentile-schedule participants were told that the CO criterion “*changed to help them smoke less*.” Thus, blinding was not present.

   However, the differing incentive contingencies were the intended randomized interventions, rather than non-protocol deviations. The report does not identify additional non-protocol interventions, differential care, or other deviations arising because of the trial context that were likely to affect abstinence. Differences in payments, including payment for low but non-abstinent CO samples in the percentile group, were specified features of that intervention.

   For the outcome of whether participants produced at least one abstinent CO sample during treatment, results were reported for all randomized participants: 23/36 percentile participants and 25/40 fixed-criterion participants produced at least one CO sample <3 ppm. This is consistent with analysing participants according to randomized assignment for this outcome. The conditional analyses among participants who first achieved abstinence should not be interpreted as an intention-to-treat estimate of the effect of assignment, but they do not undermine the reported randomized-group comparison for abstinence initiation.

3. **missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias because outcome data were unavailable for a substantial proportion of scheduled assessments, and missed visits are plausibly related to smoking/abstinence status.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants were expected to attend 60 weekday visits. However, mean numbers of submitted CO samples were only 42/60 in the percentile group and 40/60 in the fixed-criterion group (and 52/60 in the random-incentive group). Therefore, approximately 30%–33% of scheduled CO outcome measurements were missing in the two principal groups.

   The article explicitly depicts missed visits as white cells in the event records, but does not report an analysis that imputes missing CO outcomes, treats missed visits as smoking, or provides sensitivity analyses showing that the result is robust to plausible assumptions about missingness. It also does not provide reasons for each missed visit by group or evidence that missingness was unrelated to smoking status.

   In a smoking-cessation study, failure to attend a scheduled biochemical-verification visit is plausibly associated with continued smoking or relapse. Thus, missingness in CO-confirmed abstinence is likely to depend on the true outcome value. Although attendance did not differ materially between the percentile and fixed-criterion groups (42 versus 40 submitted samples; *z* = −0.10, *p* = 0.92), the large amount of missing outcome data and lack of appropriate handling or sensitivity analysis mean that missing outcomes could materially affect estimates of abstinence and abstinence maintenance.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias because abstinence was measured objectively using the same prespecified biochemical method and threshold in all groups.

   b. **Rationale:** Smoking abstinence was defined using an objective breath CO criterion: “*We used an abstinence breath CO criterion of < 3 ppm*.” CO samples were obtained using Vitalograph CO monitors with a standardized procedure: participants took a deep breath, held it for 20 seconds, and expired into the monitor; “*The peak breath CO reading was taken as the participant’s breath CO level.*”

   The same CO-monitoring method, abstinence threshold (<3 ppm), and scheduled assessment approach were used across intervention groups. Although participants and staff knew assignment, the relevant outcome was generated by an objective instrument and leaves little scope for subjective assessor judgement or differential interpretation. There is no indication that the CO monitor, threshold, or method of ascertainment differed by randomized group.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns because there is no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan available in the provided material to verify that the reported abstinence result and analysis were selected before unblinded outcome data were available.

   b. **Rationale:** The article describes several related abstinence analyses: (i) the proportion producing at least one CO <3 ppm; (ii) time to first abstinent sample among those who achieved abstinence; (iii) total number of abstinent samples among those who achieved abstinence; (iv) duration of consecutive abstinent samples; and (v) whether the first abstinent sample was followed by another abstinent sample. It also reports both omnibus three-group tests and additional percentile-versus-fixed-criterion comparisons.

   The authors state that, based on their hypothesis, “*the most important comparison was between participants in the percentile and fixed criterion incentive groups*,” which provides a rationale for that contrast. Nevertheless, the report does not establish that the exact CO <3 ppm outcome, time point/summary metric, handling of missed visits, and statistical analysis were specified in a protocol or analysis plan finalized before outcome data were unblinded. In the absence of verifiable prospective analysis intentions, and given multiple eligible abstinence measures and analyses, RoB 2 indicates some concerns rather than low risk.
