1. **Bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because random allocation was stated but the method used to generate and conceal the allocation sequence was not reported.

   b. **Rationale:** The article states that participants “were randomly allocated to the treatment or wait-list group in consecutive order” and that allocation occurred “in random order.” However, it provides no information on how the random sequence was generated (e.g., computer-generated sequence) or whether allocation was concealed until enrolment was irreversible. “In consecutive order” does not establish adequate concealment and could be compatible with a predictable allocation procedure.  
   
   Baseline characteristics appear reasonably balanced: the authors state that “Randomization was successful: significant differences emerged neither for any of the background nor psychopathological variables,” and Table 1 shows equal group sizes (105 per arm) and no statistically significant differences in reported baseline characteristics. Thus, there is no indication from baseline data that randomization was subverted. Nevertheless, absent information on allocation concealment results in some concerns under RoB 2.

2. **Deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to Deprexis versus assignment to the wait-list condition.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants necessarily knew whether they had immediate access to the online programme or were assigned to wait-list, and personnel responsible for allocation were likely also aware. However, for the assignment effect, lack of blinding is problematic only if deviations from the intended intervention arose because of the trial context, were likely to affect BDI scores, and were unbalanced between groups.

   The study protocol was implemented as intended: the Deprexis group received programme vouchers within 24 hours, while the wait-list group was to receive access after the eight-week assessment. Both groups received the same mid-study email asking about problems or concerns, which “did not contain any therapeutic information.” There is no reported evidence that trial-context effects led to differential non-protocol treatment, contamination, or differential management that would plausibly affect the BDI outcome. The authors speculate that wait-list participants might have obtained other treatments, but this is not evidence that this arose because of trial participation rather than usual care outside the trial context.

   The selected primary result used an intention-to-treat analysis: “ITT analyses considered data from all subjects with available baseline data,” with missing post-intervention scores imputed, and analyses retained participants in their randomized groups. This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment; concerns arising from missing post-intervention data are considered separately.

3. **Missing outcome data: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because outcome data were missing for a substantial proportion of randomized participants, missingness differed between groups, and missingness in self-reported depression is plausibly related to the unobserved BDI outcome.

   b. **Rationale:** Of 210 randomized participants, the per-protocol BDI analysis included 90/105 (85.7%) wait-list participants and 80/105 (76.2%) Deprexis participants—thus, approximately 19% of participants had no observed post-intervention BDI score. The article reports an overall “completion rate” of 81.9%, with completion of 85.7% in the wait-list group and 78.1% in the immediate-treatment group (Table 1). Therefore, missingness was appreciably greater in the Deprexis group.

   No reasons for non-completion are reported. In an internet-based self-help intervention for depression, failure to complete an eight-week self-report assessment could plausibly be associated with participants’ depression severity or treatment response—for example, persistent symptoms, deterioration, disengagement, or improvement reducing motivation to complete the follow-up assessment. The difference in missing-data proportions between groups further increases concern.

   The authors used multiple imputation: “Multiple imputation was adopted to estimate post-scores for non-completers.” However, the report does not provide sufficient information to establish that the imputation model appropriately addressed a plausible relationship between missingness and the unobserved BDI score, nor does it report sensitivity analyses under plausible missing-not-at-random assumptions. The reported comparison that completers and non-completers did not differ on measured baseline psychopathology does not establish that their unobserved post-intervention BDI scores were similar. Consequently, the result may be materially biased by missing outcome data.

4. **Measurement of the outcome: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias, because the BDI is a participant-reported outcome and participants were aware of whether they received immediate treatment or were on a wait-list.

   b. **Rationale:** The BDI was the primary outcome and is explicitly described as “a self-report questionnaire containing 21 items.” Thus, the participant was the outcome assessor. Participants could not realistically be blinded: those assigned to Deprexis received instructions and a voucher for immediate access, whereas wait-list participants were informed that access would be provided after the eight-week reassessment.

   The method itself—the validated BDI—was appropriate, and the same online questionnaire was administered at baseline and follow-up in both groups. Nevertheless, knowledge of assignment was likely to influence participants’ reporting of depressive symptoms. The comparison of an active, novel self-help programme with wait-list is particularly susceptible to expectancy, disappointment, attention, and perceived-treatment effects. The study’s own retrospective assessment indicates substantial positive expectations and perceived benefit among programme users—for example, 64.8% reported that expectations were met or exceeded, and 47.9% reported that “Depressive symptoms have decreased due to Deprexis.” These circumstances make it likely that knowledge of intervention assignment influenced the participant-reported BDI outcome.

5. **Selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the trial was registered before completion and identified BDI-assessed depressive symptoms as the primary outcome, but no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized statistical analysis plan was available to verify the specific reported analysis.

   b. **Rationale:** The registry record was first posted on 25 July 2011, before the reported primary completion date of September 2012. It identified “Severity of depressive symptoms as assessed with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)” as the primary outcome and specified the expectation that depressive symptoms would improve more in Deprexis than in the wait-list group over eight weeks. The publication is consistent with this broad intention: it identifies the BDI as the “primary outcome” and reports the eight-week BDI total-score comparison.

   However, the registry does not specify the precise analysis plan for the BDI total score—such as the intended estimand, ANCOVA model, covariates, handling of missing data, use of multiple imputation, or whether final values versus change scores would be the primary analysis. The article reports both per-protocol and imputed ITT analyses, but the registry does not establish that either analytic approach was specified before unblinded outcome data were available. It also does not provide a prespecified hierarchy among analyses.

   There is no clear evidence that the reported BDI total-score result was selected from multiple outcome measures or analyses because of its results: the BDI total score was consistently identified as primary, and both per-protocol and ITT results were reported. Nonetheless, absence of a detailed prospective analysis plan leaves some concerns about selection among potentially eligible analyses.
