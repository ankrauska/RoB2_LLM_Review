1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation method included a random element but allocation concealment was not adequately described.

   b. **Rationale:** The report states that subjects “were randomly allocated” and specifies that “*the randomization procedure was to pick the name of the orthosis from a bag including the names of both orthoses*.” Drawing allocation labels from a bag can constitute a random sequence-generation method. However, there is no information confirming that allocation was concealed until a participant had been irreversibly enrolled (e.g., who drew the label, whether labels were opaque and indistinguishable, and whether draws were performed only after recruitment).

   Baseline characteristics did not indicate an obvious failure of randomization: there were no statistically significant between-group differences in age, weight, height, kyphosis angle, or baseline balance score (“*balance score without orthosis (p = 0.443)*”). Nevertheless, the unequal group sizes (Spinomed, n = 10; PTS, n = 8) and the absence of information on allocation concealment support a judgement of some concerns rather than low risk.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias for the effect of assignment to Spinomed versus posture-training support at baseline.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants and personnel were necessarily likely to be aware of the assigned orthosis, because the two devices are visibly and physically distinct. However, for the RoB 2 effect-of-assignment assessment, lack of blinding causes bias only if it leads to deviations from intended intervention arising from the trial context that are likely to affect the outcome and are unbalanced between groups.

   Both orthoses were fitted by “*a certified orthotist who adjusted the straps with the same tension in all participants*,” and all participants were asked to wear their assigned orthosis for 1.5 hours before testing. The report does not describe protocol deviations, differential co-interventions, crossovers between orthoses, or differential implementation attributable to awareness of assignment.

   The between-group comparison used the change in balance score with versus without the allocated orthosis, analysed using an independent-samples t-test. The report states: “*To compare the changes in balance parameters between the two groups, an independent sample t-test was used.*” Thus, participants appear to have been analysed according to their randomized orthosis group, with no reported post-randomization exclusions or as-treated analysis. This is appropriate for estimating the effect of assignment.

3. **missing outcome data: low risk of bias**

   e. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   f. **Rationale:** Outcome data appear available for all randomized participants. The article states that “*Eighteen participants … completed the study procedure*,” with eight allocated to PTS and 10 to Spinomed. Tables report balance-score data for all 10 Spinomed participants and all eight PTS participants, both without and with orthosis.

   There is no indication of dropout, missing balance measurements, exclusions from the balance-score analysis, or imputation of missing data. Therefore, missing data are unlikely to have affected the estimated comparison of changes in balance score.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias.

   b. **Rationale:** Balance score was assessed using the computerized Sensory Organization Test (SOT) on the NeuroCom EquiTest system, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The article describes the outcome as the “*total score, which ranged between 0 and 100*,” calculated from repeated tests across six sensory configurations. This is an appropriate, standardized instrument-based method for measuring balance.

   Although participants could not realistically be blinded to whether they were wearing an orthosis, the balance score was derived from force-plate measurements and automated system calculation, rather than from a subjective rating by participants or treating clinicians. The same testing system and procedures were used in both groups, and testing with and without orthosis was performed in random order, with a 1-hour interval between conditions. There is no indication that outcome ascertainment differed systematically between randomized orthosis groups. Knowledge of the allocated orthosis is unlikely to have materially influenced the computerized balance-score measurement.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns.

   b. **Rationale:** The article identifies balance score as the main outcome measure: “*The balance score was considered in the role of the main outcome measure*.” It reports the balance-score means, standard deviations, within-group comparisons, and the between-group comparison of change scores. There is no direct evidence that the reported balance-score result was selected from multiple balance-score instruments or multiple reported analyses because of its statistical significance.

   However, no dated protocol or statistical analysis plan is available in the provided materials to establish that the balance-score analysis, measurement time point, and analytic approach were finalized before unblinded outcome data were available. The referenced trial-registration document was not available in the material provided here for comparison with the published report. In addition, several potentially eligible analytic choices existed, including analyses of scores with orthosis, without orthosis, and change scores. The publication reports more than one relevant analysis but does not document a pre-specified analysis plan selecting the between-group change-score comparison as the primary analysis. Therefore, selective reporting cannot be excluded, resulting in some concerns.
