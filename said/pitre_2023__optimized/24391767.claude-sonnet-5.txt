# Risk of Bias Assessment: RoB 2.0
## High-Field Open versus Short-Bore MRI of the Spine (Image Quality Parameters Outcome)

---

## 1. Bias due to randomization: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns arise primarily due to a lack of detailed information about the concealment implementation, combined with a substantial post-randomization process (cross-referral) that could plausibly relate to baseline characteristics, even though the initial randomization itself appears sound.

**b. Rationale:**
- Sequence generation: The article states, "Randomization was not stratified and allocation was concealed (using sealed envelopes)" and patients were "randomly assigned (computer-generated sequence)." This supports a "Yes" to signalling question 1.1 (random sequence).
- Allocation concealment: Sealed envelopes were used, but the report does not specify whether envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, and irreversibly assigned (per Table 2 minimal/extended criteria). This lack of detail leads to a "Probably yes" response at best, introducing some uncertainty.
- Baseline balance: Table 1 shows patients were well-matched with no significant differences in age, sex, BMI, or region of imaging (all P>0.05), supporting no evidence of randomization failure via baseline imbalance.
- However, 25 of 93 patients (27%) were cross-referred to the other scanner due to claustrophobic events before completing their originally assigned imaging. While this is addressed under intervention deviations rather than randomization per se, the *initial* allocation concealment quality is not fully verifiable from the report, and combined with no explicit statement on envelope security features, this supports "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk."

**Overall for this domain:** Low risk of bias for sequence generation and baseline balance, but incomplete reporting on strict allocation concealment procedures (sealed but not clearly described as opaque/sequentially numbered) leads to a domain-level judgement of **Some concerns**.

---

## 2. Deviations from intended intervention (effect of assignment to intervention): **High risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** High risk of bias because participants and personnel could not be blinded (as expected given the nature of the intervention—the two different scanner types), and substantial deviations from assigned intervention (cross-referral to the other scanner) occurred due to the trial context (claustrophobia), were unbalanced between groups, and likely affected the outcome (image quality). Additionally, the analysis excluded the switched participants from their originally assigned group rather than analyzing by original allocation, at risk of violating ITT principles.

**b. Rationale:**
- **2.1/2.2 (Awareness):** "Patients could not be blinded to the assigned study group due to the MR imaging setting." Personnel (radiographers) were necessarily aware given the different physical machines. Answer: Yes.
- **2.3 (Deviations arising from trial context):** The trial explicitly built in a cross-referral protocol: "If patients did not complete imaging in their assigned MR scanner due to claustrophobia, they were cross-referred to imaging in the other scanner in order to avoid the risks of conscious sedation." This is a deviation from the intended (assigned) intervention that is a direct consequence of the trial context (i.e., awareness of assignment and claustrophobic reaction to the specific scanner). Answer: Yes.
- **2.4 (Affecting outcome):** Since the outcome under evaluation is image quality, and the very reason for switching (claustrophobia, motion, discomfort) plausibly relates to image quality via motion artifacts, and because switching by definition means the patient is imaged on the non-randomized scanner (directly determining the image-quality result attributed to a given "arm"), this deviation critically affects the outcome. Answer: Yes.
- **2.5 (Balanced between groups):** Per Figure 1, the cross-referral was likely asymmetric (rates of claustrophobic events differed by scanner type per the companion primary trial publication, which found "a positive trend for open MR imaging" for claustrophobia reduction, implying more patients failed on the open scanner and crossed over to short-bore, or vice versa in various proportions). The article does not report symmetric numbers between groups, and given claustrophobia is scanner-specific, deviations are very likely unbalanced. Answer: No/Probably no.
- **2.6/2.7 (Analysis approach):** Critically, the authors analyzed patients according to the scanner they were actually examined on ("Examinations performed after cross-referral, with or without sedation, were included in the primary analysis" as their allocated group at the time of scan, effectively an as-treated analysis for those who switched) rather than by original randomized assignment. This is explicitly a deviation from ITT principles for the "effect of assignment" analysis. The text states plainly that the primary analysis included examinations "performed after cross-referral" — meaning the analysis groups (Short-Bore MR n=48, Open MR n=55) reflect the scanner actually used, not the original randomized allocation. This is an inappropriate analysis for the effect of assignment to intervention, and the impact was likely substantial (27% of patients affected).

Given deviations arose from trial context, affected the outcome, were plausibly unbalanced, AND an inappropriate (as-treated rather than ITT) analysis was used affecting >25% of the sample — this meets criteria for **High risk of bias** in this domain.

## 3. Missing outcome data: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias, as outcome (image quality) data were available for essentially all patients who underwent scanning, with only a single sequence from one patient excluded for a well-documented, plausible non-outcome-related reason.

**b. Rationale:**
- "Only one patient aborted MR imaging on the first scanner due to claustrophobia after one sequence, which could thus not be included in the analysis." This represents an extremely small amount of missing data (1 sequence out of many hundreds across 93 patients).
- Of 106 patients with indication for spinal imaging, 93 underwent MR imaging and "all were included in this analysis" (Figure 1, Results section: "Of these 106 patients, 93 underwent MR imaging, and all were included in this analysis").
- The reason for the single exclusion (inability to complete due to claustrophobia) is documented and unrelated to the true value of image quality (it's a data collection failure, not related to what the image quality would have been).
- No systematic differential missingness pattern related to outcome value is apparent.

This supports signalling question 3.1 ("Yes" — data available for nearly all participants), leading to a default **Low risk of bias** judgement.

## 4. Measurement of the outcome: **Some concerns**

**a. Judgement:** Some concerns, because although outcome assessors were blinded to allocation for the qualitative analysis, blinding to the *actual intervention received* (i.e., which scanner produced the image) was likely compromised by the visually distinguishable characteristics of images from open vs. short-bore scanners (e.g., different fields of view, different noise patterns, different coil-related appearance), and this is a subjective, judgment-based outcome susceptible to influence by this unblinding.

**b. Rationale:**
- Method of measurement was appropriate: standardized qualitative scoring (1–5 scales for contrast, sharpness, artifacts, noise, overall quality) and quantitative measures (SNR, CNR, contour sharpness) using ImageJ — validated approaches. Signalling Q4.1: No.
- Measurement/ascertainment method was identical/comparable across groups: same ROI placement approach, same scales applied to both scanner types. Signalling Q4.2: No.
- Blinding of assessors: "At the time of the analysis the examiners were blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity." This is a "Yes" (blinded) — however, this is an outcome that involves judgement (qualitative overall image quality, contrast, contour sharpness are all Type 3 outcomes — observer-reported involving judgement).
- Critically, true blinding to scanner type in image quality assessment is difficult to achieve in practice: images from a 1.5T short-bore scanner versus a 1T open scanner with a different coil setup often have recognizable differences in appearance (e.g., FOV shape, background noise patterns, characteristic vertical vs. horizontal field artifacts) that could allow assessors to infer which scanner produced the image, despite intended blinding. The paper does not describe any specific technique to mask scanner-identifying features (e.g., cropping, standardizing display) beyond "blinded to imaging technique."
- Given assessors could very plausibly deduce scanner type from image characteristics, and this is a judgement-based outcome (overall quality, contrast, sharpness ratings) that could be influenced by such awareness or expectation bias, there is a genuine possibility (though not certainty) that assessment was influenced by (inferred) knowledge of intervention received.
- Because the quantitative measures (SNR, CNR, contour sharpness) are objective and computed via defined ROI/pixel value formulas from ImageJ, these particular outcomes are at lower risk; however, since this domain assessment pertains to "image quality parameters" overall — including the qualitative scores which are more subjective — the mixed nature justifies a "Some concerns" rather than "Low risk" judgement, as there's no confirmation that the likely inferability of scanner type did NOT influence the judgement-based scores.

This corresponds to Signalling Q4.3 = Yes/Probably yes (blinding stated, but Q4.4 = Yes, judgement-influenceable outcome) and Q4.5 = uncertain (No clear evidence that assessment was actually influenced, but plausible) → **Some concerns**.

## 5. Selective reporting: **Low risk of bias**

**a. Judgement:** Low risk of bias — the reported image quality parameters and analyses correspond closely to those pre-specified in the trial protocol and registration, with no evidence of selective outcome or analysis reporting based on results.

**b. Rationale:**
- The trial registration (NCT00715806) is for the parent CLAUSTRO trial, whose primary outcome was claustrophobia reduction; however, the published protocol paper (Enders et al., BMC Med Imaging 2011) explicitly detailed the pre-specified image quality analysis plan (qualitative scoring 1–5, SNR, CNR, contour sharpness), as referenced: "The detailed trial protocol has been published elsewhere [15]."
- The article states: "All sequences were confirmed by local application specialists" and image analysis methodology (qualitative grading, SNR/CNR formulas, contour sharpness via ImageJ) is described in detail matching what appears to be the pre-specified protocol methodology.
- All pre-specified qualitative parameters (overall quality, contrast, contour sharpness, noise, artifacts) are reported for all three sequence types (T2 sagittal, T1 sagittas, T2 axial) and both anatomic regions (cervicothoracic, thoracolumbar) in Table 3 — no evidence of selective omission.
- All pre-specified quantitative parameters (SNR, CNR for multiple tissue interfaces, contour sharpness for multiple interfaces) are reported comprehensively in Table 4, again for all sequence types and regions.
- The authors transparently note limitations and exclusions with justification, e.g., "In 15 examinations optional sequences were acquired... which were not included in the analysis due to the small number" — a pre-specified, results-independent rationale (small n) rather than a result-dependent exclusion.
- No indication of switching primary/secondary outcomes, undisclosed subgroup-driven selective analyses, or omitted pre-specified comparisons.

Given the consistency between the detailed pre-specified protocol and the comprehensively reported results across all parameters and subgroups, this supports a judgement of **Low risk of bias**.
