1. **bias due to randomization: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because the allocation sequence was computer-generated and baseline characteristics were balanced, but the description of allocation concealment is insufficient to establish that sealed envelopes were adequately protected from prediction or manipulation.

   b. **Rationale:** The investigators report that patients were “randomly assigned (computer-generated sequence) in a 1:1 ratio,” which supports a random allocation sequence. Baseline characteristics of the 93 analysed spinal-imaging participants were similar between groups (e.g., age, sex, BMI, spinal region, claustrophobia score, and pre-scan anxiety; all reported P values ≥0.1), with no indication of an important baseline imbalance.

   However, the only concealment information is: “allocation was concealed (using sealed envelopes).” The report does not state that envelopes were sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof, or opened only after irrevocable enrolment. Under RoB 2, “sealed envelopes” alone do not provide enough detail to confirm adequate concealment. Thus, the randomization process raises some concerns rather than being clearly at low risk.

2. **deviations from intended intervention: high risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** High risk of bias for the effect of assignment to intervention at baseline, because the reported image-quality comparison was based on the scanner on which imaging was actually performed, including cross-referred participants, rather than an analysis according to randomized assignment.

   b. **Rationale:** Participants could not be blinded to their assigned scanner: “Patients could not be blinded to the assigned study group due to the MR imaging setting.” In addition, a substantial number of examinations involved departure from the originally allocated scanner. The protocol allowed patients unable to complete their assigned examination because of claustrophobia to be “cross-referred to imaging in the other scanner”; 25 examinations were performed after cross-referral, including 16 with sedation.

   Most importantly, the article states: “Examinations performed after cross-referral, with or without sedation, were included in the primary analysis.” The reported image-quality analysis compares images obtained on the short-bore scanner with those obtained on the open scanner—for example, 48 short-bore versus 55 open-scanner examinations—not participants according to their randomized group. This is effectively an as-treated comparison for the image-quality outcome.

   For the specified estimand—the effect of *assignment* to an intervention at baseline—an appropriate analysis would preserve randomized allocation (with missing outcome data considered separately). The cross-referrals were sufficiently frequent to have a substantial potential impact on results, particularly because inability to tolerate a scanner, use of sedation, and related patient characteristics may also be associated with motion and image quality. Therefore, this domain is at high risk of bias.

3. **missing outcome data: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because spinal image-quality data were unavailable for a meaningful proportion of patients with a spinal-MRI indication, and no analysis demonstrates robustness to the missing data.

   b. **Rationale:** Of 106 randomized patients with an indication for spinal MRI, only 93 underwent spinal imaging and were included in the image-quality analysis. Thus, data were unavailable for 13/106 participants (approximately 12%). This is not sufficiently close to complete outcome data to conclude that the missing observations could not affect the result.

   The report explains that non-completion was related to claustrophobia: “A claustrophobic event was defined as the inability of a patient to undergo MR imaging due to claustrophobia.” It also reports that one participant “aborted MR imaging on the short-bore MR scanner after acquisition of one sequence, which could thus not be included in the analysis.” Claustrophobia and scan tolerance could plausibly be associated with movement during scanning and therefore with some image-quality measures, particularly artefacts and qualitative ratings. The mechanism for missingness may also differ by scanner type.

   No imputation, inverse-probability method, or sensitivity analysis was reported to show that plausible values for unavailable image-quality outcomes would not materially alter the comparison. Nevertheless, the publication does not establish that missingness was *likely* related to the true image-quality values; therefore, “some concerns,” rather than high risk, is appropriate.

4. **measurement of the outcome: low risk of bias**

   a. **Judgement:** Low risk of bias, because the image-quality parameters were assessed using defined qualitative and quantitative methods, and the image assessors were reported to be blinded to scanner type and patient identity.

   b. **Rationale:** The study used prespecified image-quality measurements, including qualitative ratings of “contrast, contour sharpness, and overall image quality,” quantitative signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), and contour sharpness. The authors describe standardized procedures for regions of interest and contour-sharpness measurement.

   Crucially, they report: “At the time of the analysis the examiners were blinded to the imaging technique and patient identity.” Qualitative image analysis was conducted “by two examiners in consensus,” and quantitative analysis by one examiner using stated methods. The same outcome constructs and measurement approach were applied to images from both scanner groups.

   Although scanner characteristics and acquisition parameters necessarily differed to some extent, this reflects the interventions under comparison rather than differential outcome ascertainment. There is no indication that image assessors knowingly evaluated one group differently from the other. Therefore, measurement bias is judged low.

5. **selective reporting: some concerns**

   a. **Judgement:** Some concerns, because no sufficiently detailed, prospectively finalized analysis plan for the image-quality outcomes was available in the registration record, and there were multiple image-quality measurements, sequences, anatomical regions, and analytical choices.

   b. **Rationale:** The trial was registered prospectively (NCT00715806; first posted July 15, 2008, before recruitment began in June 2008). However, the registry identifies the primary outcome as the ability of open MRI to reduce claustrophobic reactions and the secondary outcome as the “impact of MR imaging results on subsequent measurement.” It does not specify image-quality parameters, the individual qualitative scales, SNR/CNR definitions, contour-sharpness measures, target sequences, anatomical subgroups, or the intended statistical analysis for the present image-quality comparison.

   The publication reports numerous potentially eligible image-quality results across sagittal T1-weighted, sagittal T2-weighted, and axial T2-weighted sequences; cervicothoracic and thoracolumbar regions; qualitative measures; SNR; several CNR contrasts; and contour-sharpness interfaces. Although the publication presents extensive results and there is no direct evidence that a particular favourable result was selected based on statistical significance, the absence of a detailed pre-specified analysis plan means selective selection among outcome measurements or analyses cannot be excluded.

   Accordingly, the available information supports some concerns, but not high risk, because there is no clear evidence that the reported image-quality result was selected on the basis of its findings.
